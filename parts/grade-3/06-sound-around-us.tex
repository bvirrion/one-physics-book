\chapter{Sound Around Us}\label{ch:g3:sound-around-us}

Hum your favorite song --- and while you hum, lay two fingers gently
on the front of your throat. Feel that trembling? You have just
touched a \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} at its birthplace. Every voice, every drum, every
squeaky door makes its \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} the very same way, and this chapter
goes hunting for the trick.

\section{Where sounds are born}

\begin{definition}[Vibration]\label{def:g3:sound-around-us:vibration}
A \emph{vibration}\index{vibration} is a very fast trembling --- a
back-and-forth movement repeated many times each second, often too
fast for the eye to follow. A plucked guitar string vibrates: you
see only a blur, but your fingertip feels the buzzing.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Sound]\label{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}
A \emph{sound}\index{sound} is what we hear when something
vibrates and its trembling travels through the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} to our ears. The
vibrating thing is the sound's \emph{source} --- a string, a drum
skin, a bell, your own throat.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Making rice dance]\label{met:g3:sound-around-us:rice}
To \emph{see} a \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} being born:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item scatter a few grains of rice on the skin of a drum (or on a
        tin's plastic lid, or a stretched balloon skin);
  \item tap the skin near the grains --- boom: the grains jump!
  \item now only \emph{shout} at the skin from very close: the
        grains tremble again, without being touched.
\end{enumerate}
The skin vibrates whenever it \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sounds} --- and even a \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} arriving
from your mouth sets it trembling.
\end{method}

\begin{proposition}[No vibration, no sound]\label{prop:g3:sound-around-us:novib}
Every \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} comes from a \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:vibration}{vibration} --- and when the \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:vibration}{vibration} stops,
the \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} stops with it, instantly. Strike a cymbal and let it ring;
then grab it firmly with both hands: silence, the moment your grip
stops the trembling.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Sound sources everywhere]\label{ex:g3:sound-around-us:sources}
Hunt the \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:vibration}{vibration} behind each \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound}: the guitar --- plucked strings
tremble; the drum --- the struck skin trembles; your voice --- two
little folds in your throat tremble as \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} from your lungs rushes
past (that is the buzz your fingers felt); the buzzing fly --- its
wings tremble hundreds of times a second; the slammed door --- the
whole door and its frame shudder for a moment.
\end{example}

\section{Loud and quiet, high and low}

\begin{example}[Loud is a big trembling]\label{ex:g3:sound-around-us:loud}
Pluck a stretched rubber band gently: a small blur, a quiet twang.
Pluck it hard: a wide blur, a loud twang. The harder the source
trembles --- the wider its back-and-forth --- the \emph{louder} the
\omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound}. Watch the rice grains too: a gentle tap makes them shiver, a
mighty boom throws them in the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[High and low]\label{ex:g3:sound-around-us:pitch}
Stretch a rubber band over an open box and pluck; then pinch it in
the middle and pluck the short half: the twang turns \emph{higher},
like a bird's chirp. Short, tight, thin things tremble faster and
sing high; long, loose, thick things tremble slower and sing low.
That is why the little flute chirps and the big tuba growls --- and
why the guitar's thinnest string sings highest.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Protect the little drum in your ear]\label{rem:g3:sound-around-us:ears}
Deep in your ear, a tiny skin --- thinner than paper --- trembles
with every arriving \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound}, just like the rice-grain drum. \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{Sounds}
that are too loud shake it too hard: very loud music, machines and
fireworks can damage it for good, and it does not grow back. Cover
your ears near loud noise, and never shout into anyone's ear ---
ears have no eyelids.
\end{remark}

\section{From the source to the ear}

\begin{example}[The journey of a clap]\label{ex:g3:sound-around-us:journey}
A friend claps across the playground. The clap sets the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}
trembling; the trembling spreads out through the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} in every
direction, like the rings around a pebble dropped in a pond; some of
it reaches your ear and sets your ear's little skin trembling ---
and you hear the clap. Source, journey, ear: every \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} you have
ever heard made this trip. How fast is the journey, and what can it
travel through? Next year's \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} chapter takes exactly those
questions.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:sound-around-us:1}
What is a \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:vibration}{vibration}? Name the vibrating part in: a guitar; \ a drum;
\ your voice; \ a buzzing fly.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:sound-around-us:2}
In the rice experiment, what do the jumping grains prove about a
sounding drum skin?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:sound-around-us:3}
Why does grabbing a ringing cymbal silence it instantly? Which law
of the chapter is at work?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:sound-around-us:4}
To make the rubber band twang \emph{louder}, what do you change
about your pluck? What does the blur of the band look like then?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:sound-around-us:5}
Which sings higher: the short pinched band or the whole band? \ The
little flute or the big tuba? \ The guitar's thinnest or thickest
string?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:sound-around-us:6}
Hum with your fingers on your throat, then stop humming. What do
your fingers feel, and exactly when does the feeling stop?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:sound-around-us:7}
Tell the journey of a clap from your friend's hands to your ear, in
three steps.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:sound-around-us:8}
Why must very loud \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sounds} be kept away from ears? What tiny thing
inside the ear is in danger, and what does it resemble from this
chapter?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:sound-around-us:9}
A music box's comb has teeth of different lengths. Which teeth play
the high notes and which the low ones? Answer with the rule of
\cref{ex:g3:sound-around-us:pitch}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g3:sound-around-us:10}
Pour water into a glass bottle and blow across its mouth: a hoot!
Add more water and blow again: the hoot turns higher. What is
trembling to make the hoot --- the glass, the water, or the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}
inside? Use the short-sings-high rule to defend your answer.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g3:sound-around-us:11}
Maya says: ``When the television is on mute, the screen still moves
--- so movement alone must make \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound}.'' Use
\cref{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound} and the rice experiment to
explain what kind of movement makes \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound}, and why the pictures on
a screen make none.
\end{exercise}
