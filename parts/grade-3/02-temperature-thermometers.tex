\chapter{Temperature and Thermometers}\label{ch:g3:temperature-thermometers}

Two winters ago, three bowls of water fooled your two hands: the
same water felt warm to one and cool to the other. We promised then
that an instrument would one day settle such arguments. Here it is
--- the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer}, the little glass judge that turns hot and cold
into a number.

\section{Why touch is not enough}

\begin{example}[The unfair judges]\label{ex:g3:temperature-thermometers:unfair}
Touch judges unfairly in every season: the metal slide feels colder
than the wooden bench standing in the same shade; lukewarm water
feels warm to a cold hand and cool to a warm one; and after sledding,
the chilly hallway feels toasty. To compare hot and cold honestly ---
across rooms, across days, across people --- we need a judge that
never changes its mind: an instrument.
\end{example}

\section{The thermometer and its degrees}

\begin{definition}[Temperature]\label{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}
The \emph{temperature}\index{temperature} of a thing is the number
that says exactly how hot or cold it is. Higher number, hotter
thing; lower number, colder thing. Temperature is measured in
\emph{degrees Celsius}\index{degree Celsius}, written \unit{\celsius},
with a \emph{thermometer}\index{thermometer}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Water's two milestones]\label{prop:g3:temperature-thermometers:milestones}
The Celsius scale is pegged to water's two great changes of state:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item at \qty{0}{\celsius}, water \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{freezes} into ice (and ice \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts});
  \item at \qty{100}{\celsius}, water boils into steam.
\end{enumerate}
Between the two milestones, the scale is cut into $100$ equal
degrees --- a staircase of littler steps from ice to steam.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[How the glass judge works]\label{ex:g3:temperature-thermometers:works}
Inside the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer}'s thin tube sits a trapped colored \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}.
Warmth makes that \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} swell just a little --- and in a tube so
thin, a little swelling pushes the column visibly up. Cold shrinks
it back down. The \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} cannot be fooled like a hand: it swells the
same for the same warmth, today, tomorrow, for everyone.
\end{example}

\section{Reading a thermometer}

\begin{method}[Reading a thermometer]\label{met:g3:temperature-thermometers:read}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Put the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} where you want the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} --- in
        the water, in the shade outdoors, under the tongue;
  \item \emph{wait} --- the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} needs time to finish climbing or
        sinking; watch until it stops moving;
  \item bring your eye level with the top of the column;
  \item read the number at the mark the column reaches, and write it
        with its \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:measure}{unit}: \qty{18}{\celsius}.
\end{enumerate}
Reading too soon, or from an angle, gives a wrong number --- the
judge is honest, but it must be questioned properly.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Temperatures to live by]\label{ex:g3:temperature-thermometers:livedby}
Some numbers worth knowing by heart: a pleasant room is about
\qty{20}{\celsius}; your body keeps itself at about
\qty{37}{\celsius}, in summer and winter alike (much above that is a
fever --- the doctor's \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} knows); the refrigerator holds
about \qty{4}{\celsius}; a hot summer day reaches \qty{30}{\celsius};
bath water is pleasant near \qty{35}{\celsius}.
\end{example}

\section{Below zero}

\begin{example}[Winter numbers]\label{ex:g3:temperature-thermometers:belowzero}
On a freezing morning the column \omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{sinks} \emph{below} the $0$ mark.
The marks keep going down there, and we read them with the word
``minus'': one mark below zero is \emph{minus one} degree, five
marks below is \emph{minus five}, written \qty{-5}{\celsius}.
Minus numbers are colder than zero, and the lower they go, the
harder the \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{freeze}: a $-10$ morning bites colder than a $-2$ one.
The weather report says them every winter evening.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Zero is not ``nothing'']\label{rem:g3:temperature-thermometers:zero}
On this scale, $0$ does not mean ``no \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature}'' --- it is simply
the mark where water \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{freezes}, a milestone like a road sign. Weather
can be warmer than the milestone or colder than it; the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer}
just tells you on which side, and by how many marks. (Numbers below
zero have a whole arithmetic of their own --- your mathematics
course will take them up properly in a later year.)
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:temperature-thermometers:1}
Why is a \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} a fairer judge of hot and cold than your hand?
Name one way hands were fooled in earlier chapters.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:temperature-thermometers:2}
At what \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} does water \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{freeze}? Boil? Which \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:measure}{unit} are both
numbers in?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:temperature-thermometers:3}
What happens to the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} in the thin tube when the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} is
warmed? When it is cooled? Why does such a small change show so
clearly?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:temperature-thermometers:4}
List the two mistakes \cref{met:g3:temperature-thermometers:read}
warns about when reading a \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:temperature-thermometers:5}
Match each thing with its likely \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} ---
\qty{4}{\celsius}, \qty{20}{\celsius}, \qty{37}{\celsius},
\qty{100}{\celsius}: boiling water; \ a cozy living room; \ the
inside of the refrigerator; \ your own body.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:temperature-thermometers:6}
The bath \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} reads \qty{35}{\celsius}; the pot on the stove
reads \qty{100}{\celsius}. Which is hotter, and is the pot safe to
touch? By how many degrees is the pot hotter than the bath?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:temperature-thermometers:7}
A winter \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} shows the column $3$ marks below zero. How do
we say and write that \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature}? Is it colder or warmer than
\qty{-8}{\celsius}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:temperature-thermometers:8}
Yesterday the garden \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} read \qty{6}{\celsius} at breakfast
and \qty{14}{\celsius} at lunch. By how many degrees did the morning
warm up?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g3:temperature-thermometers:9}
Puddles on the pavement have turned to ice overnight. What can you
say about the night's \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature}, without any \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer}? Which
milestone are you using?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g3:temperature-thermometers:10}
Straight after sledding, Emma runs into the hallway and cries ``It
is boiling in here!'' The hallway \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} reads
\qty{16}{\celsius}. Is the hallway hot? Why does Emma feel it so ---
and which judge should the family trust?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g3:temperature-thermometers:11}
A cook wants to know whether soup at a rolling boil gets hotter the
longer it boils. She \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:measure}{measures}: after one minute of boiling,
\qty{100}{\celsius}; after ten minutes, still \qty{100}{\celsius}.
What does the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} teach her about boiling water --- and
about water's milestones?
\end{exercise}
