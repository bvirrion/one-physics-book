\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g3:levers-and-scales} --- Levers and Balance Scales}

\begin{solution}{exo:g3:levers-and-scales:1}
Seesaw: the plank is the \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{lever}, the middle support the \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{pivot}.
Crowbar: the bar is the \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{lever}, the log the \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{pivot}. Scissors: each
blade-with-handle is a \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{lever}, and both \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{pivot} at the central screw.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g3:levers-and-scales:2}
Far from the \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{pivot}: the farther out the push, the bigger its product
--- a small push far away beats a heavy rock close in.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g3:levers-and-scales:3}
$3 \times 4 = 12$; \ $2 \times 5 = 10$; \ $1 \times 6 = 6$. None of
these products are equal, so no two of them balance each other.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g3:levers-and-scales:4}
The left product is $1 \times 8 = 8$. Two coins need $8 = 2 \times
4$: mark $4$. Four coins need $8 = 4 \times 2$: mark $2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g3:levers-and-scales:5}
Left: $3 \times 3 = 9$. Right: $2 \times 4 = 8$. The left side's
product is bigger, so the left side goes down.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g3:levers-and-scales:6}
With equal arms, level pans mean equal weights --- the comparison is
fair. A longer left arm would multiply the left load by a bigger
distance, so the scale would lie in favor of that side at every
weighing.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g3:levers-and-scales:7}
The bag weighs exactly as much as the $6$ blocks. With one block
removed, the bag's pan is heavier, and the scale tips toward the
bag.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g3:levers-and-scales:8}
Tom's product: $2 \times 3 = 6$. His sister needs $1 \times 6 = 6$:
she must sit at mark $6$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g3:levers-and-scales:9}
The \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{pivot} is where the hammer's head rests on the wood. Your hand
pushes at the far end of the handle --- a long distance, so a big
product --- while the nail resists very close to the \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{pivot}: the
\omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{lever} turns your comfortable push into a giant's pull.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g3:levers-and-scales:10}
Balance needs $4 \times (\text{Grandpa's mark}) = 1 \times
(\text{Zoe's mark})$. Two ways: Grandpa at $1$ with Zoe at $4$
($4 = 4$), or Grandpa at $2$ with Zoe at $8$ ($8 = 8$).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g3:levers-and-scales:11}
Less. On the long arm, even a \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} load makes a big product, so the
pans level when the goods weigh \emph{less} than the honest blocks
on the short arm. The empty-scale check catches him: with nothing in
the pans, his scale already tips toward the long arm.
\end{solution}
