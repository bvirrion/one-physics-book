\chapter{Kinetic Energy and Road Safety}\label{ch:g9:kinetic-energy-safety}

Since the wind-up toys of your childhood, \omterm{def:g5:everyday-energy:energy}{energy} has been a
story of stores and forms --- vivid, useful, and unnumbered.
The wait ends now. Motion's \omterm{def:g5:everyday-energy:energy}{energy} gets its formula, \omterm{def:g5:everyday-energy:energy}{energy}
gets its \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{unit}, and the arithmetic turns out to govern a matter
of life and death: why a car at double \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} is four times
harder to stop, and what a single second of inattention costs
in \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres}.

\section{The energy of motion}

\begin{definition}[The joule]\label{def:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:joule}
\omterm{def:g5:everyday-energy:energy}{Energy} --- every form of it --- is measured in
\emph{joules}\index{joule} (\unit{J}), honoring the
experimenter who proved \omterm{def:g5:heat-and-insulation:heat}{heat} itself a form of \omterm{def:g5:everyday-energy:energy}{energy}. Anchors:
lifting this book from floor to shelf spends a few joules; a
beating heart, about one joule per beat; a chocolate bar
stores a million joules of food \omterm{def:g5:everyday-energy:energy}{energy}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Kinetic energy]\label{prop:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:formula}
A body of \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} $m$ (in \unit{kg}) moving at \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} $v$ (in
\unit{m/s}) carries, by virtue of its motion, the
\emph{kinetic energy}\index{kinetic energy}
\[
E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times m \times v^2 ,
\]
in \omterm{def:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:joule}{joules}. Two dials, unequal powers: doubling the \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass}
doubles $E_k$ --- but doubling the \emph{\omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed}}
\emph{quadruples} it, for the \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} enters squared. \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{Speed} is
the dangerous dial.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[First computations]\label{ex:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:first}
A \qty{0.45}{kg} football at \qty{20}{m/s}: $E_k = 0.5 \times
0.45 \times 20^2 = \qty{90}{J}$. A \qty{60}{kg} sprinter at
\qty{10}{m/s}: $0.5 \times 60 \times 100 = \qty{3000}{J}$. A
\qty{1000}{kg} car at \qty{50}{km/h} --- convert first:
$50 \div 3.6 \approx \qty{13.9}{m/s}$ --- $E_k = 0.5 \times
1000 \times 13.9^2 \approx \qty{9.7e4}{J}$: nearly a hundred
thousand \omterm{def:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:joule}{joules}. The same car at \qty{100}{km/h}: four times
as much --- \qty{3.9e5}{J}. The formula's warning, in
numbers.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Where braking sends it]\label{ex:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:brakes}
To stop, a car must hand its entire $E_k$ to something ---
\omterm{def:g5:everyday-energy:energy}{energy} is passed on, never erased, as the chains of your
childhood insisted. The brakes' grip converts it to \omterm{def:g5:heat-and-insulation:heat}{heat}:
discs glow amber after mountain descents, and the smell of
hot brakes \emph{is} \omterm{prop:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:formula}{kinetic energy} retiring. In a crash the
handover is violent and instantaneous --- into crumpled
steel. Every road-safety number below is this bookkeeping:
the bigger the $E_k$, the longer, or uglier, its retirement.
\end{example}

\section{The stopping distance}

\begin{proposition}[Stopping = thinking + braking]\label{prop:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:stopping}
A driver who spots danger stops only after two stretches of
road:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the \emph{reaction distance}\index{reaction distance}:
        the road covered while the brain notices and the foot
        moves --- about one full second at unchanged \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed}, so
        this stretch grows \emph{in proportion} to $v$;
  \item the \emph{braking distance}\index{braking distance}:
        the road the brakes need to retire the \omterm{prop:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:formula}{kinetic energy}
        --- and since $E_k$ carries $v^2$, this stretch grows
        with the \emph{square} of the \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed}: double \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed},
        fourfold braking road.
\end{enumerate}
Their sum, the \emph{stopping distance}, is the number that
meets the child chasing the ball.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The table every driver should own]\label{ex:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:table}
Dry road, alert driver (one-second reaction; good brakes):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|c|c|c}
\omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} & reaction & braking & stopping \\
\hline
\qty{50}{km/h} & \qty{14}{m} & \qty{13}{m} & \qty{27}{m} \\
\qty{90}{km/h} & \qty{25}{m} & \qty{40}{m} & \qty{65}{m} \\
\qty{130}{km/h} & \qty{36}{m} & \qty{85}{m} & \qty{121}{m}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Read it twice. At city \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed}, a bus-length and a half; at
highway \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed}, a full running track and a fifth. And the
proportions confirm the two laws: reaction grows like $v$,
braking like $v^2$ --- at high \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed}, braking devours the
total.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Estimating a stopping distance]\label{met:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:estimate}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item convert the \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} to \unit{m/s} (divide \unit{km/h}
        by $3.6$);
  \item \omterm{prop:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:stopping}{reaction distance}: one second's travel --- the
        \unit{m/s} number itself, in \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres};
  \item \omterm{prop:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:stopping}{braking distance}: scale a known anchor by the square
        --- from \qty{13}{m} at \qty{50}{km/h}, multiply by
        $(v/50)^2$;
  \item add, and compare with what the road ahead actually
        offers.
\end{enumerate}
Wet roads double the braking share; tired or distracted
drivers stretch the reaction second toward two --- rerun the
sum with the honest inputs.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[The price of a glance]\label{ex:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:glance}
A two-second glance at a phone at \qty{90}{km/h}: the car
covers $2 \times 25 = \qty{50}{m}$ --- half a football pitch
--- driven blind, before any reaction second even begins. At
city \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} the same glance blindly spends \qty{28}{m}: past
the crossing, past the school gate. The most dangerous
component of the car is unmeasured by any formula here: the
driver's attention.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Belts, bags and helmets]\label{rem:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:belts}
In a collision the car stops in a \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metre} of crumpling --- but
an unbelted passenger continues at full \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} (the quiet
sentence of last chapter, grimly applied) until stopped by
whatever comes: dashboard, windscreen, road. Seat belts and
airbags stop the body over a longer distance and time,
retiring its \omterm{prop:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:formula}{kinetic energy} gently instead of all at once;
the cyclist's helmet does the same for the skull's
irreplaceable contents, its crushable foam buying
\omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{centimetres} of gentle stopping. None of them reduce your
$E_k$ by a \omterm{def:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:joule}{joule} --- they civilize its retirement.
\end{remark}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:2}
Compute $E_k$: a \qty{2}{kg} hare at \qty{10}{m/s}; an

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:3}
Which raises a car's $E_k$ more: adding half its \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} in
cargo, or raising its \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} by half? Show both factors.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:4}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:5}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:6}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:7}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:8}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:9}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:10}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:11}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:12}

\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Safety Campaign}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the class designs the
town's road-safety campaign; posters checked by formula; the
mayor's difficult questions}]\label{pb:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:1}
The town commissions a campaign, on one condition: every
number on every poster must survive a physics audit. The
class calculates. (Anchors: reaction one second; braking
\qty{13}{m} at \qty{50}{km/h}, scaling by the square; car
\omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} \qty{1000}{kg}.)

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Poster one: ``50 not
60''.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute both \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speeds} in \unit{m/s} (one decimal).
  \item \omterm{prop:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:stopping}{Reaction distances} at both \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speeds}?
  \item \omterm{prop:g9:kinetic-energy-safety:stopping}{Braking distances} at both (scale the anchor)?
  \item The poster's headline: ``Ten more \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{units} of \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed},
        --- how many more \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres} of stopping?'' Fill in the
        number.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Poster two: ``The wall
of \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed}''.}
The design shows crash energies as fall heights: a crash at
\omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} $v$ ``equals'' falling from the height where the same
\omterm{def:g5:everyday-energy:energy}{energy} would be gained.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the car's $E_k$ at \qty{50}{km/h} (from
        Part I) and at \qty{90}{km/h}.
  \item The artists need heights: falling from \qty{1}{m}
        gains about \qty{9.8}{kJ} for this car ($m g h$ ---
        next chapter's other formula, borrowed early). To
        what fall heights do the two crash energies
        correspond? (Divide; round to the \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metre}.)
  \item Which everyday buildings match those heights? Draft
        the poster's line.
  \item The mayor objects: ``Nobody drives into walls;
        cars crumple, belts catch.'' Defend the poster's
        physics while conceding his point --- what do
        crumple zones and belts change, and what number do
        they not change?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Poster three: ``One
second''.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item At \qty{90}{km/h}, how many \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres} does one
        distracted second cost before any braking? And a
        two-second phone glance?
  \item The tired-driver variant: reaction stretched to two
        seconds. Recompute the full stopping distance at
        \qty{90}{km/h} and compare with the alert
        driver's \qty{65}{m}.
  \item A skeptical councillor: ``Surely a second matters
        little beside those big braking numbers.'' At which
        \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speeds} is he most wrong --- low or high? (Compare
        the two components' growth laws.)
  \item Sign off the campaign: three poster-bottom lines,
        one per poster, each carrying its formula in
        plain words.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
