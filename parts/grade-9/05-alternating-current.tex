\chapter{Alternating Current}\label{ch:g9:alternating-current}

Every circuit you have ever built ran on a \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}: current
marching steadily from $+$ to $-$, dutiful as a clock's hand.
But the sockets in your walls deliver something wilder --- a
current that reverses direction a hundred times each second,
its \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage} swinging ceaselessly between plus and minus. Meet
the \omterm{def:g9:alternating-current:dcac}{alternating current}: the form in which virtually all the
world's electricity is born, shipped and delivered.

\section{Two kinds of current}

\begin{definition}[Direct and alternating current]\label{def:g9:alternating-current:dcac}
A \emph{direct current}\index{direct current} (DC) flows
always in the same direction, driven by a steady \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage} ---
the \omterm{def:g7:short-circuits-safety:battery}{battery's} gift, constant until the store runs down. An
\emph{alternating current}\index{alternating current} (AC)
reverses direction periodically, driven by a \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage} that
swings smoothly from positive to negative and back, over and
over --- the socket's regime, and the alternator's (next
chapter tells whose).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Period and frequency]\label{def:g9:alternating-current:period}
The \emph{period}\index{period} $T$ of an alternating \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage}
is the \omterm{def:g2:measuring-time:duration}{duration} of one complete cycle --- one full swing up,
down and back, in seconds. Its \emph{\omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{frequency}} $f$ is the
number of cycles per second, in \omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{hertz} --- \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound}'s \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{unit},
serving again --- and the two are each other's reciprocals:
\[
f = \frac{1}{T}, \qquad T = \frac{1}{f}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The mains' vital statistics]\label{prop:g9:alternating-current:mains}
Across most of the world the mains alternates at
$f = \qty{50}{Hz}$ --- fifty full cycles per second, a \omterm{def:g9:alternating-current:period}{period}
of $T = 1/50 = \qty{0.02}{s}$: twenty milliseconds per swing
(some regions run at sixty). The current in your reading
lamp's cord reverses direction a hundred times each second
--- twice per cycle --- and has never once marched steadily
from one \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{terminal} to the other.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Frequency arithmetic]\label{ex:g9:alternating-current:arithmetic}
The reciprocal pair computes both ways. A generator cycling at
\qty{60}{Hz}: $T = 1/60 \approx \qty{0.017}{s}$ --- seventeen
milliseconds. An \omterm{met:g9:alternating-current:scope}{oscilloscope} shows a cycle lasting
\qty{0.04}{s}: $f = 1/0.04 = \qty{25}{Hz}$. A bicycle dynamo
at slow pedaling makes \qty{10}{Hz}: \omterm{def:g9:alternating-current:period}{period} a tenth of a
second --- and its lamp visibly flickers, ten pulses to the
second, until \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} smooths them beyond the eye's noticing.
\end{example}

\section{Reading the swing}

\begin{method}[Reading an oscilloscope trace]\label{met:g9:alternating-current:scope}
The \emph{oscilloscope}\index{oscilloscope} --- or any
analyzer app --- draws \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage} against time on a squared
screen; each square's worth in \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{volts} and in milliseconds is
set by two dials. To harvest a trace:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item count squares for one full cycle and multiply by the
        time-per-square: that is $T$; then $f = 1/T$;
  \item count squares from the middle line to a crest and
        multiply by the volts-per-square: the \emph{peak
        \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage}} $U_{\text{max}}$;
  \item check the shape: mains and alternators draw the
        smooth, endlessly repeated wave of the figure ---
        the \emph{sinusoid}, mathematics' name for the
        perfect swing.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{proposition}[Effective voltage]\label{prop:g9:alternating-current:effective}
What is ``the'' \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage} of a swing that visits every value
from $+U_{\text{max}}$ to $-U_{\text{max}}$ a hundred times a
second? The honest single number is the \emph{effective
voltage}\index{effective voltage} $U$: the steady DC \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage}
that would \omterm{def:g5:heat-and-insulation:heat}{heat} a \omterm{def:g8:ohms-law:resistor}{resistor} equally well. For the sinusoid it
sits well below the crest --- $U_{\text{max}}$ is about $1.4$
times $U$ --- and it is what every AC \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltmeter}{voltmeter} is built to
read. The famous ``\qty{230}{V}'' of the socket is the
effective value; the crests actually reach about
\qty{325}{V}, fifty times a second, each way.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Which devices care]\label{ex:g9:alternating-current:whocares}
The \omterm{prop:g8:electric-current-effects:three}{heating effect} works fine either way --- a filament or
kettle coil warms regardless of the march's direction, which
is why lamps and heaters drink AC without complaint. But
electronics --- phones, laptops, anything with chips ---
need steady DC: their charger bricks and hidden power
supplies convert the socket's swing into a battery-like
constancy (feel the brick's warmth: conversion pays its
tax). And the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery} world cannot charge on a swing either:
charging is chemistry run backward, and chemistry insists on
a consistent direction.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Why the world chose the swing]\label{rem:g9:alternating-current:why}
If batteries are so agreeably steady, why is civilization
wired for AC? Two reasons, both waiting two chapters ahead:
\omterm{def:g9:alternating-current:dcac}{alternating current} is what spinning machines \emph{naturally
make} --- and, decisively, only AC feeds the transformer,
the humble humming box that lets electricity travel a country
at colossal \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage} and enter your home at a tame one. The
grid is alternating because the journey demands it; the next
two chapters follow the current from spinning coil to
kitchen wall.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The swing and the body]\label{rem:g9:alternating-current:safety}
Safety's old arithmetic gains one grim detail: the mains'
fifty swings a second lie near the rhythm that most easily
disrupts the heart's own electrical timing --- alternating
shocks are, \omterm{def:g8:intensity-ammeter:intensity}{ampere} for \omterm{def:g8:intensity-ammeter:intensity}{ampere}, more dangerous than steady
ones. Every childhood rule stands, doubled: the socket's
\qty{230}{V} effective --- \qty{325}{V} at crest --- is not
merely a bigger \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}. It is a different, less forgiving
animal.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:1}
Distinguish DC from AC in one sentence each, with each one's
household source.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:2}
Define \omterm{def:g9:alternating-current:period}{period} and \omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{frequency} and write their reciprocal
relation. Compute $T$ for \qty{50}{Hz} and $f$ for
$T = \qty{0.01}{s}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:3}
How many times per second does the mains current reverse
direction at \qty{50}{Hz}? (Careful: reversals per cycle.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:4}
A scope shows one cycle spanning $4$ squares at
\qty{5}{ms} per square. Find $T$ and $f$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:5}
The socket says \qty{230}{V}; the crest reaches
\qty{325}{V}. Name the two values and the meaning of the
smaller one.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:6}
Why do kettles accept AC happily while phone chargers must
convert it? One effect, one chemistry.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:7}
Sketch (or describe) the two traces: a \qty{9}{V} \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery},
and an alternating \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage} of \omterm{def:g9:alternating-current:period}{period} \qty{20}{ms} and peak
\qty{9}{V}. What single instrument-drawn feature tells them
apart at a glance?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:8}
Give the two reasons civilization wired itself for AC, as
previewed by the chapter.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:9}
A slow bicycle dynamo \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} its lamp with visible flicker;
pedaling harder steadies the glow. Explain both regimes with
\omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{frequency} --- and estimate the \omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{frequency} below which
flicker annoys, given that cinema fools the eye at about
$24$ pictures per second.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:10}
An AC \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltmeter}{voltmeter} across a socket reads \qty{230}{V}. A
(theoretical) fearless \omterm{met:g9:alternating-current:scope}{oscilloscope} across the same socket
shows crests at what value --- and how many crests per
second, counting both signs?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:11}
A region's grid runs at \qty{60}{Hz} with effective
\qty{120}{V}. Compute its \omterm{def:g9:alternating-current:period}{period} and approximate peak
\omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage} --- and name which of your travel devices would
object (consult
\cref{ex:g9:alternating-current:whocares}; modern chargers
read the fine print on their labels).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g9:alternating-current:12}
Old films of screens and fluorescent lamps sometimes show
dark rolling bars --- the camera's frames catching the
lamp's brightness mid-swing. A mains lamp at \qty{50}{Hz}
brightens at every crest of \emph{either} sign: how many
brightness pulses per second? A camera at $25$ frames per
second samples that pulsing: reason out why the beat between
the two rhythms paints slow-moving bars, and why filming in
a \qty{60}{Hz} region changes the pattern.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Oscilloscope Bench}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- qualification night on
the oscilloscope bench; four mystery supplies, one screen;
the technician's ledger}]\label{pb:g9:alternating-current:1}
Four unlabeled supplies must be identified from their
traces. The scope's dials: \qty{5}{ms} per horizontal
square, \qty{2}{V} per vertical square (except where
noted). You keep the ledger.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The four traces.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Supply A: a flat line, $2.25$ squares above the
        middle. Identify its kind and \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage} --- and its
        likely household identity.
  \item Supply B: a smooth wave, one cycle per $4$
        squares, crests $3$ squares high. Kind, \omterm{def:g9:alternating-current:period}{period},
        \omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{frequency}, peak \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{voltage}?
  \item Supply C: a flat line \emph{below} the middle by
        $2.25$ squares. What is it, and what tiny error
        explains it?
  \item Supply D: a smooth wave, one cycle per $2$
        squares, crests $1.5$ squares. Full statistics ---
        and why D's lamp would flicker less than B's if
        both fed one.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The technician's
questions.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item An AC \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltmeter}{voltmeter} across supply B reads about
        \qty{4.2}{V}, though its crests hit \qty{6}{V}.
        Reconcile the two numbers.
  \item Which of the four supplies could charge a phone
        directly, and what must stand between the others
        and its \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}?
  \item The bench's warning plate: ``AC bites worse,
        \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{volt} for \omterm{def:g8:voltage-voltmeter:voltage}{volt}.'' Restate the reason.
  \item A trainee asks why the scope draws B's wave so
        smoothly rounded rather than zigzag. Name the
        shape and its origin (what kind of machine, next
        chapter, draws it naturally?).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The grid's own
trace.}
A safely isolated demonstrator shows the mains, at
\qty{100}{V} and \qty{5}{ms} per square.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Predict the trace: squares per cycle, and crest
        height in squares.
  \item From the trace, recover $f$ and the \omterm{prop:g9:alternating-current:effective}{effective
        voltage} --- and check both against the socket's
        famous label.
  \item The demonstrator is switched to a \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery} bank of
        equal effective heating power. Describe the new
        trace in one line.
  \item Close the ledger with the technician's three-line
        summary: what the flat line means, what the wave
        means, and which single number lets the two be
        fairly compared.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
