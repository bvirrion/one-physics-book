\chapter{Air Pressure and Weather}\label{ch:g5:air-pressure-weather}

You live at the bottom of an ocean --- an ocean of \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}, hundreds of
kilometres deep, with the whole \omterm{def:g4:weight-and-mass:weight}{weight} of it resting on your
shoulders. Why does it not crush you? Why do you not even feel it?
And what does any of this have to do with tomorrow's weather? \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{Air},
our old invisible friend, has one more big secret to give up.

\section{Air has weight}

\begin{example}[Weighing the invisible]\label{ex:g5:air-pressure-weather:weighing}
A firm football is placed on a fine \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:scale}{balance scale} and balanced
exactly. Then its valve is opened and the extra \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} hisses out ---
same ball, same valve, same everything, minus some \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}. Back on the
scale: the deflated ball is \emph{lighter}. The difference is the
\omterm{def:g4:weight-and-mass:weight}{weight} of the escaped \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}. \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{Air} is stuff, stuff has \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass}, and \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass}
is pulled down by the Earth: the ocean above us is heavy.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[The atmosphere and its pressure]\label{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}
The \emph{atmosphere}\index{atmosphere} is the deep layer of \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}
wrapped around the whole Earth. Its \omterm{def:g4:weight-and-mass:weight}{weight} squeezes the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} at the
bottom --- where we live --- and squeezed \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} pushes hard on every
surface it touches. That push is called \emph{air
pressure}\index{air pressure}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The push from all sides]\label{prop:g5:air-pressure-weather:allsides}
\omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{Air pressure} pushes on every surface from \emph{every} direction
--- downward on tables, sideways on walls, even \emph{upward} on
ceilings and palms. And it is mighty: on an area the size of your
palm, the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} pushes about as hard as the \omterm{def:g4:weight-and-mass:weight}{weight} of a large,
full suitcase. We never notice, because the pushes come balanced:
\omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} inside us and behind every surface pushes back just as hard.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[The card that holds back water]\label{met:g5:air-pressure-weather:card}
\omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{Air}'s upward push, caught red-handed (do it over the \omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{sink}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item fill a glass with water to the very brim;
  \item lay a stiff, smooth card flat on top, touching the water
        all around;
  \item hold the card in place, turn the glass exactly upside
        down, and gently let the card go.
\end{enumerate}
The card stays --- holding back the whole glassful. The water's
\omterm{def:g4:weight-and-mass:weight}{weight} presses down on the card, but the \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{atmosphere} presses
\emph{up} on it harder still. You have just seen the suitcase-push
from below.
\end{method}

\section{Machines run by the atmosphere}

\begin{example}[The straw]\label{ex:g5:air-pressure-weather:straw}
You think you pull juice up a straw. In truth, you cannot pull a
\omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} at all --- try pulling water with your fingers! What you
really do is suck the \emph{\omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}} out of the straw. With the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}
gone from inside, the \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{atmosphere} pressing down on the juice in the
glass pushes juice up the empty straw to your mouth. Every sip is
the \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{atmosphere} doing the lifting; you only clear the road.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Suction cups and crushed bottles]\label{ex:g5:air-pressure-weather:suction}
The bathroom hook's rubber cup is pressed flat to the tiles,
squeezing the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} out from underneath: now the \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{atmosphere} pushes
the cup against the wall with nothing behind it pushing back ---
pinned by the sky itself. And screw the cap onto an empty plastic
bottle high on a mountain, then drive down into the valley: the
bottle crumples as if squeezed by a ghost hand. More \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} presses
outside than the thin mountain \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} sealed inside --- the
\omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{atmosphere} wins, and the bottle folds.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Thinner with height]\label{ex:g5:air-pressure-weather:height}
Climb, and you leave part of the ocean below you: less \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} above
means a weaker push. Your ears pop in a fast cable car as the
pressure outside changes; mountaineers gasp in \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} grown thin. And
here the mountain-tea mystery of last year surrenders: with the
\omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{atmosphere} pressing less on the water, boiling comes easier ---
water on great peaks boils well below \qty{100}{\celsius}, too
cool for a proper brew.
\end{example}

\section{The barometer and the weather}

\begin{definition}[Barometer]\label{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:barometer}
A \emph{barometer}\index{barometer} is the instrument that \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:measure}{measures}
\omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{air pressure} --- one more member of our family of honest judges,
beside the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} and the scale. Its needle rises when the
\omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{atmosphere} above presses harder, and falls when the press
lightens.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Pressure talks about tomorrow]\label{prop:g5:air-pressure-weather:weather}
The \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{atmosphere}'s pressure is restless: heavy, sinking \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} brings
calm, clear skies; \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}, rising \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} invites clouds and rain. So
the \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:barometer}{barometer} is a fortune teller of a modest, honest kind:
\emph{steady or rising} pressure promises fair weather;
\emph{falling} pressure warns of clouds coming; a \emph{fast fall}
means a storm may be on its way.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[The weather map's H and L]\label{rem:g5:air-pressure-weather:map}
Tonight's weather report will show great swirls labeled H and L:
regions of high and low pressure drifting across the map like
slow whales. \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{Air} forever pours from the heavy H regions toward the
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} L regions --- and that pouring \emph{is the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:wind}{wind}}. One
chapter, three old friends explained: the \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:barometer}{barometer}'s forecast,
the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:wind}{wind}'s origin, and the reason your ears know when the weather
is about to break.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:air-pressure-weather:1}
How does the football experiment show that \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} has \omterm{def:g4:weight-and-mass:weight}{weight}? What
exactly is compared?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:air-pressure-weather:2}
What is \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{air pressure}, and where does it come from? In which
directions does it push?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:air-pressure-weather:3}
The \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{atmosphere} presses on your palm about as hard as a full
suitcase weighs --- yet you feel nothing. Why not?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:air-pressure-weather:4}
In the card trick, two pushes fight over the card. Name them, and
say which one wins.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:air-pressure-weather:5}
``I pull the juice up the straw.'' Correct this sentence: what do
you really remove, and who does the lifting?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:air-pressure-weather:6}
Why does a suction cup grip the tiles? What was squeezed out, and
what holds the cup on?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:air-pressure-weather:7}
What happens to \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{air pressure} as you climb a mountain, and why?
Give two signs of it a traveler can notice.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:air-pressure-weather:8}
What does a \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:barometer}{barometer} \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:measure}{measure}? Your \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:barometer}{barometer} has been falling
fast since breakfast --- what should the picnic plans expect?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g5:air-pressure-weather:9}
The sealed bottle crumpled on the drive down from the mountain.
Explain the crumpling with pushes inside and outside --- and
predict: what would happen instead to a bottle sealed in the
valley and carried \emph{up}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g5:air-pressure-weather:10}
Where does \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:wind}{wind} come from, according to the weather map's H and L
swirls? Connect it to the oldest definition in this book: \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:wind}{wind} is
\omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} on the move --- moved by what?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g5:air-pressure-weather:11}
Mountain climbers complain that on great peaks, tea never brews
strong and pasta stays hard however long it boils. Gather the
whole explanation across two years: what does the thin \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{atmosphere}
do to the \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:boiling-point}{boiling point}, and why does longer boiling not help?
(Two propositions, one from last year, close this case.)
\end{exercise}
