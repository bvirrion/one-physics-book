\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g5:stars-night-sky} --- Stars and the Night Sky}

\begin{solution}{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:1}
A \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} is a sun: a gigantic ball of glowing-hot stuff making its
own \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}. They look like sparks only because of their distance ---
even \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} spends years on the road from the nearest one.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:2}
Yes --- they shine at noon exactly as at midnight. The daytime sky
is flooded with sunlight scattered by the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}, and no spark can
show beside that glare, as no candle shows beside a bonfire.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:3}
The blue-white one --- \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} colors run from orange (coolest)
through yellow to blue-white (hottest). A coal glowing orange
versus steel heated white-hot tells the same story.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:4}
A named pattern of bright \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}, invented as a sky map and memory
aid. Its \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} are not real neighbors --- they only happen to line
up as seen from Earth.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:5}
Find the Big Dipper; take the two pointer \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} at the front edge
of its pan; prolong their line about five spacings across the sky;
the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} reached is the \omterm{met:g5:stars-night-sky:northstar}{North Star}, and below it on the horizon
lies north.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:6}
It stands almost exactly over the Earth's spin axis, so the
wheeling of the sky leaves it nearly motionless --- a fixed mark of
north, replacing the \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:7}
The Earth is turning, carrying us around once a day. The same spin
parades the Sun across the daytime sky --- one turning, two
parades.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:8}
Countless faint \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}, too crowded to separate by eye: the \omterm{ex:g5:stars-night-sky:milkyway}{Milky
Way} --- our own flat city of \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}, seen edgewise from inside.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:9}
The spinning Earth wheels the whole sky around the axis through the
\omterm{met:g5:stars-night-sky:northstar}{North Star}. Near that still point, \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} ride small circles ---
the camera records rings. Toward the east, the wheeling carries
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} up over the horizon along slanting paths --- the camera
records rising streaks. One spin, two views of it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:10}
Direction: the Dipper's pointers give her the \omterm{met:g5:stars-night-sky:northstar}{North Star}, and with
it north --- no needle required. Clock: the Dipper itself wheels
around the \omterm{met:g5:stars-night-sky:northstar}{North Star} like the hand of a great clock, one full
turn per day; watching how far it has swung tells her how the
night is passing.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:11}
No: you see the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} as it \emph{was}, years ago, when tonight's
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} left it --- the messenger, not the sender. Every telescope
therefore looks into the past: the farther the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star}, the older the
news --- which is precisely what makes distant \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} so precious
to astronomers.
\end{solution}
