\chapter{Stars and the Night Sky}\label{ch:g5:stars-night-sky}

Far from the city lamps, on a clear moonless night, the sky is
crowded: thousands of sparks, faint and fierce, white and
orange. Sailors steered by them, farmers planted by them, and every
science began by wondering at them. What are they --- and why do
they wheel so tidily overhead while we sleep?

\section{What a star is}

\begin{example}[Suns beyond counting]\label{ex:g5:stars-night-sky:suns}
Each \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} is a sun: a gigantic ball of glowing-hot stuff, pouring
out its own \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} --- many are mightier than our own Sun. They look
like humble sparks for one reason only: distance. The Sun is our
next-door \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star}; the others are so far that even \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}, the champion
runner that crosses a room in no time at all, spends \emph{years}
on the road from the nearest of them. The night sky is a crowd of
suns, shrunk by distance to glitter.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Star colors]\label{ex:g5:stars-night-sky:colors}
Look carefully and the sparks are not all white: some burn
orange-red, some yellow like our Sun, some blue-white. The color is
a message about heat --- and it runs opposite to taps and paint
pots: the orange \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} are the \emph{coolest} of the family, and
the blue-white ones the hottest, hotter far than the Sun. A
glowing coal and a white-hot blade of steel tell the same story
here on Earth: hotter glows whiter.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Where the stars go by day]\label{rem:g5:stars-night-sky:daytime}
The \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} never leave. They shine above you at noon exactly as at
midnight --- but the daytime sky is flooded with sunlight scattered
by the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}, a blue glare far brighter than any spark. A candle is
invisible beside a bonfire. When the Sun sets and the glare drains
away, the sky's permanent crowd simply becomes visible again.
\end{remark}

\section{Maps made of stars}

\begin{definition}[Constellation]\label{def:g5:stars-night-sky:constellation}
A \emph{constellation}\index{constellation} is a named pattern of
bright \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} --- a hunter, a swan, a great bear --- invented by
sky-watchers long ago as a map and a memory aid. The \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} of a
constellation are not really neighbors; they only line up as seen
from Earth. But the patterns are steady: your grandparents learned
the very same shapes.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Finding the North Star]\label{met:g5:stars-night-sky:northstar}
The sky offers a free \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item find the Big Dipper --- seven bright \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}, a saucepan with
        a long handle, easy to spot through the year;
  \item take the two \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} at the front edge of the pan --- the
        \emph{pointers};
  \item prolong the line from one to the other, onward across the
        sky, about five times their spacing;
  \item the modest \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} you land on is the \emph{North
        Star}\index{North Star} --- hang a plumb line from it down
        to the horizon, and there is north.
\end{enumerate}
Check it against your \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass}: needle and \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} agree. Sailors bet
their lives on that agreement for centuries.
\end{method}

\section{The wheeling sky}

\begin{proposition}[The sky turns around the North Star]\label{prop:g5:stars-night-sky:wheel}
Through the night, the whole crowd of \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} wheels slowly across
the sky in great circles --- all around one nearly motionless
point: the \omterm{met:g5:stars-night-sky:northstar}{North Star}. Nothing up there truly moves so tidily; it
is our own Earth spinning, as it spins the Sun across the daytime
sky. The \omterm{met:g5:stars-night-sky:northstar}{North Star} barely stirs because it happens to stand
almost exactly over the Earth's spin axis --- the one direction
the spin leaves in peace.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Our city of stars]\label{ex:g5:stars-night-sky:milkyway}
On the darkest nights, a faint milky band arches right across the
sky: the \emph{Milky Way}\index{Milky Way}. Binoculars dissolve the
milk into truth: countless \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}, too faint and crowded to
separate by eye. It is our home city of \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} seen from inside ---
a flat, swirling island of more suns than there are grains of sand
on a beach, with our Sun living in a quiet suburb. Every \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} you
can see by eye is a neighbor from this one city.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Keep looking up]\label{rem:g5:stars-night-sky:lookup}
Astronomy is the oldest science, and the only one whose laboratory
opens free of charge every clear night. A blanket, a dark spot away
from streetlights, patience while the eyes wake to the dark ---
that is the whole equipment list. The Dipper, the \omterm{met:g5:stars-night-sky:northstar}{North Star}, a
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet} or two and the \omterm{ex:g5:stars-night-sky:milkyway}{Milky Way} make a fine first expedition; the
deeper laws of the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}' \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} will find you well prepared, years
from now, near the end of this course.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:1}
What is a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star}? Why do \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} look like tiny sparks although many
outshine our Sun?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:2}
Are the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} still in the sky at noon? Why can we not see them?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:3}
An orange \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} and a blue-white \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} shine side by side. Which is
hotter? What everyday glow tells the same color story?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:4}
What is a \omterm{def:g5:stars-night-sky:constellation}{constellation}? Are its \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} truly close to one another?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:5}
Describe, step by step, how to find the \omterm{met:g5:stars-night-sky:northstar}{North Star} from the Big
Dipper.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:6}
What is special about the \omterm{met:g5:stars-night-sky:northstar}{North Star}'s place in the wheeling sky,
and what everyday instrument does it replace?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:7}
The \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} wheel across the night in tidy circles. What is really
turning? What else does the same turning parade across the daytime
sky?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:8}
What does the milky band across the darkest skies turn out to be,
seen through binoculars? What is it really?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:9}
Left alone all night, a camera aimed at the \omterm{met:g5:stars-night-sky:northstar}{North Star} records
circles; aimed at the eastern horizon instead, it records rising
slanted streaks. Explain both pictures with one spinning Earth.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:10}
A shipwrecked sailor on a clear night has no \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass}, no phone ---
and no hurry. Explain how the sky hands her both a direction and,
with patience, a clock. (Two chapter ideas, one wheeling sky.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g5:stars-night-sky:11}
The \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} of a far \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} spends years on the road to your eye.
When you look at that \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} tonight, are you seeing it as it is
\emph{now}? What are you actually looking at --- and why does this
make every telescope a kind of time machine?
\end{exercise}
