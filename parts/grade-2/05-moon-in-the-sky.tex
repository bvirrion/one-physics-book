\chapter{The Moon in the Sky}\label{ch:g2:moon-in-the-sky}

Some evenings a thin silver smile hangs over the rooftops; two weeks
later, a full round lantern floods the garden with pale \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}. It is
the same \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} --- our closest neighbor in the sky, and the only other
world people have ever walked on. Time to get properly acquainted.

\section{Our neighbor in the sky}

\begin{definition}[The Moon]\label{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}
The \emph{Moon}\index{Moon} is a huge ball of gray rock that travels
around the Earth, our faithful companion in space. It is much
smaller than the Earth, and very far away --- farther than any
airplane flies, so far that the astronauts who visited it traveled
for three days to get there.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Looking closely]\label{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:closely}
To the plain eye the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} shows pale patches and bright zones ---
some people see a face in them. Through binoculars (a wonderful
thing to try on a full-moon night) the patches sharpen into plains
and round \emph{craters}, bowl-shaped scars left by rocks from space
that crashed there long ago. The \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} keeps its scars forever: it
has no \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:wind}{wind} and no rain to wipe them away --- indeed it has no \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}
at all. No \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} also means a silent world: on the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}, even a shout
makes no sound.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The Moon that follows you]\label{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:follows}
Walk down the street on a moonlit evening: houses and trees slide
past, but the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} seems to follow you, always at your shoulder. It
is not really chasing you --- it is so far away that walking a whole
street changes your view of it not at all, while the nearby houses
change all the time. Very distant things seem to follow every
traveler; the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} does it best because it is the champion of far.
\end{example}

\section{Reflected light}

\begin{example}[Moonlight is reflected sunlight]\label{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:borrowed}
Is the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} a \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light source}? Remember the test from last year: does
it make its own \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}? It does not --- the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} is a ball of dark
rock, like a huge pebble in the sky. What we call moonlight is
sunlight \emph{reflected} by that rock --- bounced back toward us,
the way a white wall \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights}
a room without being a lamp. The Sun \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} bright on one
side; we see whatever part of that bright side faces us.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[A daytime Moon]\label{rem:g2:moon-in-the-sky:daytime}
Many people believe the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} is a creature of the night --- but look
up on some afternoons and there it is, a pale ghost in the blue sky!
The \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} travels on its own schedule around the Earth and does not
care whether it is our daytime or our night. It simply shows less
proudly by day, when the Sun's glare fills the sky.
\end{remark}

\section{The Moon changes shape}

\begin{example}[The Moon's changing shapes]\label{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}
Watch the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} over many evenings and it changes shape: a thin
\emph{crescent}\index{crescent} like a fingernail clipping, then a
half-circle, then a fat almost-round shape, then the perfect disc of
the \emph{full moon}\index{full moon} --- and then back down again,
to a \omterm{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}{crescent} facing the other way, until for a night or two the
\omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} seems to vanish entirely. The whole show takes about a month,
and then it repeats, as reliably as day and night.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Keeping a Moon diary]\label{met:g2:moon-in-the-sky:diary}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Each clear evening, at about the same hour, look for the
        \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon};
  \item draw the shape you see in a little calendar square --- a
        circle, and shade the dark part;
  \item if clouds hide it, write ``clouds'' and keep going;
  \item after two weeks, lay your drawings in a row.
\end{enumerate}
Your own drawings will show the shape changing, a little more each
night. \emph{Why} the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} does this is
a delicious puzzle --- it will be solved properly in a later year of
this course, and your diary is the first step of the proof.
\end{method}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:moon-in-the-sky:1}
Is the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} bigger or smaller than the Earth? Nearer or farther than
the clouds? How do we know people can visit it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:moon-in-the-sky:2}
What are the round bowl-shaped marks on the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} called, and what
made them? Why are they still there after so long?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:moon-in-the-sky:3}
Is the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} a \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light source}, like a lamp, or a light-catcher, like a
white wall? Where does moonlight really come from?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:moon-in-the-sky:4}
Which half of the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} does the Sun \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}: always the half facing
the Sun, always the same half, or a different half at random?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:moon-in-the-sky:5}
Can the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} ever be seen in the daytime? If a friend says no, what
would convince them?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:moon-in-the-sky:6}
Name the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}'s shapes in order, starting from the thin fingernail
clipping and ending at the round lantern. About how long does the
whole show take before it repeats?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:moon-in-the-sky:7}
On the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} there is no \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}. What would happen to a shout there?
And to a kite?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:moon-in-the-sky:8}
Start the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} diary of \cref{met:g2:moon-in-the-sky:diary} tonight.
What two things must stay the same each evening to make the diary
fair?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g2:moon-in-the-sky:9}
``The \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} followed our car all the way home!'' says little Rosa.
Explain to her, with the street-walk of
\cref{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:follows}, why very far things seem to
follow, while nearby things slide past.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g2:moon-in-the-sky:10}
On a \omterm{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}{crescent} night, look carefully: sometimes the \emph{dark} part
of the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} glows very faintly, so the whole circle can just be
guessed. The Sun does not shine on that part --- but something else
shines nearby. What big, bright, sunlit ball could be lighting the
\omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}'s dark side, the way the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} our nights? (Hint: you are
standing on it.)
\end{exercise}
