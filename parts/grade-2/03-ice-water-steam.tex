\chapter{Ice, Water, Steam}\label{ch:g2:ice-water-steam}

The same water that clinks in your glass as an ice cube pours as a
drink and rises from the soup pot as a white plume. One single stuff,
three \emph{states}. Last year we watched warmth \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melt} an ice cube;
this year we meet all three states of matter that water can take.

\section{Water's three states}

\begin{example}[Where each state lives]\label{ex:g2:ice-water-steam:where}
Water can be in three \emph{states of matter}. \emph{Solid}: ice ---
ice cubes, icicles under the roof, frost on the grass, snow. A solid
is hard and keeps its shape. \emph{Liquid}: the water that pours ---
rain, rivers, the sea, your glass. A liquid flows and takes the shape
of its cup. \emph{Gas}: water vapor, water flying invisibly in the
air. Solid, liquid, gas --- but always the same stuff underneath.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Water vapor is invisible]\label{rem:g2:ice-water-steam:invisible}
The white plume you see over the pot is made of tiny floating water
droplets --- little drops of \emph{liquid}. The true gas --- water
vapor mixed into the air --- cannot be seen at all! Look closely at
the pot's spout: just above it there is a short \emph{clear} gap
before the white plume begins. In that gap the water is a gas,
invisible; a little higher it gathers into the droplets you can see.
Invisible water vapor is hiding in the air of every room, and we
will catch it red-handed before the chapter ends.
\end{remark}

\section{Changing state}

\begin{definition}[Melting and freezing]\label{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}
When warmth turns solid ice into liquid water, the ice
\emph{melts}\index{melt}. When strong cold turns liquid water solid,
the water \emph{freezes}\index{freeze}. Melting and freezing are
opposite doors between the same two states: warmth pushes one way,
cold pushes the other.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Evaporating and condensing]\label{def:g2:ice-water-steam:evapcond}
When warmth makes liquid water fly off into the air as a gas, the
water \emph{evaporates}\index{evaporate} --- the puddle shrinks, the
laundry dries. When water vapor touches something cold and turns
back into liquid droplets, it \emph{condenses}\index{condense} ---
mist on the cold window, fog on the bathroom mirror. Again two
opposite doors: warmth sends water into the gas state, cold calls it
back to the liquid.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Try it: catch the invisible water]\label{ex:g2:ice-water-steam:catch}
Take a can or a glass straight from the refrigerator, dry its
outside carefully with a towel, and set it on the table. Wait while
you count to $100$. The outside turns misty, then wet --- drops
appear from nowhere! They are not leaking through the glass: the
invisible water vapor in the room's air touched the cold wall and
condensed. You just caught the invisible gas.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Breath clouds]\label{ex:g2:ice-water-steam:breath}
Your breath always carries invisible water vapor. On a warm day it
stays invisible. On a freezing morning, the cold outdoor air
\omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:evapcond}{condenses} it at once into a little cloud in front of your mouth ---
and the same happens on a cold window when you breathe on it to draw
with your finger.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Steam and stoves: careful]\label{rem:g2:ice-water-steam:safety}
The water vapor above a boiling pot is burning hot --- hotter than
the water itself feels. Never put your hand into the plume, and lift
lids away from you, with an adult nearby. Changes of state are for
watching, not for touching, whenever a stove is involved.
\end{remark}

\section{The same water, round and round}

\begin{example}[The shrinking puddle]\label{ex:g2:ice-water-steam:puddle}
After the rain, a puddle shines on the playground. By afternoon it
has shrunk; by tomorrow it is gone. Nobody drank it and it did not
leak through the tarmac: the Sun's warmth made it \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:evapcond}{evaporate}, drop by
drop, into the air. Draw a chalk line around a puddle in the morning
and check after lunch: the water has retreated inside your line.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[From the sea to the rain]\label{ex:g2:ice-water-steam:cycle}
What \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:evapcond}{evaporates} must \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:evapcond}{condense} somewhere. Water flies up from seas
and puddles, invisible; high in the sky, where the air is cold, it
\omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:evapcond}{condenses} into the droplets that make clouds; the droplets gather,
grow heavy, and fall --- rain! The rain fills the puddles and the
seas, and the round trip starts again. The water in your glass has
made this journey more times than anyone can count.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:ice-water-steam:1}
Which state of matter is each of these --- solid, liquid, or gas:
an icicle; \ the sea; \ the invisible water just above the soup pot's
spout; \ frost on the grass; \ rain?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:ice-water-steam:2}
Name the change of state: a snowman shrinking in the sun; \ a puddle
disappearing; \ mist appearing on the bathroom mirror; \ a pond
turning hard in winter.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:ice-water-steam:3}
In the change-of-state picture, which way does warmth always push:
toward the solid or toward the gas? And cold?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:ice-water-steam:4}
The drops on the cold can of \cref{ex:g2:ice-water-steam:catch} ---
did they leak out through the glass? Where did they come from?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:ice-water-steam:5}
Why can you see your breath on a freezing morning but not on a warm
day? Use the words of \cref{def:g2:ice-water-steam:evapcond}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:ice-water-steam:6}
Wet laundry dries faster in the sun than in the shade. Which change
of state is at work, and what does the Sun add to speed it up?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:ice-water-steam:7}
Why is the plume above a boiling pot dangerous, and what is the safe
rule from \cref{rem:g2:ice-water-steam:safety}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g2:ice-water-steam:8}
Tell the round trip of a raindrop: start at the sea, end back in the
sea, and use the words \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:evapcond}{evaporate} and \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:evapcond}{condense} on the way.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g2:ice-water-steam:9}
On the pot's spout there is a clear gap, and only above it does the
white plume begin. What is in the clear gap? What does this show
about water in its gas state?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g2:ice-water-steam:10}
Nino leaves a glass of water on the windowsill for a week; the level
\omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{sinks} day after day, though nobody drinks it. His cat is accused.
Defend the cat: explain what really happened, and invent a way to
show the water leaves \emph{into the air}, not into the cat.
\end{exercise}
