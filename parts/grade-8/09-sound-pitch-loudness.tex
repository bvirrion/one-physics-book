\chapter{Sound: Speed, Pitch, Loudness}\label{ch:g8:sound-pitch-loudness}

Short strings sing high, hard plucks sing loud --- your
rubber-band guitar found the rules years ago. This year the
rules get numbers: a count-per-second for pitch, a swing-size
for loudness, and a window --- with edges --- on what human
ears can catch at all. Beyond one edge, bats are laughing at
us.

\section{Frequency: pitch counted}

\begin{definition}[Frequency]\label{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}
The \emph{frequency}\index{frequency} $f$ of a \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:vibration}{vibration} is
the number of complete back-and-forth swings it makes each
second. Its \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{unit} is the \emph{hertz}\index{hertz} (\unit{Hz}):
one swing per second --- honoring the physicist who first made
and caught invisible waves. A guitar string trembling $110$
times a second vibrates at \qty{110}{Hz}; a mosquito's wings,
near \qty{600}{Hz}; the slow flap of a big flag, a few hertz.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Pitch is frequency]\label{prop:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:pitch}
What the ear calls \emph{pitch} is \omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{frequency} and nothing
else: the faster the source trembles, the higher the note.
The rubber-band rules translate at once --- short, tight,
thin things tremble at higher $f$. Music's anchor: concert
instruments the world over tune to the note A at
\qty{440}{Hz}. Doubling any note's \omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{frequency} raises it by the
most consonant of all intervals --- the octave: A again, an
octave up, at \qty{880}{Hz}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Seeing sound]\label{met:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:seeing}
The rice-grain drum showed \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound}'s trembling; a microphone
draws it.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item run a sound-analyzer application on a phone or
        computer --- its screen plots the microphone's swing
        against time, a modern oscilloscope;
  \item hum low and steady: a lazy, wide-spaced trace; sing
        high: the peaks crowd together;
  \item read the \omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{frequency} readout while whistling: most
        whistles land between $500$ and \qty{2000}{Hz};
  \item strike the tuning fork marked ``A 440'' and check
        the readout honors its engraving.
\end{enumerate}
One screen replaces a chapter of guesswork: pitch counted
before your eyes.
\end{method}

\section{The ear's window}

\begin{proposition}[The range of human hearing]\label{prop:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:range}
Healthy young ears catch \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:vibration}{vibrations} between about
\qty{20}{Hz} and \qty{20000}{Hz}. Below the window lies
\emph{infrasound}\index{infrasound} --- felt sometimes as
rumble in the chest, heard never; above it,
\emph{ultrasound}\index{ultrasound} --- silence to us,
however violent the trembling. The window narrows with age
and with abuse: the highest octave is usually the first
casualty of loud habits.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Beyond the edges]\label{ex:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:beyond}
Nature and industry both work beyond our window. Bats hunt by
ultrasonic clicks near \qty{50000}{Hz}, reading \omterm{ex:g4:how-sound-travels:echo}{echoes} finer
than any audible \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} could paint --- dolphins likewise, in
the sonar chapter's spirit. Dogs answer ``silent'' whistles
pitched just over our edge. Medical scanners \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{image} unborn
children with harmless \omterm{prop:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:range}{ultrasound} \omterm{ex:g4:how-sound-travels:echo}{echoes}. At the other edge,
elephants converse in infrasonic rumbles across kilometres,
and storms and earthquakes announce themselves in tones no
ear attends.
\end{example}

\section{Loudness: the swing's size}

\begin{proposition}[Loudness is amplitude]\label{prop:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:loudness}
What the ear calls \emph{loudness} corresponds to the
\emph{amplitude}\index{amplitude} of the \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:vibration}{vibration} --- the
size of the swing, the strength of the traveling squeeze.
Pluck gently or savagely: the trace's peaks stand low or
tall while their \emph{spacing} --- the pitch --- stays
untouched. \omterm{prop:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:loudness}{Amplitude} and \omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{frequency} are independent dials:
any pitch may be whispered or bellowed.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The decibel ladder]\label{ex:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:decibels}
Loudness is graded in \emph{decibels}\index{decibel}
(\unit{dB}) --- a ladder built for the ear's astonishing
range, on which each tenfold strengthening of the \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} adds
the same number of rungs. Landmarks: rustling leaves,
\qty{10}{dB}; quiet conversation, \qty{50}{dB}; busy
traffic, \qty{80}{dB}; a rock concert's front rows,
\qty{110}{dB}; pain and instant harm, near \qty{120}{dB}.
The ladder's strange arithmetic --- equal steps for tenfold
strengths --- is a logarithm, one of the great inventions
awaiting you in high school mathematics.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[The ear's budget]\label{rem:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:earsafety}
The little drum in your ear, met in your third-year chapter,
keeps lifetime accounts. Above about \qty{85}{dB}, damage
accrues with exposure time --- a workday's limit at a loud
factory, minutes only at concert-front levels; headphones at
full volume sit squarely in the danger band. \omterm{def:g1:five-senses:senses}{Hearing} lost to
the tall swings never returns: the highest notes leave first,
quietly, permanently. Ears have no eyelids --- and no spare
parts.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Speed, unmoved by either dial]\label{ex:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:speed}
Neither dial touches the delivery. High and low, loud and
soft, all \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sounds} ride the \omterm{def:g7:sound-production:medium}{medium}'s one relay at the \omterm{def:g7:sound-production:medium}{medium}'s
one pace --- \qty{340}{m/s} in \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}. The proof performs
nightly: an orchestra heard across a park arrives \emph{in
tune and in time} --- were high notes swifter than low, every
distant chord would smear into arpeggios. (Loudness fades
with distance --- the spreading shells of the g7 chapter ---
but fading is weakening, never slowing.)
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:1}
Define \omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{frequency} and its \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{unit}. What is the pitch of concert
A, and of the A one octave above?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:2}
Two traces span the same $20$ milliseconds: one shows $4$
complete swings, the other $16$. Compute both frequencies;
which \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sounds} higher?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:3}
Give the human \omterm{def:g1:five-senses:senses}{hearing} window, and the names of the two
countries beyond its borders --- with one inhabitant of
each.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:4}
On the analyzer's screen, what changes when you sing the
same note louder? And when you sing a higher note equally
loud?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:5}
Translate the old rubber-band rules into frequency-language:
what do short, tight and thin each do to $f$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:6}
Place on the decibel ladder: whispering leaves; traffic; the
concert's front row; pain. Where does the danger band
begin?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:7}
Why do dog whistles seem broken to their owners? And why
does the dog disagree?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:8}
A distant orchestra arrives in tune and in time. What does
this prove about \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound}'s \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} across pitch and loudness?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:9}
A bat clicks at \qty{50000}{Hz}. How many complete swings
does one \qty{0.01}{s} click contain? Why must fine \omterm{ex:g4:how-sound-travels:echo}{echo}
imaging use such rapid trembling? (Recall what the finest
paintbrushes have in common.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:10}
The lowest key of a grand piano vibrates near \qty{27}{Hz};
the highest, near \qty{4200}{Hz}. Locate both within (or
against) the \omterm{def:g1:five-senses:senses}{hearing} window --- and explain why an aging
piano tuner may need the analyzer's help at one end of the
keyboard first.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:11}
Concert rules allow $8$ \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours} at \qty{85}{dB} but only
minutes near \qty{110}{dB}, halving the budget every few
rungs. What kind of growth hides in the ladder's equal
steps --- and what does the budget's collapse teach about
``just a bit louder''?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:12}
Design the full sound-check of a school hall with one
analyzer phone: how to verify the piano's A against $440$;
how to find the hall's loudness at the back row during a
drum test; how to catch whether the ventilation emits an
infrasonic rumble the ear misses; and which of the three
findings the phone's microphone is \emph{least} equipped to
certify (consult the phone's own \omterm{def:g1:five-senses:senses}{hearing} window --- built
for voices).
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Sound Check}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- sound check at the school
festival; one analyzer, three bands and a nervous nurse; the
mixing desk's evening}]\label{pb:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:1}
Festival eve: you run the analyzer at the mixing desk, with
the school nurse watching the decibel meter over your
shoulder.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Tuning hour.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The choir's tuning fork sings at \qty{440}{Hz}; the
        analyzer shows the piano's A at \qty{432}{Hz}.
        Diagnose the piano, and name the dial --- pitch or
        loudness --- that needs the tuner.
  \item The bass guitar's lowest string reads \qty{41}{Hz}.
        Locate it in the \omterm{def:g1:five-senses:senses}{hearing} window --- and explain why
        the audience will \emph{feel} it in the chest as
        much as hear it.
  \item The lead singer's held note shows peaks spaced
        exactly as the fork's, but three times taller.
        Compare the two \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sounds} in both dials.
  \item A young roadie claims the hall's new speakers
        ``reproduce up to \qty{22000}{Hz}''. Who in the
        audience will certify the top of that range ---
        and who cannot?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The nurse's ledger.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Rehearsal levels: acoustic set \qty{75}{dB}; rock
        set \qty{100}{dB}; finale planned ``a touch above''
        at \qty{110}{dB}. Which sets does the nurse flag,
        against which budget line?
  \item The nurse issues foam earplugs rated to soften
        \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sounds} by \qty{20}{dB}. What does the rock set
        become behind them --- and why do wise roadies wear
        plugs at every show?
  \item Standing twice as far from the stage, the meter
        drops noticeably. Which old law of spreading
        shells is at work --- and which dial (pitch or
        loudness) does distance turn?
  \item The drummer protests: ``Quieter means duller ---
        high notes die first when we play soft!'' Untangle
        the two dials for him: what does playing softly
        actually change?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Festival physics.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Fireworks close the show \qty{680}{m} away. Compute
        the flash-to-bang delay the crowd will notice ---
        and the \omterm{def:g8:sound-pitch-loudness:frequency}{frequency} of the rumble if the big shells
        thump twice per second (window check: heard or
        felt?).
  \item The night's last mystery: a phone video of the
        concert, played back, \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sounds} thin --- the chest
        rumble is gone. Consult exercise 12's caveat: what
        was the microphone never built to catch?
  \item The nurse's report requests ``one sentence each''
        on the evening's two dials and the third thing
        neither dial can touch. Draft them.
  \item Pack-down: the caretaker's ultrasonic pest
        repeller, running all evening by the stage door,
        bothered no listener and, allegedly, many mice.
        Explain both audiences with one window
        proposition.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
