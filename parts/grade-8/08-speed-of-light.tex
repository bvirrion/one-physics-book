\chapter{The Speed of Light; Distances in the Universe}\label{ch:g8:speed-of-light}

For seven years this book has whispered that \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} is ``as good
as instant'' --- the flash before the thunder, the seen strike
starting the stopwatch. This chapter keeps the family's oldest
promise: \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}'s \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} is not infinite, humans measured it
against all odds, and the number is so vast that it becomes,
in the same breath, astronomy's ruler and its time machine.

\section{The impossible measurement}

\begin{example}[Lanterns on two hills]\label{ex:g8:speed-of-light:galileo}
Four centuries ago, the great pioneer of experiments set two
observers on distant hills at night, each with a shuttered
lantern: uncover yours on seeing mine, and the round-trip
delay should betray \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}'s \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed}. The delay they found was
only human reflexes; between the hills, \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}'s true crossing
took a few \emph{millionths} of a second. The experiment
failed magnificently --- proving, at least, that \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} is
faster than anything a hilltop could clock, and daring
astronomy to find longer race-tracks.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The moon that ran late]\label{ex:g8:speed-of-light:romer}
The race-track came courtesy of Jupiter. Its innermost great
moon eclipses into Jupiter's \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} on a superbly regular
schedule --- a clock in the sky. Yet a patient astronomer
found the clock running \emph{late} by many minutes in the
months when the Earth's \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbit} had carried us to the far side
of the Sun from Jupiter, and early again as we swung near.
His leap: the moon is punctual --- its \emph{\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}} is
delayed, needing extra time to cross the extra width of the
Earth's \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbit}. From the lateness and the \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbit}'s size came
the first estimate of \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}'s \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} --- breathtakingly close,
for the sixteen-hundreds, to the truth.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[The speed of light]\label{prop:g8:speed-of-light:value}
In vacuum --- and in \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}, to excellent approximation --- \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}
travels at
\[
c = \qty{300000}{km/s} = 3 \times 10^{8}\ \unit{m/s},
\]
three hundred thousand kilometres each second: seven and a
half laps of the Earth between two heartbeats. Every color of
the blend travels at this same $c$ in vacuum; nothing that
carries matter or news has ever been clocked faster. Later,
Earth-bound ingenuity confirmed the astronomers --- a \omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beam}
chopped by a spinning toothed wheel, racing eight kilometres
to a \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{mirror} and back through the next tooth-gap --- and
modern instruments have polished $c$ to a certainty so
complete that the \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metre} itself is now defined from it.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Travel times in the neighborhood]\label{ex:g8:speed-of-light:times}
With $t = d/c$, the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:family}{Solar System} acquires a timetable. The
\omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}, \qty{384000}{km}: $384000 \div 300000 \approx
\qty{1.3}{s}$ --- Earth--Moon radio conversations carry that
awkward pause. The Sun, $150$ million km: $150000000 \div
300000 = \qty{500}{s}$ --- eight minutes twenty: sunshine is
always eight-minute-old news. Mars, at a typical $2 \times
10^{8}$ km: about eleven minutes --- rover drivers send the
day's plan and wait; no joystick can steer across a
twenty-minute round trip.
\end{example}

\section{The light-year}

\begin{definition}[Light-year]\label{def:g8:speed-of-light:lightyear}
A \emph{light-year}\index{light-year} is a \emph{distance}:
the stretch \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} covers in one year --- about $9.5 \times
10^{12}$ km, nearly ten million million kilometres. The name
misleads the hasty (it is no more a time than a
``footstep'' is a foot); astronomers use it because
kilometres collapse under the sky's true scale.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The neighborhood in light-years]\label{ex:g8:speed-of-light:ladder}
The nearest \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} beyond the Sun: about $4$ \omterm{def:g8:speed-of-light:lightyear}{light-years} ---
tonight's photons left it while you were in your first
physics chapters. The bright \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} of the winter sky: tens
to hundreds of \omterm{def:g8:speed-of-light:lightyear}{light-years}. The \omterm{ex:g5:stars-night-sky:milkyway}{Milky Way}, our city of
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}: about $100000$ \omterm{def:g8:speed-of-light:lightyear}{light-years} across. The nearest great
neighboring galaxy: some $2.5$ million \omterm{def:g8:speed-of-light:lightyear}{light-years} --- its
faint smudge, visible to a dark-adapted naked eye, is the
oldest \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} a human can see unaided.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Telescopes are time machines]\label{prop:g8:speed-of-light:timemachine}
Because \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}'s news travels at $c$ and the universe is vast,
\emph{looking far means looking back}. The \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} at $4$
\omterm{def:g8:speed-of-light:lightyear}{light-years} shows itself as it was $4$ years ago; the
neighbor galaxy, as it was when our ancestors first chipped
stone tools. The g5 stargazer's suspicion is now a theorem
of arithmetic: every telescope aimed outward is aimed
pastward, and astronomy is history read by starlight.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Converting distance and lookback]\label{met:g8:speed-of-light:convert}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item distance in \omterm{def:g8:speed-of-light:lightyear}{light-years} $\to$ lookback in years: the
        same number --- that is the \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{unit}'s whole genius;
  \item distance in kilometres $\to$ light-time: divide by
        \qty{300000}{km/s}, then tame the seconds into
        minutes, \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours} or years;
  \item \omterm{def:g8:speed-of-light:lightyear}{light-years} $\to$ kilometres (rarely worth it):
        multiply by $9.5 \times 10^{12}$ --- and remember
        why astronomers seldom do.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{remark}[What ``now'' means out there]\label{rem:g8:speed-of-light:now}
Is the \omterm{def:g8:speed-of-light:lightyear}{four-light-year} \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} still shining \emph{right now}?
Almost surely --- \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} live long --- but no faster answer
than \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}'s own can ever reach us: the freshest possible
news is four years stale, always. For the deep sky, the
question softens into strangeness: some of the faintest
smudges in great telescopes are galaxies whose \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} left
before the Earth existed. Physics will sharpen what ``now at
a distance'' can even mean in the university years; for this
year, carry the honest version: the sky is not a scene ---
it is an archive.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: Mission Control}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a shift at deep-space
mission control; light-lag conversations from the Moon to
the stars; the operations manual}]\label{pb:g8:speed-of-light:1}
Tonight you direct the communications desk: every message
travels at $c = \qty{300000}{km/s}$, and the light-lag rules
every decision.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Near operations.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The lunar base (\qty{384000}{km}) requests
        confirmation of a supply drop. How long after your
        ``confirmed'' does the base hear it, and what is the
        soonest you can hear their acknowledgment?
  \item A satellite in high \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbit} at \qty{36000}{km} relays
        a television feed. Compute its one-way lag ---
        and say why intercontinental phone calls via such
        satellites carry a just-noticeable hesitation
        (round trip up and down).
  \item The solar observatory reports a great flare ``as it
        happens''. Correct the operator's phrase: when did
        the flare truly erupt?
  \item Protocol asks all clocks to allow for lag. Which
        near-Earth link --- \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} or high satellite --- needs
        the larger allowance, and by roughly what factor?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The Mars window.}
Mars stands tonight at $1.8 \times 10^{8}$ km.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the one-way lag in seconds, then in minutes.
  \item The rover meets an unexpected crevasse and its
        cameras show it approaching at walking pace. Explain,
        with the round-trip figure, why a joystick rescue is
        hopeless --- and what the rover must therefore carry
        on board.
  \item The evening's plan upload takes \qty{3}{min} of
        transmission plus the lag. When must the upload
        start to be fully received by the rover at 21:00
        control time?
  \item Six months hence, Mars will stand at $3.8 \times
        10^{8}$ km. What becomes of the lag, and what does
        this teach about ``the'' distance to a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The far desk.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item An interstellar probe has reached one full
        light-\emph{day} from Earth. Express that distance
        in kilometres (powers of ten; a day holds $86400$
        seconds), and state the round-trip conversation
        time with the probe.
  \item Dreamers at the back desk draft a ``message to the
        nearest \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star}''. Draft the schedule instead: sent
        tonight, when is the soonest any reply arrives?
  \item The archive wall shows tonight's deep \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{image} of the
        neighbor galaxy, $2.5$ million \omterm{def:g8:speed-of-light:lightyear}{light-years} out. Add
        the caption's honest tense --- what era of Earth's
        past does this snapshot broadcast back, were anyone
        there to look?
  \item End-of-shift log: write the desk's three rules ---
        one about instantaneous (that nothing is), one about
        joysticks and lag, one about telescopes and the
        archive.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
