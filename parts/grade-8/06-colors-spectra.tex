\chapter{Colors and Spectra of Light}\label{ch:g8:colors-spectra}

Where do colors live? In the apple, says common sense --- the
red is right there on the peel. Yet carry the apple into a
gymnasium lit deep blue, and the ``red'' apple turns black as
coal. Colors, it turns out, are a three-way conversation between
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}, object and eye --- and it opens with the strangest fact
in optics: white \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} is not white.

\section{White light unpacked}

\begin{proposition}[White light is a mixture]\label{prop:g8:colors-spectra:mixture}
Ordinary ``white'' \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} --- sunlight, lamplight --- is a
blend of all the colors at once. Sent through a glass prism,
the blend unpacks: each color bends by a slightly different
amount crossing the glass, so the colors fan apart into a
continuous band from red (bent least) to violet (bent most).
The band is called a \emph{spectrum}\index{spectrum}, and the
unpacking, \emph{dispersion}\index{dispersion}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Spectra without a laboratory]\label{ex:g8:colors-spectra:everyday}
Nature and the junk drawer are full of prisms. The rainbow:
every raindrop is a tiny prism-and-mirror, and the sky-wide arc
is sunlight dispersed by millions of drops --- always in the
half of the sky \emph{opposite} the Sun, which is why rainbow
hunters stand with the Sun at their backs. The garden hose's
fine mist conjures one to order. A CD's grooved face throws
little spectra across the ceiling; the beveled edge of a
\omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{mirror} stripes the wall on sunny afternoons. White \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}
betrays its mixture everywhere it meets fine structure or
slanted glass.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Packing the mixture back up]\label{ex:g8:colors-spectra:newtondisc}
The unpacking runs in reverse. Paint a disc in rainbow sectors
and spin it fast: the eye, too slow to follow, receives all
the colors practically at once from each spot --- and reads
the blend: a pale, washed white-gray. The great physicist who
first unpacked sunlight with a prism spun exactly such a disc
to argue the packing-up --- and his boldest stroke was a
second prism, catching the \omterm{prop:g8:colors-spectra:mixture}{spectrum} and folding it back into
a single white \omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beam}: mixture proven both ways.
\end{example}

\section{Why the apple is red}

\begin{proposition}[The color of an object]\label{prop:g8:colors-spectra:objectcolor}
An \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:coats}{opaque} object's color is made by subtraction. Receiving
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}, the object \emph{absorbs} part of the blend and
diffuses the rest --- and the diffused remainder is what
reaches your eye and names the color. A red apple absorbs
nearly all of white \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}'s colors and returns chiefly the
red. A white page returns nearly everything; a black coat
returns nearly nothing --- absorbing the lot (and warming
accordingly, as dark cars in summer testify). The color is
not \emph{in} the peel: it is the peel's answer to the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}
it was offered.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The gymnasium test]\label{ex:g8:colors-spectra:gym}
Now the blue-lit gymnasium. The red apple's peel does what it
always does: absorbs everything but red, and stands ready to
return red --- but the blue floodlight \emph{contains} no red
to return. Nothing diffused, nothing received: the apple shows
black. The white page under the same \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} returns what it is
given: it looks blue. Rule of the theater: an object can only
return colors the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} brought --- change the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}, and you
change every costume in the hall.
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Predicting a color under any light]\label{met:g8:colors-spectra:predict}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item list what the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} brings (white: everything; a
        colored lamp: chiefly its own color);
  \item list what the object returns in white \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} --- its
        daylight color names it;
  \item intersect the lists: the object diffuses what appears
        on both;
  \item empty intersection: the object shows black.
\end{enumerate}
A blue cap under red \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}: returns blue only, offered red
only --- intersection empty: black cap. Under white \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}: the
full offer meets the blue answer --- blue cap.
\end{method}

\section{Building colors from light}

\begin{example}[Three lamps make them all]\label{ex:g8:colors-spectra:additive}
Mixing \emph{\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights}} adds. Overlap three floodlights --- red,
green, blue: red and green together make yellow; green and
blue, a turquoise cyan; blue and red, magenta; all three,
white. From just three well-chosen primaries, light-mixing
builds virtually every color the eye can name --- which is why
three is the magic number of every glowing screen.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The screen in your pocket]\label{ex:g8:colors-spectra:screen}
Examine a white area of a phone or television screen through a
strong magnifier: no white anywhere --- only minute red, green
and blue strips, all ablaze. Every color the screen ever
shows is these three lamps in dosed proportions; every
``white'' is the trio at full song, blended by your eye's
distance. The paint box works the other way --- pigments
absorb, and mixing them subtracts \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} rather than adding it
--- which is why mixing every paint gives murky darkness while
mixing every \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} gives white; the painter's full story
belongs to art class and to chemistry.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[The sunset, paid in full]\label{rem:g8:colors-spectra:sunset}
Last year's \omterm{def:g1:day-and-night:daynight}{sunset} promise falls due. \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{Air} diffuses sunlight
--- but not impartially: the blue end of the blend is
scattered far more readily than the red. Look away from the
Sun by day and you see the stolen share, forwarded from the
whole sky: blue. At \omterm{def:g1:day-and-night:daynight}{sunset}, the direct \omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beam}'s grazing road
through the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} is longest, the theft of blue nearly
complete --- and the survivor that reaches your eye is the
red-orange remainder, with pink clouds catching the late
scatterings. Blue sky and red \omterm{def:g1:day-and-night:daynight}{sunset} are one theft, seen from
the two sides of the ledger.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:1}
What is a \omterm{prop:g8:colors-spectra:mixture}{spectrum}, and what is \omterm{prop:g8:colors-spectra:mixture}{dispersion}? Which color bends
least in the prism, and which most?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:2}
Why must a rainbow hunter stand with the Sun at their back?
What plays the prism?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:3}
Give the two demonstrations that white \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} is a mixture ---
one unpacking, one packing back up.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:4}
In subtraction-language: why is grass green? Why is the white
page white and the blackboard black?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:5}
Predict with the method: a yellow scarf under white \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light};
under blue \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:6}
Why does the red apple turn black in the blue gymnasium ---
yet the white handkerchief beside it turn blue?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:7}
Name the three primaries of light-mixing and the results of
each pairwise overlap --- and of all three together.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:8}
What does the magnifier reveal in a screen's ``white''? Where
does the blending happen?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:9}
Why does mixing all the \emph{\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights}} give white while mixing
all the \emph{paints} gives murky darkness? One sentence per
direction of the ledger.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:10}
Complete the \omterm{def:g1:day-and-night:daynight}{sunset} ledger: what the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} steals preferentially,
who receives the stolen goods and as what, and what survives
the longest grazing road --- and why the \emph{eclipsed \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}}
of last year glows with exactly that survivor.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:11}
A shop's changing room is lit with warm reddish lamps
``flattering to skin tones''. A customer buys a jacket that
looked deep warm brown --- and finds it dull green in
daylight. Reconstruct the deception with the prediction
method.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g8:colors-spectra:12}
Stage-craft: a designer needs a banner with bright, readable
lettering on a black ground under white houselights --- but the
whole banner must vanish, uniformly black, letters and ground
alike, under the finale's deep red wash. Choose the letter
color and the ground color (from: white, red, green, blue,
black), justify each choice with the subtraction rules, and
explain why \emph{white} lettering --- the tempting choice ---
would betray the finale.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Theater of Light}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- dress rehearsal under
colored floods; costumes, filters and one sabotaged scene;
the lighting designer's exam}]\label{pb:g8:colors-spectra:1}
Dress rehearsal: three floodlights (red, green, blue) with
dimmers, a rack of costumes, and a script with tricks in it.
You hold the lighting plot.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Warm-up drills.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item All three floods at full: what \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} bathes the stage,
        and why?
  \item Red and green at full, blue dark: name the stage's
        \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}.
  \item Under that red-green mix, predict the look of: a white
        shirt; a blue sash; a yellow gown.
  \item The white cyclorama backdrop under blue alone ---
        and the reason a backdrop is always white cloth.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Costume clinic.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The villain's coat must look black in scene one (red
        wash) yet richly green in scene two (white \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}).
        What daylight color of coat serves, and why?
  \item The ghost's robe must stay white-looking in
        \emph{every} lighting. What color robe, and what will
        it actually show under each single flood?
  \item An actor's red scarf must ``bleed away'' ---
        indistinguishable from the black curtain --- at one
        moment. Which floods may be lit for the trick, and why
        does it fail the instant the red flood joins them?
  \item The programme's gold lettering looked splendid at
        noon but muddy under the blue-heavy evening preset.
        Explain, treating gold as a warm yellow.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The sabotage.}
Opening scene: the set's rainbow arch (painted stripes: red,
orange, yellow, green, blue, violet) under full white \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A prankster swaps the white preset for pure red.
        Stripe by stripe, what does the audience now see?
  \item The director, improvising, calls for red \emph{and}
        blue floods together. Which stripes revive, and what
        does the magenta-ish wash do to the green stripe?
  \item The stage manager asks why the arch cannot simply be
        ``lit back to normal'' with a very bright single
        green flood. Answer with the only \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} that shows
        all six stripes.
  \item Write the designer's exam answer: the two laws of the
        theater of \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} --- one about what \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} do when
        mixed, one about what objects do with what they are
        given --- each in one sentence.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
