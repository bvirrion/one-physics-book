\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g4:changes-of-state} --- Melting and Boiling: Changes of State}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:changes-of-state:1}
Melting; freezing (solidifying); boiling; condensation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:changes-of-state:2}
\omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{Melting point} \qty{0}{\celsius}, \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:boiling-point}{boiling point} \qty{100}{\celsius}.
Chocolate \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts} near \qty{34}{\celsius} --- mouth \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:changes-of-state:3}
\qty{0}{\celsius} --- and still \qty{0}{\celsius} five minutes
later: while ice and water live together, the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} is pinned
to the \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{melting point}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:changes-of-state:4}
While a stuff \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts} or boils, its \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} stands still at the
milestone. The warmth is all spent on the change of state itself ---
turning \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} into steam --- not on climbing.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:changes-of-state:5}
\omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{Solid} at \qty{20}{\celsius} (below $60$); \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} at
\qty{80}{\celsius} (above $60$); still \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid} at \qty{35}{\celsius}
--- though a pocket chocolate bar would already have melted there.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:changes-of-state:6}
No hotter: both pots stand at \qty{100}{\celsius}, the boiling
plateau. The extra warmth only makes steam faster --- wasted flame,
same pasta.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:changes-of-state:7}
By the two flat stretches --- the plateaus. Wherever the curve runs
level while the stove keeps heating, a change of state is under
way.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:changes-of-state:8}
Gold ($1064 < 1200$) pours; iron ($1538 > 1200$) stays \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid}. The
furnace misses iron's milestone by $1538 - 1200 = 338$ degrees.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:changes-of-state:9}
Melting ice pins the drink to \qty{0}{\celsius}: all incoming warmth
is spent melting ice, none on warming the drink. Only when the last
sliver is gone can the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} start to climb.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:changes-of-state:10}
Her best guess: the stuff is ice --- frozen water. The law that
milestones are identity marks: a pure stuff's melting and \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:boiling-point}{boiling
points} never change, so matching water's two numbers exactly is as
good as a signature.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:changes-of-state:11}
The certainty that water always boils at \qty{100}{\celsius}. High
on a peak it boils well below that --- hot but not
\qty{100}{\celsius} --- so the ``boiling'' water is too cool to brew
tea properly. The culprit is the thinner \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} pressing less on the
water, as the air-pressure chapter will show.
\end{solution}
