\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g4:solar-system} --- The Solar System}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:solar-system:1}
A \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} makes its own \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}; a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet} makes none and travels around
a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star}, shining only by returned sunlight. The Sun is a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star}; the
Earth is a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:solar-system:2}
Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune ---
ours is the third, Earth.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:solar-system:3}
Rocky: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars. Giants: Jupiter, Saturn,
Uranus, Neptune. Biggest: Jupiter. The rings: Saturn.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:solar-system:4}
It circles the Earth, not the Sun directly --- so it is a moon, a
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}'s companion, not a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:solar-system:5}
The farther the road from the Sun, the longer the lap: close
Mercury races around quickly, far Neptune takes lifetimes.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:solar-system:6}
A football is the Sun; the Earth (a peppercorn in the model)
stands about $30$ big steps away. By room, the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:family}{Solar System} is almost entirely empty
space.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:solar-system:7}
From the Sun: Venus, like the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}, only sends sunlight back --- its
white clouds return it so generously that it outshines every true
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} in our evening sky.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:solar-system:8}
Watch it against the fixed sparkles for a few weeks: a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}
slowly drifts --- wanders --- among them, while true \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} keep
their places in the patterns.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:solar-system:9}
$150 \div 30 = 5$: Jupiter is about five times farther from the Sun
than the Earth.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:solar-system:10}
Almost all drawings lie about the distances: drawn to scale, a page
showing Neptune's orbit would shrink the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} to invisible
specks --- so artists squeeze the orbits together to show the
worlds at all. In truth --- as the walked model shows --- the
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} travel in vast emptiness: even \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} and fast probes must
cross hundreds of ``steps'' of nothing, which takes years.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g4:solar-system:11}
The wanderers sometimes drift backward against the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} for weeks
--- clumsy to explain with everything circling an enthroned Earth.
With the Sun at the center, the backward drift is a simple
overtaking: the Earth, on a faster inner road, passes a slower
outer \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}, which then \emph{seems} to slide backward. The cost:
giving up the center --- accepting that our home is \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet} number
three.
\end{solution}
