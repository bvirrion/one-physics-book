\chapter{Melting and Boiling: Changes of State}\label{ch:g4:changes-of-state}

You have known for years that warmth \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts} ice and boils water. But
now you own a \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} --- and the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} has something
astonishing to report from inside the melting ice bucket: while the
ice \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts}, the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} \emph{refuses to move}. Let us watch the
old changes of state with new eyes.

\section{The proper names}

Water's changes of state now get their grown-up names, good for
every stuff, not just water. From \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid} to \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}:
\emph{melting}\index{melting}. From \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} back to \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid}:
\emph{freezing}\index{freezing} (also called solidifying). From
\omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} to \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas}: \emph{boiling}\index{boiling} when it happens fast
with big bubbles, evaporation when it happens quietly from the
surface. From \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} back to \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}: condensation. Every stuff in the
world --- water, chocolate, iron, gold --- can play this game, each
at its own \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperatures}.

\begin{definition}[Melting point]\label{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}
The \emph{melting point}\index{melting point} of a stuff is the
\omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} at which it \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts} --- and also the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} at which
the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{freezes} back. For water it is \qty{0}{\celsius}. Each
stuff has its own: chocolate \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts} near \qty{34}{\celsius} (mouth
\omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} --- no accident, ask any chocolate maker), candle wax
near \qty{60}{\celsius}, iron only at \qty{1538}{\celsius}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Boiling point]\label{def:g4:changes-of-state:boiling-point}
The \emph{boiling point}\index{boiling point} of a stuff is the
\omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} at which it boils. For water it is \qty{100}{\celsius}.
Each stuff again has its own --- which is why a careful cook can
boil water away while the oil in the same pan stays quietly \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Reading the world with two numbers]\label{ex:g4:changes-of-state:reading}
Why is a chocolate bar \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid} on the shelf (\qty{20}{\celsius}) but
\omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} in your pocket on a summer hike (\qty{35}{\celsius})? Because
its \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{melting point}, near \qty{34}{\celsius}, sits \emph{between} the
two. A stuff is \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid} below its \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{melting point}, \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} above it ---
up until its \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:boiling-point}{boiling point}, where it leaves as \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas}. Two numbers tell
the whole story of any stuff at any \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature}.
\end{example}

\section{The stubborn thermometer}

\begin{method}[Watching ice melt properly]\label{met:g4:changes-of-state:watch}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Fill a bowl with crushed ice or snow and plant the
        \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} in it; wait and read: \qty{0}{\celsius};
  \item bring the bowl into the warm kitchen and read the
        \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} every few minutes while the ice \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts};
  \item keep reading until long after the last sliver of ice has
        gone.
\end{enumerate}
The report: all through the melting --- water and ice together in
the bowl --- the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} stands stubbornly at \qty{0}{\celsius}.
Only when the \emph{last} piece of ice has melted does the
\omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} begin to climb.
\end{method}

\begin{proposition}[The plateau law]\label{prop:g4:changes-of-state:plateau}
While a stuff is changing state --- melting or boiling --- its
\omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} stands still at the milestone, however hard the stove
works. All the incoming warmth is spent on the change of state
itself, none on climbing. \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{Solid} and \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} live together at the
\omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{melting point}; \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} and \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} at the \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:boiling-point}{boiling point}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The plateau in the kitchen]\label{ex:g4:changes-of-state:kitchen}
This is why gently boiling and furiously boiling water cook pasta
equally fast: both pots stand at exactly \qty{100}{\celsius} --- the
furious one only wastes its extra warmth making steam faster. And
it is why a drink with ice cubes stays at \qty{0}{\celsius} down to
the last sliver: the melting ice pins the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} to the
milestone.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[A number the eye cannot see]\label{rem:g4:changes-of-state:invisible}
Without a \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer}, nobody would guess the plateau: the pot looks
busier and busier, the ice bucket looks calmer and calmer, and the
\omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} in both stands rock still. It is one more victory of
measuring over seeming --- and one of the first great surprises that
instruments ever handed to science.
\end{remark}

\section{Each stuff keeps its milestones}

\begin{proposition}[Milestones are identity marks]\label{prop:g4:changes-of-state:identity}
The \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{melting point} and \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:boiling-point}{boiling point} of a pure stuff never change:
water \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts} at \qty{0}{\celsius} today, tomorrow, in a palace or a
tent. The milestones are part of the stuff's identity --- so well
kept that a scientist can recognize an unknown stuff by measuring
where it \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts} and boils.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The candle and the foundry]\label{ex:g4:changes-of-state:foundry}
A candle by the fireplace slumps and drips: wax passes its \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{melting
point} near \qty{60}{\celsius}. In a foundry, workers pour
glowing-orange \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} iron like soup --- their furnace passed
\qty{1538}{\celsius}. Same game, wildly different milestones: what
the fireplace does to wax, only a furnace can do to iron. And gold
\omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts} at \qty{1064}{\celsius}: goldsmiths have known that number,
in their hands if not in degrees, for thousands of years.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:changes-of-state:1}
Give the proper name: \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid} to \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}; \ \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} to \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid}; \ \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}
to \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} with big bubbles; \ \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} to \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} on a cold window.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:changes-of-state:2}
What are water's two milestones, with their \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperatures}? And
chocolate's \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{melting point}, roughly?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:changes-of-state:3}
A bowl holds ice and water together, well stirred. What does the
\omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} read? What will it read five minutes later, if some ice
still remains?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:changes-of-state:4}
State the plateau law. Where does the stove's warmth go while the
water boils, since the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} no longer climbs?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:changes-of-state:5}
Wax \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts} near \qty{60}{\celsius}. Is a candle \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid} or \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}: in
a \qty{20}{\celsius} living room? \ next to a flame that warms it
to \qty{80}{\celsius}? \ in a \qty{35}{\celsius} summer attic?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:changes-of-state:6}
Pasta cooks in gently boiling water. Papa turns the flame to
maximum and the pot boils furiously. Is the water now hotter? What
does the extra warmth produce instead?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:changes-of-state:7}
On the plateau graph of the chapter, how can you spot the two
changes of state without reading a single word?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:changes-of-state:8}
Iron \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts} at \qty{1538}{\celsius} and gold at \qty{1064}{\celsius}.
A furnace runs at \qty{1200}{\celsius}: which of the two metals
pours like soup in it, and which stays \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid}? By how many degrees
does the furnace miss the other one?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:changes-of-state:9}
Why does a drink with ice cubes stay just as cold down to the last
sliver of ice --- and only then start warming up?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:changes-of-state:10}
A scientist receives an unknown white \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid}. She finds it \omterm{def:g2:ice-water-steam:meltfreeze}{melts} at
exactly \qty{0}{\celsius} and its \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} boils at exactly
\qty{100}{\celsius}. What is her best guess about the stuff --- and
which law lets milestones serve as identity cards?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:changes-of-state:11}
Mountain climbers report that high on great peaks, water boils
while only \emph{just} too hot to touch --- and their tea never
brews properly. What everyday certainty about boiling water breaks
down up there? (The reason hides in the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} itself, and a later
chapter on \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} pressure will catch it.)
\end{exercise}
