\chapter{Series Circuits}\label{ch:g4:series-circuits}

Last year, a string of party \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} went dark because one single
\omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} was missing --- and we promised that its arrangement had a
name. Here it is: the \omterm{def:g4:series-circuits:series}{series circuit}, the simplest way to put many
players on one loop. It has strict house rules, and a flashlight in
your drawer has been obeying them all along.

\section{One loop, several players}

\begin{definition}[In series]\label{def:g4:series-circuits:series}
Players in a circuit are \emph{in series}\index{series circuit} when
they sit on a single loop, one after another, like beads on one
necklace: the current's road passes through the first, then the
second, then the third, with no branching anywhere. A circuit built
this way is a \emph{series circuit}.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Building it]\label{met:g4:series-circuits:build}
With one \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}, two \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulbs}, a \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch} and wires:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item start at the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}'s $+$ \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{terminal} with a wire to the
        \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch};
  \item wire from the \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch} to the first \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb}, from the first \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb}
        to the second;
  \item close the loop: from the second \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} back to the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}'s
        $-$ \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{terminal};
  \item click the \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch} and watch both \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulbs} together.
\end{enumerate}
Trace the loop with your finger before switching on: if your finger
must ever choose between two roads, it is not a \omterm{def:g4:series-circuits:series}{series circuit}.
\end{method}

\section{The house rules}

\begin{proposition}[Rules of the single loop]\label{prop:g4:series-circuits:rules}
In a \omterm{def:g4:series-circuits:series}{series circuit}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{all or nothing}: one gap anywhere --- an open \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch},
        a broken \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb}, a loose wire --- and every player stops at
        once;
  \item \emph{order does not matter}: swap the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulbs}, move the
        \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch} to the other side of the loop --- everything behaves
        exactly as before;
  \item \emph{sharing}: the more \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulbs} on the loop, the dimmer each
        one glows; the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}'s push is shared along the whole
        necklace.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Seeing the rules]\label{ex:g4:series-circuits:seeing}
Build the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{two-bulb} circuit and test each rule. Unscrew \emph{either}
\omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb}: both go dark --- rule one (the unscrewed socket is a gap).
Swap the two \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulbs}, or move the \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch} between them: no change at
all --- rule two. Now rebuild with a single \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb}: it shines
noticeably brighter alone than it did with its partner --- rule
three, in reverse.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The party lights, solved]\label{ex:g4:series-circuits:partylights}
The old mystery is now no mystery: the party \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulbs} are \omterm{def:g4:series-circuits:series}{in series},
dozens of beads on one necklace. One missing \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} is a gap, and rule
one darkens the whole string. Finding the guilty \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} by trying
them one by one taught generations of families rule one the hard
way --- modern strings are built differently, as next year will
show.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[The switch commands everyone]\label{rem:g4:series-circuits:switch}
Rule two has a handy consequence: a single \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch}, placed anywhere
on the loop, commands every player on it. That is why one wall
\omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch} can control a whole row of ceiling \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} --- and why, for
the same reason, you cannot switch off just \emph{one} \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} of a
series string without killing them all.
\end{remark}

\section{More batteries on the necklace}

\begin{example}[Nose to tail]\label{ex:g4:series-circuits:batteries}
Batteries can also queue up \omterm{def:g4:series-circuits:series}{in series} --- but they are fussier than
\omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulbs}: they must stand \emph{nose to tail}, each \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}'s $+$
touching the next one's $-$. Two batteries queued this way push
together, and the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} shines clearly brighter than with one. Turn
one \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery} around --- nose to nose --- and the two pushes fight
each other: the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} gives nothing.
\end{example}

{\small Two batteries queued nose to tail push together: the same
\omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} glows brighter than with one \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery} alone.}
\end{omfigure}

\begin{example}[Inside the flashlight]\label{ex:g4:series-circuits:flashlight}
Open a flashlight: the batteries slide in one behind the other in a
tube --- a ready-made nose-to-tail queue --- then the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} and the
\omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{button-switch} complete one single loop through the case. A
flashlight \emph{is} a \omterm{def:g4:series-circuits:series}{series circuit} in a pocket. And now you know
why it goes dark if a single \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery} is inserted backward: nose to
nose, the pushes cancel.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:series-circuits:1}
What does ``\omterm{def:g4:series-circuits:series}{in series}'' mean? What picture from everyday life does
the chapter use for it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:series-circuits:2}
State the three house rules of the \omterm{def:g4:series-circuits:series}{series circuit}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:series-circuits:3}
In the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{two-bulb} circuit, \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} 2 is unscrewed. What happens to
\omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} 1, and why? Which rule is at work?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:series-circuits:4}
Zoe moves the \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch} from before \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} 1 to after \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} 2. What
changes in how the circuit behaves? Which rule says so?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:series-circuits:5}
One \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery} \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} one \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} nicely. Predict the brightness with:
two \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulbs} \omterm{def:g4:series-circuits:series}{in series}; \ three \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulbs} \omterm{def:g4:series-circuits:series}{in series}. What is being shared?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:series-circuits:6}
How must two batteries stand to push together? Draw the queue with
$+$ and $-$ marked on each \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:series-circuits:7}
Trace the loop of a flashlight: name the players the current visits
from the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}'s $+$ back to its $-$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:series-circuits:8}
A doorbell circuit has a \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}, a button and the bell \omterm{def:g4:series-circuits:series}{in series}.
Where could you add a second button so that the bell rings only
when \emph{both} buttons are pressed?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:series-circuits:9}
A string of ten series party \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} is dark. The \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulbs} all look
fine. Describe a patient plan, using a known-good \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb}, to find the
broken one.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:series-circuits:10}
Tom's flashlight takes two batteries and shines brightly. After
changing batteries in the dark, it gives nothing --- yet both new
batteries are full, the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} is fine, and no wire is loose. What
has Tom done, and why does the flashlight stay dark? (See
\cref{ex:g4:series-circuits:batteries}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:series-circuits:11}
An engineer must place one emergency \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{stop-switch} for a machine
whose motor sits on a single loop with its \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}. She says:
``Anywhere on the loop will do.'' Is she right? And why would her
plan fail if the machine's circuit had branching roads? (Next
year's chapter has the name for those.)
\end{exercise}
