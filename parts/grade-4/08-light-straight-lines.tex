\chapter{Light Travels in Straight Lines}\label{ch:g4:light-straight-lines}

Sunbeams slant through the morning mist between the trees ---
straight as stretched strings, every one. A torch in a dusty attic
throws a straight-edged blade of \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}. You can hear a friend
calling from around the corner, but you cannot \emph{see} them
there. All these are one law wearing different clothes --- the
oldest law of \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}.

\section{The law of the straight line}

\begin{proposition}[Light travels in straight lines]\label{prop:g4:light-straight-lines:law}
In \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} (as in water, glass, or empty space), \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} travels in
straight lines. It does not curve around corners, does not drift
like smoke, does not bend to find you behind a wall. Where a
straight line from the source cannot reach, no \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} from that
source arrives.
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}[Light ray]\label{def:g4:light-straight-lines:ray}
To draw \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}, physicists use the \emph{light ray}\index{light
ray}: a straight line with an arrow, showing one thread of \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}
and the way it runs. A torch beam is drawn as a fan of rays; a thin
laser beam as a single ray. Like the \omterm{def:g2:pushes-and-pulls:force}{force} arrow, the ray is a
little drawing that carries a law inside it: \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}'s roads are
ruler-straight.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Where the law shows itself]\label{ex:g4:light-straight-lines:sightings}
Once you know the law, you see it everywhere: the straight sunbeams
through mist or blinds; the sharp straight edge of a torch beam in
dusty \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}; the laser pointer's dead-straight thread; and every
lighthouse sweeping straight blades of \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} across the night sea.
Bent \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} is never seen --- fountains and forests may curve, \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}
will not.
\end{example}

\section{Proving it with three cards}

\begin{method}[The three-card experiment]\label{met:g4:light-straight-lines:cards}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Punch a small hole in the middle of three stiff cards, at
        the same height;
  \item stand the cards upright in a row, a hand-span apart, and a
        lit candle (or torch) beyond the last one;
  \item move your head until you see the flame through all three
        holes --- possible only when the three holes and the flame
        stand on one straight line (check: a taut string through
        the holes reaches the flame);
  \item now slide the middle card sideways, just a finger's width.
\end{enumerate}
The flame vanishes. \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{Light} that could have zigzagged --- through
hole one, a jog sideways, through the shifted hole, and on to your
eye --- simply does not: no straight line, no \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Shadows explained for good]\label{ex:g4:light-straight-lines:shadows}
Now the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} of your first school year gives up its secret. Rays
run straight from the lamp; the ball stops the ones that hit it;
the rays just missing its edge run straight on. Behind the ball
lies exactly the space that no straight ray can reach --- the
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}, with its sharp edge drawn by the last rays to slip past.
Straight rays, sharp \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadows}: crooked \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} would smear every
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} into fog.
\end{example}

\section{Seeing is receiving light}

\begin{proposition}[How seeing works]\label{prop:g4:light-straight-lines:seeing}
You see a thing when \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} from it travels in a straight line into
your eye --- its own \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} if it is a source, bounced sunlight or
lamplight otherwise. Nothing leaves your eye to grab the world:
seeing is \emph{receiving}, catching rays like a net catches balls.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Around the corner]\label{ex:g4:light-straight-lines:corner}
Your friend calls from around the corner of the school: you hear
them, but cannot see them. \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{Sound} spreads like pond rings and washes
around the corner's edge; \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} holds its straight roads, and no
straight line joins your friend's face to your eye through the
brick. To see around corners, you must \emph{fold the road} ---
give the ray a hard, shiny surface to bounce off. That trick is
called the mirror, and it owns the next year's first \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} chapter.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[The champion runner]\label{rem:g4:light-straight-lines:speed}
\omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{Sound}, we measured, jogs at \qty{340}{m} each second. \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{Light} on its
straight roads is in another league entirely: it crosses the whole
sky faster than any counting could catch --- which is why the flash
arrives ``at once'' and the thunder trudges in seconds behind. How
fast exactly? The number is so outrageous that we save it for a
later year --- with the tale of the clever tricks it took to
\omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:measure}{measure} it at all.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:light-straight-lines:1}
State the law of this chapter. Name two sightings of it outdoors.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:light-straight-lines:2}
What is a \omterm{def:g4:light-straight-lines:ray}{light ray}, and what two things does its drawing show?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:light-straight-lines:3}
In the three-card experiment, when exactly can you see the flame?
What single move makes it vanish, and why?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:light-straight-lines:4}
How does the straight-line law explain the sharp edge of a \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:light-straight-lines:5}
You see this book right now. Tell the journey of the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} that
makes that possible, from a lamp to your eye.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:light-straight-lines:6}
Why can you hear a friend around the corner but not see them? What
does each traveler --- \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} and \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} --- do at the corner?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:light-straight-lines:7}
A keyhole, a lit room beyond, and your eye: from where in the dark
corridor can you see the lamp through the keyhole? Answer with a
straight line.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:light-straight-lines:8}
Which arrives first from a distant firework, the bang or the
flash? Which law and which measurement from this year explain the
gap?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:light-straight-lines:9}
``When I look at a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star}, something shoots out of my eye to touch
it.'' Correct this old belief with
\cref{prop:g4:light-straight-lines:seeing} --- what travels, and
which way?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:light-straight-lines:10}
Design, with a drawing of rays, a way to watch the corridor from
inside your room using the mirror trick teased in
\cref{ex:g4:light-straight-lines:corner}: where must the shiny
surface stand at the doorway, and what does it do to the ray's
road? (Submarines use exactly this, stacked twice, to see above
the waves.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:light-straight-lines:11}
On a misty morning, sunbeams fan out between the trees in visibly
straight lines --- yet your friend says: ``We see the beams, so
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} must be visible stuff hanging in the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}.'' What are you both
actually seeing when a ``beam'' shows in mist or dust, and what
would the beam look like in perfectly clean \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}?
\end{exercise}
