\chapter{Conductors and Insulators}\label{ch:g4:conductors-insulators}

Why are electric wires made of metal --- but dressed in plastic?
Why does the \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch}'s little bridge conduct the current, while its
casing keeps your fingers safe? Last year we closed loops; this year
we ask which materials electricity can travel through at all. The
answer sorts every material into two kinds: \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:conductor}{conductors} and
\omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:insulator}{insulators}.

\section{The tester}

\begin{method}[Building a tester]\label{met:g4:conductors-insulators:tester}
Build the loop of last year --- \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}, wires, \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} --- but leave
one deliberate gap: two free wire ends, a finger's width apart.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item touch the two free ends together: the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} ---
        the tester works;
  \item bridge the gap with the object on trial, one metal end
        pressed firmly to each wire;
  \item watch the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb}: \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} means electricity crossed the object;
        darkness means it could not.
\end{enumerate}
The \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} gives the answer: it \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} exactly when the tested object
completes the loop.
\end{method}

\section{Conductors and insulators}

\begin{definition}[Conductor]\label{def:g4:conductors-insulators:conductor}
A \emph{conductor}\index{conductor} is a material that lets electricity
travel through it. In the tester, a conductor bridging the gap
closes the loop and \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Insulator]\label{def:g4:conductors-insulators:insulator}
An \emph{insulator}\index{insulator} is a material that blocks
electricity. Bridging the gap with an insulator leaves the loop as
open as before: the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} stays dark.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[A morning of tests]\label{ex:g4:conductors-insulators:trials}
Results from a classroom tester: a steel paperclip --- \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}. A
coin --- \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}. Aluminium kitchen foil --- \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}. A key --- \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}.
A plastic ruler --- dark. A wooden pencil --- dark. A glass marble
--- dark. A rubber eraser --- dark. A strip of paper --- dark. The
pattern jumps out: the objects that \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} are all made of
metal.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Metals conduct]\label{prop:g4:conductors-insulators:metals}
All metals conduct electricity --- iron, copper, aluminium, gold,
every one, which is why the \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet}'s fussiness about iron surprised
us but the tester never will. Most other everyday \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solids} ---
plastic, wood, glass, rubber, paper, wax --- are \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:insulator}{insulators}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Conductor is not magnetic]\label{rem:g4:conductors-insulators:notmagnet}
Do not mix up the two metal talents. The \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} chooses only iron
and its family; the electric current is far less picky and travels
through \emph{every} metal. The aluminium foil that ignored your
\omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} carries current beautifully --- two different games, two
different rules.
\end{remark}

\section{Conductors and insulators work together}

\begin{example}[Anatomy of a wire]\label{ex:g4:conductors-insulators:wire}
Look at the end of a lamp's cable where it enters the plug: inside
runs shiny copper --- a \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:conductor}{conductor}, the current's road; around it
wraps colored plastic --- an \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:insulator}{insulator}, the road's fence. The
copper does the work; the plastic keeps the current in and your
fingers out. Every cable in your house pairs the two: a \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:conductor}{conductor}
inside an \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:insulator}{insulator}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Reading everyday objects]\label{ex:g4:conductors-insulators:objects}
Now many designs explain themselves. The \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch}: a metal bridge (it
must carry current) in a plastic case (your finger must not). The
electrician's screwdriver: steel blade, thick insulating handle.
The socket: metal deep inside, plastic face outside. Pylons carry
bare metal cables high out of reach, hanging from long insulating
discs so the current cannot leak into the tower.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Water, people, and why rules exist]\label{rem:g4:conductors-insulators:safety}
One more \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:conductor}{conductor} must be named: \emph{damp
things} --- including damp human skin. We conduct badly, but the
wall socket pushes so mightily that badly is more than enough. This
is the deep reason behind the safety rules you know: never poke
sockets, never touch anything electric with wet hands, keep hair
dryers far from the bath. The \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery} tester is safe \emph{because}
a \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery}'s push is tiny; the socket forgives nothing.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:conductors-insulators:1}
What is a \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:conductor}{conductor}? An \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:insulator}{insulator}? Which one \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} the tester's
\omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:conductors-insulators:2}
Sort into \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:conductor}{conductors} and \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:insulator}{insulators}: a steel spoon; \ a cork; \ kitchen foil; \
a glass; \ a copper coin; \ a plastic brick.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:conductors-insulators:3}
Before any trial, how do you check that the tester itself works?
Why is this check important?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:conductors-insulators:4}
A trial object leaves the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb} dark. Give two possible reasons ---
one about the object, one about sloppy testing.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:conductors-insulators:5}
Why is a lamp cable copper on the inside and plastic on the
outside? What job does each material do?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:conductors-insulators:6}
In a \omterm{ex:g3:first-electric-circuit:switch}{switch}, which part is a \omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:conductor}{conductor} and which an
\omterm{def:g4:conductors-insulators:insulator}{insulator}? Why does it need both?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:conductors-insulators:7}
Aluminium foil ignores a \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} completely. Will it \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} the
tester's \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulb}? Which rule answers, and what mix-up does
\cref{rem:g4:conductors-insulators:notmagnet} warn against?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:conductors-insulators:8}
A pencil is wood with a graphite lead. Nino's tester stays dark
across the painted wood but \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} faintly tip-to-tip through the
sharpened graphite. What did Nino discover about the pencil's two
materials?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:conductors-insulators:9}
Why do electricians' screwdrivers and pliers have thick plastic
handles? Which material is protecting whom?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:conductors-insulators:10}
A necklace chain gives ``\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}'' in the tester; a wooden bead
necklace gives ``dark''; a necklace of metal beads knotted on a
thick cotton string gives ``dark'' too --- although every bead
conducts! Explain the third result by following the current's
road bead to bead.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:conductors-insulators:11}
Why is it far more dangerous to touch electrical things with wet
hands than with dry ones? And why is the \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:battery}{battery} tester safe for
your experiments while the socket never is? Use
\cref{rem:g4:conductors-insulators:safety}.
\end{exercise}
