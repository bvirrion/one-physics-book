\chapter{Magnets and the Compass}\label{ch:g4:magnets-and-compass}

Years ago you discovered that a \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet}'s two ends are different:
one way two \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnets} snap together, flipped around they push apart.
We promised those ends had names and a famous job --- guiding
travelers across the world. Today the promise is kept: welcome to
the \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{poles}, and to the little instrument that found the way for
every explorer before satellites existed.

\section{The two poles}

\begin{definition}[Poles]\label{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}
The two special ends of a \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} --- where its pull is strongest,
where the paperclips cluster --- are its \emph{poles}\index{pole}.
They are named the \emph{north pole}\index{north pole} (often
painted red, marked N) and the \emph{south pole}\index{south pole}
(marked S). Every \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} has both: one of each, always.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The rule of the poles]\label{prop:g4:magnets-and-compass:rule}
Opposite \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{poles} \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{attract} each other: N snaps to S. Like \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{poles} repel
each other: N pushes N away, S pushes S away. This one rule
explains every snap and every springy refusal you have ever felt
between two \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnets}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Reading old experiments]\label{ex:g4:magnets-and-compass:reading}
When your two \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnets} snapped together, an N was facing an S. When
they refused springily, N faced N (or S faced S). And the paperclips
always clustered at the ends because the \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{poles} are where a \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet}'s
power lives --- its middle is the calm between them.
\end{example}

\section{The Earth is a magnet}

\begin{example}[The greatest magnet of all]\label{ex:g4:magnets-and-compass:earth}
Hang a bar \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} from a thread so it can turn freely, wait for it
to settle --- and something wonderful happens: it always comes to
rest pointing the \emph{same way}, one \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{pole} toward the far north of
the world, the other toward the far south. Some giant, invisible
\omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} must be pulling its \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{poles} into line. That giant is the Earth
itself: our whole planet is a \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet}, with its magnetic \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{poles} near
the top and bottom of the globe.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Compass]\label{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}
A \emph{compass}\index{compass} is a small, \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} needle
balanced on a fine \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{pivot} so it can swing freely. Left in peace, the
needle obeys the \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{Earth-magnet}: its marked end settles pointing
north. Under the needle, a card shows the four \emph{cardinal
directions}\index{cardinal directions}: North, East, South, West.
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Using a compass]\label{met:g4:magnets-and-compass:use}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Hold the \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass} flat and steady, far from \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnets} and big
        iron things (fences, cars --- they distract the needle);
  \item wait for the needle to stop swinging;
  \item turn the \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass} card gently until its N sits under the
        needle's marked end;
  \item read your directions: East on your right when facing north,
        West on your left, South at your back.
\end{enumerate}
Check with the sky: around here, the midday \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} of the \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:sundial}{sundial}
chapter points north --- \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} and needle should agree.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Make one yourself]\label{ex:g4:magnets-and-compass:make}
Stroke a steel sewing needle with one \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{pole} of a \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet}, twenty
times, always the same way, lifting the \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} away between
strokes: the needle becomes a small \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} itself. Lay it gently on
a slice of cork floating in a bowl of water --- and watch the cork
slowly turn until the needle points north--south. You have built
the sailor's \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass} of a thousand years ago.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[The instrument that opened the oceans]\label{rem:g4:magnets-and-compass:history}
Before the \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass}, sailors hugged the coasts and prayed for clear
nights to steer by stars. With a floating needle, they could hold a
course through fog, storm and moonless dark --- across whole
oceans. Few instruments have redrawn the map of the world so
completely. And one puzzle remained open for centuries: \emph{why}
does the Earth act as a \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet}? The full story waits in the
university years of this course --- it is worth the wait.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:magnets-and-compass:1}
What are the \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{poles} of a \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet}, and what are their names? Where on
a bar \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} do paperclips cluster?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:magnets-and-compass:2}
State the rule of the \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{poles}. Two \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnets} snap together: which \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{poles}
were facing?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:magnets-and-compass:3}
The N \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{pole} of one \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} approaches the N \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{pole} of another. What
happens? And N toward S?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:magnets-and-compass:4}
Why does a freely hanging bar \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} always settle pointing the
same way? What does this reveal about the Earth?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:magnets-and-compass:5}
Name the four \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{cardinal directions} in order, starting from North and
turning right. Facing north, which direction is on your right?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:magnets-and-compass:6}
Why must a \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass} be kept away from \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnets}, iron fences and cars
while you read it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:magnets-and-compass:7}
Describe how to turn a sewing needle into a \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass} needle, and how
to let it swing freely without a \omterm{def:g3:levers-and-scales:lever}{pivot}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:magnets-and-compass:8}
From the \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:sundial}{sundial} chapter: which direction does the midday \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}
point around here? How can this check a \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass} --- or replace a
lost one?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:magnets-and-compass:9}
Why was the \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass} such a treasure for sailors, compared with
steering by the coastline or the stars?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:magnets-and-compass:10}
A hiker walks toward a mountain hut that lies due north. Her
\omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass} needle, held properly, points at her friend's belt buckle
instead of north whenever he stands close on her left. What is
happening, in which direction is she in danger of drifting, and
what is the one-step fix?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:magnets-and-compass:11}
A \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} is sawed in half between its two \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{poles}, hoping to get a
lone N in one hand and a lone S in the other. Sailors' lore --- and
every experiment ever tried --- says each half turns out to be a
complete little \omterm{def:g1:magnets:magnet}{magnet} with \emph{both} \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{poles}. Test the claim in
thought with the compass-needle recipe: the stroked sewing needle
is short --- does it have one \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{pole} or two? What does this suggest
about hunting a lone \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:poles}{pole} by cutting?
\end{exercise}
