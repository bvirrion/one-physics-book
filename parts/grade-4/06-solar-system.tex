\chapter{The Solar System}\label{ch:g4:solar-system}

The Earth, you know, makes a yearly lap around the Sun. But is it
traveling alone? Look up on a clear evening: among the fixed
sparkles, a few bright \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} slowly wander from week to week. The
ancients called them the wanderers; we call them \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} --- worlds
circling the same Sun as we do.

\section{One star, eight planets}

\begin{definition}[Star]\label{def:g4:solar-system:star}
A \emph{star}\index{star} is a gigantic ball of glowing-hot stuff
that makes its own \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} --- a true \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light source}. The Sun is a
star: our star, close enough to warm our faces. (The night's other
stars get a chapter of their own next year.)
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Planet]\label{def:g4:solar-system:planet}
A \emph{planet}\index{planet} is a big, round world that travels
around a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} and makes no \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} of its own --- we see planets, as
we see the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}, by the sunlight they send back. The Earth is a
planet.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The Solar System]\label{def:g4:solar-system:family}
The \emph{Solar System}\index{Solar System} is the Sun together
with everything that travels around it: the eight \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} --- in order: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn,
Uranus, Neptune --- plus their moons and a crowd of smaller rocks
and ice balls.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The planets' habits]\label{prop:g4:solar-system:habits}
The \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} keep tidy habits: each travels around the Sun on its
own nearly-circular road, all of them circle the same way, and all
the roads lie nearly in one flat plane --- like rings drawn on one
tabletop. And the farther a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}'s road from the Sun, the longer
it takes for one lap: Mercury laps in $88$ days, the Earth in one
year, far Neptune in $165$ years.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Rocky planets and gas giants]\label{ex:g4:solar-system:kinds}
The four inner \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} --- Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars --- are
balls of rock, small and \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid}: worlds you could stand on. The four
outer ones --- Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune --- are the
\emph{giants}: enormous balls mostly of thick \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas}, with no ground
to stand on at all. Jupiter, the biggest, could swallow more than a
thousand Earths; Saturn wears its famous shining rings --- countless
little pieces of ice circling it like a crowd of tiny moons.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Moons are not planets]\label{ex:g4:solar-system:moons}
Our \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} circles the \emph{Earth}, not the Sun directly --- so it
is not a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet} but a \emph{moon}, a companion of a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}. Other
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} have moons of their own: Mars has two small ones, Jupiter
and Saturn dozens each. A moon belongs to its \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}; the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}
belongs to the Sun.
\end{example}

\section{How far is far}

\begin{method}[A scale model of the Solar System]\label{met:g4:solar-system:model}
The distances between \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} are enormous --- millions of
kilometres. To feel them, build a scale model of the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:family}{Solar System}
in a park, shrinking every size and distance by the same factor:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item let the Sun be a football placed at the gate;
  \item then the Earth is a peppercorn --- and to place it fairly,
        walk about $30$ big steps from the football;
  \item Mercury, a pinhead, stands about $12$ steps from the
        football; Jupiter, a marble, about $150$ steps; Neptune, a
        small bead, about $900$ steps --- most of a kilometre away!
\end{enumerate}
Walk it, and feel what no drawing shows: the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:family}{Solar System} is almost
entirely empty space, with tiny worlds very far apart. In this model
one big step stands for about five million kilometres of real space.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[The wanderers in our sky]\label{ex:g4:solar-system:wanderers}
Venus is often the first ``\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star}'' of the evening, blazing in the
west after \omterm{def:g1:day-and-night:daynight}{sunset} --- so bright because it is our nearest
neighbor, wrapped in white clouds that send sunlight back
generously. Mars glows faintly orange; Jupiter and Saturn shine
steady and bright. Watch one of them for a few weeks against the
fixed \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}: it drifts --- the very wandering that gave \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets}
their ancient name.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[An old picture, redrawn]\label{rem:g4:solar-system:history}
For most of history, people placed the Earth at the center of
everything, with Sun, \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} and \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} circling us. Careful
watchers of the wanderers found that picture creaking --- \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets}
sometimes drift \emph{backward} for a while, hard to explain with
Earth enthroned in the middle. The Sun-centered picture you
have just learned explained the wanderings simply, and it stands
among the boldest ideas humans ever accepted: our home is not the
center --- it is the third \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet} from the Sun.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:solar-system:1}
What is the difference between a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star} and a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}? Which one is
the Sun? The Earth?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:solar-system:2}
Recite the eight \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} in order from the Sun. Which is ours?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:solar-system:3}
Which \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} are small and rocky? Which are the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} giants? Name
the biggest of all, and the one with the famous rings.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:solar-system:4}
Why is the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} not counted among the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets}? What does it circle?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:solar-system:5}
Mercury laps the Sun in $88$ days, the Earth in $365$, Neptune in
$165$ years. What habit of the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} do these numbers show?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:solar-system:6}
In the park scale model, what plays the Sun, and how many steps
away does the Earth stand? What is nearly all of the
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:family}{Solar System} made of, by room?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:solar-system:7}
Venus shines brilliantly, yet it is no \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star}. Where does its \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}
come from? (An old friend answers: think of the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:solar-system:8}
How could you tell, watching the sky for a few weeks, that a bright
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} is a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet} and not a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g4:solar-system:9}
In the park model, Jupiter stands at about $150$ steps and the
Earth at about $30$. Roughly how many times farther from the Sun is
Jupiter than the Earth?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:solar-system:10}
A friend draws the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:family}{Solar System} with all eight \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets} packed
snugly around the Sun like the chapter's first picture, and asks
why probes take years to reach the outer \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planets}. Set them straight
with the park scale model --- what do almost all drawings of the
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:family}{Solar System} lie about, and why do they have to?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g4:solar-system:11}
When the Earth was believed to sit at the center of everything,
what sky-watching puzzle creaked against that picture? How did
putting the Sun at the center dissolve the puzzle --- and what did
the new portrait cost us in pride?
\end{exercise}
