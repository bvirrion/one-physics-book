\chapter{Sources of Light and Propagation}\label{ch:g7:light-sources-propagation}

Why can you read this page? A lamp shines --- but the page is no
lamp, and yet your eye receives \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} from every printed line.
Between the things that make \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} and the things that merely
hand it on lies a distinction optics cannot live without; and
with it comes the full answer to the oldest question in this
book: what does it take to see something?

\section{Makers and forwarders}

\begin{definition}[Primary sources]\label{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:primary}
A \emph{primary source}\index{primary source} makes its own
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}: the Sun and the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}, flames, glowing filaments, the
white-hot steel of the forge, fireflies, lightning. Its \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}
exists whether or not anything else shines --- switch off the
world, and a primary source still glows.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Diffusing objects]\label{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:diffusion}
Every other visible thing is a \emph{diffusing
object}\index{diffusion}: it receives \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} and re-sends ---
\emph{diffuses} --- part of it in all directions. The page, the
apple, the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}, your own face: none makes a glimmer of its own;
each scatters received \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} generously to every side. A
diffuser in the dark is simply invisible.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Diffusion is not mirroring]\label{ex:g7:light-sources-propagation:notmirror}
Both \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{mirrors} and pages send received \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} back --- but with
opposite manners. The \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{mirror} bounces in \emph{one} disciplined
direction (equal angles --- your old bounce rule), preserving
the picture; the page's microscopic roughness sprays \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}
\emph{everywhere}, destroying the picture but offering itself to
every eye at once. That is why a \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{mirror} shows the lamp, while
the page merely shows itself, from wherever you stand.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The Moon's file, closed]\label{ex:g7:light-sources-propagation:moon}
The childhood verdict now has its formal words: the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} is no
\omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:primary}{primary source}; it is a \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:diffusion}{diffusing object} of planetary size ---
gray rock scattering sunlight in all directions, our way
included. So moonlight is secondhand sunshine, and the ``dark
side'' is simply the half whose file holds, at that moment, no
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} to forward.
\end{example}

\section{Seeing, settled}

\begin{proposition}[The two conditions of vision]\label{prop:g7:light-sources-propagation:vision}
An eye sees an object exactly when
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} \emph{leaves} the object --- made, if the object is
        a \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:primary}{primary source}; diffused, if a forwarder --- and
  \item that \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} travels in a straight, unblocked line into
        the eye.
\end{enumerate}
Break either condition --- an unlit diffuser, or a wall across
the sight line --- and the object is unseen. Every visibility
puzzle in this book unpacks into these two checks.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The beam made visible]\label{ex:g7:light-sources-propagation:beam}
In clean \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}, a torch \omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beam} crossing the room cannot be seen from
the side --- \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} forwards almost nothing, and no \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} from the
\omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beam} turns toward your eye. Shake a dusty cloth: the \omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beam} leaps
into view. Each dust mote is a tiny diffuser, catching \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{beam-light}
and scattering some your way --- thousands of specks drawing the
\omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beam}'s straight line for you. Fog, mist and smoke perform the
same service for lighthouse \omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beams} and cinema projectors.
\end{example}

\section{Three coats for light}

\begin{definition}[Transparent, translucent, opaque]\label{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:coats}
Materials meet \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} in three manners:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{transparent}\index{transparent}: \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} crosses
        nearly undisturbed, keeping its order --- clear glass,
        still water, \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}; \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{images} pass intact;
  \item \emph{translucent}\index{translucent}: \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} crosses,
        but scrambled --- frosted glass, tracing paper, a thin
        curtain; brightness passes, \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{images} do not;
  \item \emph{opaque}\index{opaque}: \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} is stopped ---
        absorbed or diffused back --- wood, stone, metal, your
        hand; behind an opaque thing lies \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{method}[Sorting materials by torchlight]\label{met:g7:light-sources-propagation:sort}
A dark room, a torch, a printed page, and the specimen between:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item hold the specimen over the print with the torch behind
        it;
  \item read the print through it? --- \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:coats}{transparent};
  \item see glow but no letters? --- \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:coats}{translucent};
  \item see darkness, and a \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} beyond? --- \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:coats}{opaque}.
\end{enumerate}
Test honestly: many materials change team with thickness --- a
sheet of water is \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:coats}{transparent}, the deep sea is night-dark; one
paper sheet is \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:coats}{translucent}, the closed book \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:coats}{opaque}.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Architecture's three teams]\label{ex:g7:light-sources-propagation:architecture}
Builders deploy all three on purpose. \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:coats}{Transparent} window glass:
view and \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} together. \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:coats}{Translucent} bathroom panes and frosted
office partitions: daylight in, privacy kept --- brightness
without \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{images} is exactly their job description. \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:coats}{Opaque} walls
and shutters: darkness on demand. Lampshades are translucency in
service of comfort: the blazing filament's glare is scrambled
into a soft, sourceless glow.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Nothing to forward, nothing to see]\label{rem:g7:light-sources-propagation:space}
Riddle: an astronaut between the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} looks \emph{sideways}
across a sunbeam-filled void. What does she see? Blackness ---
though sunlight floods past her eyes. Space carries no dust
worth mentioning: nothing diffuses the passing \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} toward
her, and \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} that does not enter the eye paints nothing. The
daytime sky is blue precisely because our \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} \emph{does}
forward sunlight down to us --- a \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}'s worth of gentle
\omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:diffusion}{diffusion} overhead. No \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}, black sky, even at noon: the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}'s
astronauts stood in sunshine under a starless black dome.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:1}
Sort: candle flame; \ the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}; \ a firefly; \ this page; \ a
red traffic \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}; \ the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet} Venus at dusk.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:2}
What does a \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:diffusion}{diffusing object} do with the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} it receives ---
and what becomes of it in a dark room?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:3}
State the two conditions of vision, and use them: why can you
not see a perfectly clean \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{mirror}'s \emph{own} surface, only the
things it reflects? (Careful --- fine question; answer simply:
what does its polish refuse to do with \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}?)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:4}
Why does everyone around the table see the same apple, while
only one lucky eye position catches the lamp's \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{reflection} in a
spoon?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:5}
Why is a torch \omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beam} invisible from the side in clean \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}, and
visible in mist? Name the process.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:6}
Classify: window glass; \ tracing paper; \ a brick; \ shallow
clear water; \ a frosted lightbulb's envelope; \ aluminium
foil.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:7}
What job does translucency do in a bathroom window and in a
lampshade? What passes, what is stopped?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:8}
The \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} is sunlit --- yet lunar astronauts saw a black sky at
noon. Explain with
\cref{rem:g7:light-sources-propagation:space}, and say why
Earth's noon sky is bright blue instead.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:9}
A single sheet of paper glows warmly in front of a torch; the
closed book stops the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} entirely. What does thickness do to
a material's team membership? Give a second example of a
team-changer.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:10}
Cinema trick: projectors' \omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beams} are invisible in a clean hall,
yet films show them slicing dramatically through the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}. What
must the set crew add to the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}, and what physics are they
buying?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:11}
You see a friend's face by a campfire. Trace the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}'s full
journey --- source, \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:diffusion}{diffusion}, straight lines --- and count how
many diffusers stand in the chain if you then \emph{also} see
that face reflected in the lake.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:light-sources-propagation:12}
Nature's own \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} show: on a bright day, the sky away from the
Sun is blue, the Sun's disc blinding white --- and at \omterm{def:g1:day-and-night:daynight}{sunset} the
low Sun turns orange while the clouds above catch pink. Using
only ``\omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} diffuses a little of the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} passing through it,
and more so the longer the path'', explain why the low sun is
robbed of part of its \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} --- and who receives the stolen
share. (The full color story is next year's spectra chapter;
reason here only with more-path, \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:diffusion}{more-diffusion}.)
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: Lighting the Museum}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the new sculpture hall; a
lighting designer's night shift; sources, diffusers and three
kinds of glass}]\label{pb:g7:light-sources-propagation:1}
The museum's new hall opens Monday: one marble statue, paintings
on the walls, a glass display case, and a nervous curator. You
assist the lighting designer.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- First principles on site.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The curator asks why the statue, ``so brilliantly
        white'', still needs lamps at all. Answer with the two
        conditions of vision.
  \item List the \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:primary}{primary sources} and the diffusers in the hall
        once the lamps are on: lamps, statue, paintings,
        visitors, the case's glass.
  \item The marble seems to glow from everywhere, with no
        blinding spot. Which manner of returning \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} is at
        work, and why does every visitor position receive it?
  \item One polished brass plaque, by contrast, throws one
        dazzling \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{reflection} of a lamp. Name the other manner,
        and state the rule governing where that dazzle goes.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Taming the glare.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Bare \omterm{def:g3:first-electric-circuit:bulb}{bulbs} glare in visitors' eyes. The designer sheathes
        each in a frosted globe. Which material team is the
        globe on, and what does it do to the filament's \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}?
  \item The paintings must be lit, but no lamp may appear
        \emph{reflected} in their varnish from the main viewing
        spot. Using the \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{mirror} rule, where does the designer
        place the lamps relative to painting and viewer?
  \item The display case's clear glass is nearly invisible ---
        until a visitor's white sleeve appears in it like a
        ghost. Why does clear glass both transmit and faintly
        \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{mirror}? What does the designer's black-walled niche
        behind the case achieve?
  \item Dust motes sometimes drift through the spotlights'
        \omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beams}, drawing bright threads in the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}. Curator:
        ``Beautiful! Keep it.'' Designer: ``It means dirty
        \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}.'' Who is right about the physics, and what are the
        threads?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The night test.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{Lights} out, shutters sealed: the guard reports the hall
        ``absolutely black --- not even the white statue''.
        Explain with the two conditions why whiteness is
        helpless in the dark.
  \item A skylight is proposed: frosted glass overhead. What
        will it deliver by day, and what will it refuse to
        deliver (views of clouds, direct sun-dazzle)?
  \item The curator fears sunlight fading the paintings and
        asks for ``\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} without any sun''. The designer smiles:
        by day, even the north-facing windows' soft \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} \emph{is}
        sunshine. Explain --- what has forwarded it?
  \item Write the designer's report: three sentences --- one on
        why \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:diffusion}{diffusion} is the museum's best friend, one on where
        mirror-manner \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{reflections} must be watched, and one on
        the team of glass chosen for each duty.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
