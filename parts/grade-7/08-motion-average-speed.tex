\chapter{Motion Graphs and Average Speed}\label{ch:g7:motion-average-speed}

Two years ago, \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} meant ``kilometres covered in one hour,''
counted on your fingers. Now, armed with letters and graphs, the
idea grows teeth: a formula that computes in three directions, a
picture that shows a whole journey at a glance --- and a famous
trap about averages that catches adults daily.

\section{The formula}

\begin{definition}[Speed, by formula]\label{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}
For a journey (or stretch of one) covered at a steady pace, the
\emph{speed}\index{speed} $v$ is the distance $d$ divided by the
travel time $t$:
\[
v = \frac{d}{t}.
\]
With $d$ in kilometres and $t$ in \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours}, $v$ speaks
\emph{kilometres per hour} (\unit{km/h}); with \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres} and
seconds, \emph{\omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres} per second} (\unit{m/s}) --- the
scientist's favorite. Like \omterm{def:g7:volume-mass-density:formula}{density}'s, this formula computes in
all three directions: $v = d/t$, \ $d = v \times t$, \
$t = d/v$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The recipes at work]\label{ex:g7:motion-average-speed:recipes}
A train covers \qty{240}{km} in $2$ \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours}: $v = 240 \div 2 =
\qty{120}{km/h}$. How far at that pace in $5$ \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours}? $d = 120
\times 5 = \qty{600}{km}$. How long for \qty{300}{km}? $t = 300
\div 120 = 2.5$ \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours} --- two \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours} and thirty minutes (mind the
rebel: $0.5$ \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours} is $30$ minutes, never ``$50$'').
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Two units, one speed]\label{ex:g7:motion-average-speed:units}
A sprinter runs \qty{100}{m} in \qty{10}{s}: $v = \qty{10}{m/s}$.
How fast is that in road \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{units}? In one hour --- $3600$ seconds
--- the sprinter would cover $10 \times 3600 = \qty{36000}{m} =
\qty{36}{km}$: so $\qty{10}{m/s} = \qty{36}{km/h}$. The general
exchange rate: each \unit{m/s} is worth \qty{3.6}{km/h} ---
because an hour holds $3600$ seconds and a kilometre only
$1000$ \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres}. \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{Sound}'s \qty{340}{m/s}, in road \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{units}: over
\qty{1200}{km/h}.
\end{example}

\section{The journey as a picture}

\begin{proposition}[The graph laws]\label{prop:g7:motion-average-speed:graphs}
Plot distance covered against time, and motion writes its
autobiography:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:uniform}{uniform motion} draws a \emph{straight} line --- equal
        distances in equal times, step after step;
  \item the \emph{steeper} the line, the faster the motion:
        steepness is \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} made visible;
  \item a \emph{flat} stretch is a stop: time passes, distance
        stands.
\end{enumerate}
Bends tell of change: curving upward, speeding up; flattening,
slowing down.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Reading a distance--time graph]\label{met:g7:motion-average-speed:read}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item check the axes and their \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{units} first --- minutes or
        \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours}, \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres} or kilometres;
  \item split the line at its bends into stretches; label each:
        straight-and-steep, straight-and-gentle, flat;
  \item for any straight stretch, read off its rise and its run
        --- distance gained, time taken --- and divide: the
        stretch's \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed};
  \item for the whole story, read total distance at the final
        time --- and remember the flat stretches are part of the
        total time.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{example}[The courier, decoded]\label{ex:g7:motion-average-speed:decoded}
Apply the method to the figure. First stretch: \qty{10}{km} in
$20$ minutes --- a third of an hour --- so $v = 10 \div
\tfrac{1}{3} = \qty{30}{km/h}$. Second: flat from minute $20$ to
$35$ --- a delivery stop. Third: \qty{10}{km} in $25$ minutes,
a shade under \qty{24}{km/h}. Total: \qty{20}{km} in one hour
--- which hands us the chapter's next idea on a plate.
\end{example}

\section{Average speed --- and its famous trap}

\begin{definition}[Average speed]\label{def:g7:motion-average-speed:average}
The \emph{average speed}\index{average speed} of a whole journey
is the total distance divided by the \emph{total} time --- stops
included:
\[
v_{\text{average}} = \frac{d_{\text{total}}}{t_{\text{total}}}.
\]
It is the one steady pace that would have covered the same road
in the same overall time --- the courier's \qty{20}{km} in one
hour: average \qty{20}{km/h}, though the wheels never once
turned at that \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The trap]\label{prop:g7:motion-average-speed:trap}
The \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:average}{average speed} of a journey is \emph{not}, in general, the
midpoint of its \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speeds}. Slow stretches eat more time than fast
ones, so they weigh more heavily in the average. Only the full
recipe --- total distance over total time --- is trustworthy;
averaging the \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} numbers themselves is the most seductive
wrong move in the chapter.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The trap, sprung]\label{ex:g7:motion-average-speed:sprung}
A cyclist rides \qty{60}{km} out at \qty{30}{km/h}, and the same
\qty{60}{km} home at \qty{20}{km/h}. ``Average:
\qty{25}{km/h}''? Check honestly. Out: $t = 60 \div 30 = 2$
\omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours}. Home: $t = 60 \div 20 = 3$ \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours}. Whole journey:
\qty{120}{km} in $5$ \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours} ---
$v_{\text{average}} = \qty{24}{km/h}$. The slow half claimed
three \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours} of the five, and dragged the average below the
midpoint. The faster you go on one half, the less time that
half even exists.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[What the speedometer knows]\label{rem:g7:motion-average-speed:speedometer}
\omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:average}{Average speed} describes a whole journey; the speedometer needle
answers a different question --- \emph{how fast right now}. On
the graph, ``right now'' lives in the line's steepness \emph{at
a single point} --- easy to see on a straight stretch, subtle
where the line curves. Making ``steepness at a point'' precise
is one of mathematics' greatest inventions, and it waits for you
at the end of high school. Until then: straight stretches get
numbers, curves get stories.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:1}
Write the \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} formula and its two everyday \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{units}. Which recipe
finds a distance? A time?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:2}
A ferry covers \qty{45}{km} in $3$ \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours}; a hare runs
\qty{100}{m} in \qty{8}{s}. Compute both \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speeds}, each in its
natural \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{unit}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:3}
Convert, with the exchange rate: \qty{5}{m/s} to \unit{km/h}; \
\qty{72}{km/h} to \unit{m/s}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:4}
State the three graph laws. What does a bend that flattens
gradually tell?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:5}
On the courier's graph: between which minutes is the pace
gentlest (but not zero)? How can you tell without computing?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:6}
A walker's graph shows a straight line through the points
($30$ min, \qty{2}{km}) and ($60$ min, \qty{4}{km}). Uniform or
varied? \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{Speed} in \unit{km/h}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:7}
Define \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:average}{average speed}. A hike: \qty{12}{km} in $4$ \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours}
including a one-hour picnic. \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:average}{Average speed} --- and \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:average}{average speed}
\emph{while walking}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:8}
Why is a slow stretch ``heavier'' in an average than a fast one
of equal length? Answer with time, not with formulas.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:9}
Replay the trap: \qty{30}{km} out at \qty{15}{km/h},
\qty{30}{km} back at \qty{30}{km/h}. Predicted midpoint,
honest average --- and which half of the journey owned most of
the clock?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:10}
Sketch (or describe precisely) the graph of this trip: uniform
\qty{40}{km/h} for half an hour; stopped for a quarter hour;
uniform \qty{60}{km/h} for a quarter hour. Then compute the
trip's \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:average}{average speed}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:11}
Storm-counting, upgraded: thunder arrives \qty{6}{s} after the
flash. With \omterm{def:g3:sound-around-us:sound}{sound} at \qty{340}{m/s}, use $d = v \times t$ for
the storm's distance --- and check the old ``divide by three
for kilometres'' rule against your formula.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:motion-average-speed:12}
An old chestnut, worth every minute: a driver covers the first
half \emph{of the distance} of a trip at \qty{30}{km/h} and
wants an overall average of \qty{60}{km/h}. Show --- with the
total-distance-over-total-time recipe on a \qty{60}{km} trip
--- that the second half would have to be covered in zero time:
the wish is impossible, not merely difficult.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Courier's Friday}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- one bicycle courier, one
city Friday; the dispatcher's graph, the trap in the bonus
sheet}]\label{pb:g7:motion-average-speed:1}
The dispatcher records courier Lena's Friday on a
distance--time graph and settles her pay from it. You audit the
sheet.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The morning, from the
graph.}
The graph shows: a straight climb from ($0$ min, \qty{0}{km})
to ($30$ min, \qty{9}{km}); flat until $45$ min; straight from
there to ($75$ min, \qty{15}{km}); flat until $90$ min.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Tell the morning's story in words: stretches, stops,
        and what the stops presumably were.
  \item \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{Speed} on the first stretch, in \unit{km/h}?
  \item \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{Speed} on the second riding stretch?
  \item Lena's \emph{average} \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} over the whole $90$-minute
        morning, stops included?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The afternoon, from the
formula.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Afternoon leg one: \qty{12}{km} at a steady
        \qty{24}{km/h}. How long did it take, in minutes?
  \item Leg two: a delivery uptown, \qty{20}{minutes} at
        \qty{18}{km/h}. How many kilometres?
  \item Leg three: the long ride to the depot, \qty{10}{km},
        done in $25$ minutes. Steady-pace \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} in
        \unit{km/h}?
  \item Total afternoon: add the distances and the times (legs
        only, no breaks): what \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:average}{average speed} did the wheels
        keep while rolling?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The bonus sheet's trap.}
The bonus rule: ``\omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:average}{average speed} over a full tour above
\qty{20}{km/h} earns the fast-rider bonus.''
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Lena's full Friday: \qty{37}{km} in $3$ \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours} (all
        stops included). Does she earn the bonus?
  \item Her colleague Max claims it: ``I rode half my tour at
        \qty{30}{km/h} and half at \qty{15}{km/h} ---
        that averages $22.5$!'' The dispatcher checks: both
        halves were \qty{12}{km}. Compute Max's true average
        and rule on his bonus.
  \item Explain to Max, in time-language, where his $22.5$
        went wrong.
  \item Lena proposes a fairer bonus rule for couriers ---
        one that does not punish delivery stops. Suggest one
        (the graph knows how), and say which \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speed} it
        \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{measures}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The dispatcher's lesson.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Friday review: write the dispatcher's three
        sentences --- what a straight stretch, a flat
        stretch, and a steeper stretch each mean on the
        graph --- and the one warning about averaging
        \omterm{def:g7:motion-average-speed:formula}{speeds}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
