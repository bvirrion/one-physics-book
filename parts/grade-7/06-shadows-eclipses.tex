\chapter{Shadows and Eclipses}\label{ch:g7:shadows-eclipses}

Once or twice in a lifetime, for a few minutes, day becomes night:
the Sun's disc goes black behind the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} and \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} appear at
noon. Ancient peoples read doom in it; you will read geometry.
An eclipse is nothing but a \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} --- the largest \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} you
will ever stand in --- and your ruler-and-ray toolkit is exactly
what it obeys.

\section{Shadows from honest lamps}

\begin{proposition}[Umbra and penumbra]\label{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}
A \emph{point-like} source throws a single, sharp-edged \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}.
A \emph{broad} source --- a frosted globe, the Sun's whole disc
--- throws a two-part \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the \emph{umbra}\index{umbra}: the region reached by
        \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} from \emph{no} part of the source --- full
        darkness;
  \item the \emph{penumbra}\index{penumbra}: the fringe reached
        by \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} from \emph{part} of the source only --- half
        \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}, fading outward.
\end{enumerate}
From inside the \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{umbra}, the source is entirely hidden; from the
\omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{penumbra}, partly hidden --- peeking over the blocker's edge.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Constructing the two shadows]\label{met:g7:shadows-eclipses:construct}
For a broad source, rays from its \emph{edges} rule the map:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item draw rays from the source's top edge grazing the
        blocker's two sides, and the same from the bottom edge;
  \item the region behind the blocker that \emph{both} edges'
        rays miss is the \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{umbra};
  \item the flanking regions missed by one edge but reached by
        the other are the \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{penumbra};
  \item where all rays arrive, full \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}.
\end{enumerate}
The \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{umbra} of a round blocker is a cone --- and whether that cone
reaches a given screen decides everything about eclipses.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Soft edges everywhere]\label{ex:g7:shadows-eclipses:soft}
Now the smeared \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadows} of frosted lamps and cloudy skies are
accounted for: broad sources wrap every \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} in \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{penumbra}, and
a big enough source (the whole overcast sky) is all \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{penumbra} ---
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadows} washed to nothing. Your own noon \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}'s edge, looked
at closely, is a soft penumbral hem a \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{centimetre} wide: the Sun
is a disc, not a point, and the hem is its confession.
\end{example}

\section{The Moon steps into the beam}

\begin{definition}[Solar eclipse]\label{def:g7:shadows-eclipses:solar}
A \emph{solar eclipse}\index{solar eclipse} happens when the
\omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} passes exactly between Sun and Earth, and its \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} falls
on us. Where the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}'s \emph{\omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{umbra}} touches the Earth --- a
racing spot rarely two hundred kilometres wide --- the Sun's
disc is entirely hidden: \emph{total} eclipse, night at noon,
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} out. In the vast surrounding \emph{\omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{penumbra}}, the Sun is
only partly bitten: \emph{partial} eclipse.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Lunar eclipse]\label{def:g7:shadows-eclipses:lunar}
A \emph{lunar eclipse}\index{lunar eclipse} happens when the
\emph{\omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}} sails into the \emph{Earth's} \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} cone: the \omterm{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}{full
moon} darkens --- often to a deep copper glow --- for an hour or
more, visible to the whole night half of the world at once. This
time we are the blockers, and the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} stands in our \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{umbra}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Why so rare?]\label{ex:g7:shadows-eclipses:rare}
New moon comes every month --- why not a \omterm{def:g7:shadows-eclipses:solar}{solar eclipse} every
month? Because the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}'s \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbit} is \emph{tilted}, by about five
degrees, against the Earth's path around the Sun. Most months,
the new moon passes \emph{above} or \emph{below} the Sun--Earth
line, and its \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} spears harmlessly into space. Only when a
new moon happens just as the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} crosses the alignment plane do
the three worlds line up --- a few times a year somewhere on
Earth, and for any \emph{particular} town, totality is a
once-in-lifetimes visitor.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The coincidence that makes totality]\label{ex:g7:shadows-eclipses:coincidence}
Your sports-field model found it: the Sun is about $400$ times
wider than the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} --- and stands about $400$ times farther.
The two discs therefore match in the sky almost exactly, so the
\omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} can cover the Sun \emph{just barely}: totality is brief and
its umbra-spot small. When the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} is at the far end of its
slightly oval \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbit}, its disc falls short, and a burning ring of
Sun rims the black \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} --- an \emph{annular} eclipse, beautiful
and never safe to watch unprotected.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Watching without weeping]\label{rem:g7:shadows-eclipses:safety}
The old law has no eclipse exception: \emph{never} look at the
Sun --- eclipsed, ringed or whole --- with bare eyes,
sunglasses, or any lens. A partly hidden Sun burns retinas as
surely as the whole one, and painlessly. The safe instruments
are old friends: the pinhole --- project the Sun's \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{image} through
a small hole onto paper and watch the bite grow at comfort ---
or a leafy tree, whose thousand leaf-gap pinholes strew the
ground with little \omterm{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}{crescents}. Only certified eclipse glasses,
inspected and undamaged, may face the Sun directly; during
\emph{totality's} few minutes alone, the bare corona may be
admired --- and the first returning sliver ends the truce.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:1}
Define \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{umbra} and \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{penumbra} by what an eye inside each can see of
the source.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:2}
Why does a point-like lamp throw no \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{penumbra} at all? And why
does an overcast sky throw almost no \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:3}
In the construction method, whose rays draw the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} map ---
the source's center or its edges? What marks the \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{umbra}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:4}
\omterm{def:g7:shadows-eclipses:solar}{Solar eclipse}: put Sun, \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} and Earth in order, and say what
the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}'s phase must be that day. \omterm{def:g7:shadows-eclipses:lunar}{Lunar eclipse}: same
questions.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:5}
During a \omterm{def:g7:shadows-eclipses:solar}{solar eclipse}, who sees totality, who sees a partial
bite, and who sees nothing unusual at all?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:6}
Why is a \omterm{def:g7:shadows-eclipses:lunar}{lunar eclipse} visible from half the world at once,
while totality's path is a narrow racing stripe?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:7}
Why is there no eclipse every month, despite a new moon and a
\omterm{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}{full moon} every month?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:8}
List two safe ways to follow a \omterm{def:g7:shadows-eclipses:solar}{solar eclipse}'s progress, and the
one moment when bare eyes are permitted.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:9}
Explain the $400$-and-$400$ coincidence and both of its
children: the near-perfect fit of totality, and the ring of the
annular eclipse.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:10}
Look closely at your \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}'s edge at noon: crisp at your feet,
softer at your head's \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}. Explain the gradient with the
Sun's disc and the construction method --- why does \omterm{prop:g7:shadows-eclipses:umbra}{penumbra}
widen with distance from the blocker?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:11}
During a partial \omterm{def:g7:shadows-eclipses:solar}{solar eclipse}, the leafy-tree \omterm{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}{crescents} of two
years ago return. Explain them now in full: what is each
leaf-gap, what is each ground-patch, and why do all the
\omterm{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}{crescents} point the same way?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:shadows-eclipses:12}
During a total \emph{lunar} eclipse the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} rarely vanishes: it
glows deep copper-red. The \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} reaching it has grazed the rim
of the Earth --- passing through our \omterm{def:g1:day-and-night:daynight}{sunset} \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} all around the
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet} at once. Assemble the explanation from last chapter's
long-path \omterm{def:g7:light-sources-propagation:diffusion}{diffusion}: what has our \omterm{def:g5:air-pressure-weather:pressure}{atmosphere} done to the
grazing \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}, and why is the survivor \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} red? (You are
explaining, in one stroke, why the eclipsed \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} wears the color
of every \omterm{def:g1:day-and-night:daynight}{sunset} on Earth.)
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Eclipse Expedition}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the class plans its eclipse
expedition; geometry chooses the campsite, physics packs the
bags; totality, minute by minute}]\label{pb:g7:shadows-eclipses:1}
Next spring, a total \omterm{def:g7:shadows-eclipses:solar}{solar eclipse} crosses the continent. The
class plans an expedition --- with ray diagrams, not brochures.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Choosing where to stand.}
The atlas shows the predicted path: a stripe \qty{160}{km} wide
running across the map, flanked by a partial-eclipse zone
thousands of kilometres broad.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} region does the stripe trace, and what
        region flanks it?
  \item The class's town sits \qty{300}{km} from the stripe's
        centerline. What will the town see on eclipse day?
  \item Why must the expedition travel to the stripe --- why
        will ``ninety-five percent partial'' at home not show
        night at noon, \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}, or the corona?
  \item The stripe's forecast marks totality at 10:41, lasting
        $3$ minutes $12$ seconds at the centerline and less
        toward the edges. Why is totality longest at the
        center of the stripe?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Packing the physics.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The teacher packs certified eclipse glasses and confiscates
        two pairs of sunglasses. Justify both acts.
  \item Each pupil must build a pinhole projector from a
        shoebox. Recall its architecture, and state which way
        to stand relative to the Sun.
  \item One pupil proposes ``watching in the \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{reflection} of a
        bucket of water''. Rule on the proposal.
  \item The group will also carry a colander to hold over a
        white sheet. Predict, and explain, what its dozens of
        holes will paint during the partial phases.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The day, minute by
minute.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item 09:30, first contact: through the glasses, a small bite
        appears at the Sun's edge. What body is taking the
        bite, and which of its \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} regions has reached the
        camp?
  \item 10:39: the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} turns eerie and thin; \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadows} on the
        ground grow strangely sharp-edged. Explain the
        sharpening with umbra-and-penumbra thinking --- what is
        the Sun becoming as its visible sliver narrows?
  \item 10:41, totality: glasses off, by the rule. For $3$
        minutes the class stands inside something. Name it
        precisely, and state what has become visible around the
        black disc --- and why the truce ends at the first
        returning sliver.
  \item 12:00, on the bus home, the teacher asks for the
        one-sentence summary: ``An eclipse is\dots'' Complete
        it worthily, with the chapter's geometry inside it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
