\chapter{States of Matter and Changes of State}\label{ch:g7:states-of-matter}

Six years of \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solids} that keep their shape, \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquids} that lie level,
gases that fill every corner --- and never once did we ask
\emph{why}. This year, physics opens the lid. Underneath the three
states hides one of the greatest ideas humans ever had: everything,
absolutely everything, is made of particles too small to see. Grant
that one idea, and the rules you have collected since childhood
explain themselves in a single stroke.

\section{The great idea}

\begin{definition}[Molecules]\label{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}
All matter is made of \emph{molecules}\index{molecule}:
unimaginably small particles, far below what any eye or school
microscope can see. A single drop of water holds more molecules
than there are drops of water in all the seas. Each pure substance
is built of its own kind of molecule --- water of water-molecules,
iron of iron's own particles --- and a substance's molecules are
all identical.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Believing without seeing]\label{rem:g7:states-of-matter:believing}
No one has ever watched a single \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecule} with the naked eye ---
so why believe in them? Because the idea \emph{earns} belief: it
predicts, correctly, everything this chapter contains, and
mountains more. That is how physics accepts an invisible guest:
not on faith, but on relentless usefulness. (Direct \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{images} from
marvelous modern instruments finally arrived a century ago ---
long after physicists were already sure.)
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[The particle model of the three states]\label{prop:g7:states-of-matter:model}
The three states are three arrangements of the same \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{\omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid}}: \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} packed tight in an orderly
        stack, each trembling in place but holding its position
        --- hence the fixed shape;
  \item \emph{\omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}}: \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} still touching, but disordered
        and free to slide past one another --- hence flowing, and
        the level surface;
  \item \emph{\omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas}}: \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} far apart, flying fast in all
        directions, colliding and rebounding --- hence filling
        every corner offered.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Six years of rules, one explanation]\label{ex:g7:states-of-matter:rules}
Run the old rules through the model. \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{Solids} keep their shape:
every \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecule} holds its post. \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{Liquids} take the container's shape
and lie level: the sliding crowd slumps until no \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecule} can
slide lower. Gases fill all the room offered: nothing holds the
flyers back. The perfume crossing the room: \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} flying,
colliding, spreading. Sand pouring like a \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}: not \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules}
sliding, but grains --- each grain itself a tiny orderly \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid} of
trillions of \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules}.
\end{example}

\section{The empty space test}

\begin{method}[Two syringes]\label{met:g7:states-of-matter:syringe}
The model claims \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} fly far apart, \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules}
touch. Then \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} should squeeze, and \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} should not. Test it:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item fill a plastic syringe with \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}, cap the nozzle with a
        fingertip, and push the plunger: it yields --- you can
        halve the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air}'s room, feeling a springy fight-back;
  \item fill the same syringe with water, cap, and push: the
        plunger stands like a wall --- water refuses to shrink by
        even a hair;
  \item release each plunger: the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} springs back; the water
        never moved.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{proposition}[Gases squeeze; liquids and solids do not]\label{prop:g7:states-of-matter:squeeze}
A \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} can be \emph{compressed}\index{compressible} --- forced into
less room --- because most of a \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} is empty space between flying
\omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules}. \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{Liquids} and \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solids} are practically
\emph{incompressible}: their \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} already touch, and matter
that touches cannot be packed tighter by any push a hand ---
or a hydraulic press --- can give.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Squeezed air at work]\label{ex:g7:states-of-matter:squeezed}
The bicycle pump squeezes room-fulls of \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} into the tire ---
possible only because \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} is mostly emptiness. The squeezed
crowd, \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} hammering the tire walls faster and thicker,
holds the bicycle up: a \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas}'s push \emph{is} its molecular
hailstorm. Divers' tanks pack an afternoon of breathing into a
steel bottle; the incompressible brake fluid, meanwhile, carries
your foot's push to the wheels undiminished --- each state hired
for its own talent.
\end{example}

\section{Changes of state, reread}

\begin{example}[The doors, molecule by molecule]\label{ex:g7:states-of-matter:doors}
Warm a \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid} and its trembling \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} shake harder; at the
\omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{melting point} the shaking bursts the orderly stack --- the crowd
begins to slide: \omterm{def:g6:water-states:changes}{melting}. Warm the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} further and the fastest
\omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} tear free of the crowd's grip entirely and fly:
evaporation --- and at the \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:boiling-point}{boiling point} the escape becomes a
stampede from within the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} itself. Cooling runs the film
backward: flyers caught (\omterm{def:g6:water-states:changes}{condensation}), sliders locking into
ranks (\omterm{def:g6:water-states:changes}{solidification}). The six doors of last year are one story
of molecular grip against molecular shaking.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Old laws for free]\label{ex:g7:states-of-matter:free}
Two hard-won laws now come free of charge. \emph{\omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{Mass} survives
changes of state}: \omterm{def:g6:water-states:changes}{melting} rearranges \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} but neither
creates nor destroys a single one --- same particles, same \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass}.
\emph{\omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{Temperature} plateaus}: at the \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{melting point}, incoming
warmth is spent breaking the stack's grip, \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecule} by \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecule},
not on faster shaking --- the \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} waits until the last
rank is broken. What measurement discovered, the model explains.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Water's odd swelling]\label{rem:g7:states-of-matter:iceswell}
Even water's strange freeze-swelling joins in: \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{water-molecules}
happen to lock into an unusually \emph{airy} stack --- an open
lattice with more empty space than the sliding crowd had. Airier
stack, more room, lower \omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density}: ice \omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{floats} on its own \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}.
Most substances stack tighter than they slide; water's
architecture is the beautiful exception.
\end{remark}

\section{Heat, seen from inside}

\begin{proposition}[Temperature and molecular motion]\label{prop:g7:states-of-matter:temperature}
\omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{Temperature} \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{measures} the liveliness of molecular motion: the
hotter a substance, the faster its \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} tremble, slide or
fly. \omterm{def:g5:heat-and-insulation:heat}{Heat} flowing from hot to cold is the lively crowd calming
down as it stirs up the sluggish one --- collision by collision
--- until both share one pace: one \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature}. The old downhill
law of \omterm{def:g5:heat-and-insulation:heat}{heat} is molecular table tennis.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Cold is not a stuff]\label{ex:g7:states-of-matter:cold}
The model retires an old temptation for good: nothing called
``cold'' ever flows. There is only slower and faster molecular
motion; the lively lends liveliness, never the reverse. At the
coldest imaginable --- \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} as still as nature permits ---
lies a true floor of \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature}, far below any freezer; its
strange and famous number belongs to the High School volume.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:1}
State the particle model's picture of each of the three states,
in one line each.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:2}
Why does a \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid} keep its shape while a \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} takes its
container's? Answer with \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules}, not with rules.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:3}
In the syringe test, why does the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} yield and spring back,
while the water stands like a wall?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:4}
A bicycle pump packs many room-fulls of \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} into one tire. What
does this prove about the spacing of \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules}? Could a pump
do the same with water?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:5}
Tell the story of \omterm{def:g6:water-states:changes}{melting} in molecular language: what does warmth
do, and what breaks at the \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{melting point}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:6}
Why does \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} survive every change of state, according to the
model? What would have to happen to \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} for \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} to be
lost?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:7}
What does \omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{temperature} \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{measure}, in the model? Describe \omterm{def:g5:heat-and-insulation:heat}{heat}'s
downhill flow as molecular table tennis.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:8}
Perfume opened at one end of a still room is smelled at the other
end a minute later. Explain the journey --- and why a breeze
\omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speeds} it up.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:9}
Evaporation cools what it leaves behind: sweat chills your skin,
and a wet finger held up feels the \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:wind}{wind}'s cold side. Explain with
the escape of the \emph{fastest} \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} --- what kind of crowd
stays behind?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:10}
A sealed balloon left in the summer sun swells; in the
refrigerator it sags. The number of \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} inside never
changed. Explain both changes with molecular \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed} and the
hailstorm on the walls.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:11}
Why does ice \omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{float}, in molecular language? What is unusual about
the stack \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{water-molecules} build, and what would ponds do in
winter if water stacked like ordinary substances?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g7:states-of-matter:12}
A drop of ink released gently at the bottom of a glass of
\emph{still} water slowly spreads, over \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours}, until all the
water is faintly blue --- no stirring, no currents. Explain what
this patient spreading reveals about the \omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} of a \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}
--- both the water's and the ink's --- and why the same drop
spreads faster in warm water. (This humble observation was among
the great early evidences for the whole molecular idea.)
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Molecular Theater}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the class stages the particle
model in the gymnasium; pupils as molecules, states as
choreography; the critics' questions}]\label{pb:g7:states-of-matter:1}
The physics class stages matter itself: thirty pupils play the
\omterm{def:g7:states-of-matter:molecule}{molecules} of one substance, and the gymnasium is the container.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Three scenes.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Scene one, the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid}: how should the thirty stand, and
        what small motion must each keep making? What may none of
        them do?
  \item Scene two, the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}: what changes in the arrangement,
        and what new freedom appears? What must the pupils still
        keep doing that the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} scene will abolish?
  \item Scene three, the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas}: describe the choreography --- and
        say why this scene needs the \emph{whole} gymnasium while
        the first two huddle in a corner.
  \item The audience asks: ``Is it the same thirty pupils in all
        three scenes?'' What law of last year does the answer
        stage?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The critics test the play.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A critic pushes gently on the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} scene's crowd, herding
        it into half the gym. Does the scene allow it? And the
        same push on the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} scene's huddle? Name the
        proposition being staged.
  \item To stage \emph{\omterm{def:g6:water-states:changes}{melting}}, the director turns up an
        imaginary \omterm{def:g5:heat-and-insulation:heat}{heat} dial. What must the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid}'s pupils do
        more and more of, and at what moment does the scene
        legally become the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} scene?
  \item The play must show the \omterm{def:g6:water-states:changes}{melting} plateau: while the change
        happens, what is the \omterm{def:g5:heat-and-insulation:heat}{heat} dial's \omterm{def:g5:everyday-energy:energy}{energy} spent on --- and
        what must the ``\omterm{def:g3:temperature-thermometers:temperature}{thermometer} critic'' report about the
        pupils' average liveliness during it?
  \item Stage evaporation from the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} scene: which pupils
        leave the huddle --- any at random, or a particular
        kind? What happens to the huddle's average liveliness as
        they go, and what everyday chill does this explain?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Special effects.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The balloon effect: the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:gas}{gas} scene is fenced by pupils
        holding a rope-ring (the balloon's skin). What do the
        molecular actors do to the rope, and what happens to the
        ring when the actors move faster?
  \item Water's odd freezing must be staged. What unusual
        instruction about \emph{spacing} does the director give
        the \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid} scene's stack, and what floating fact does it
        explain?
  \item The ink-drop finale: one pupil in a blue shirt starts in
        a corner of the \emph{\omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}} scene. Without any
        director's push, how does the blue shirt end up far from
        its corner --- and why faster if the \omterm{def:g5:heat-and-insulation:heat}{heat} dial is up?
  \item Write the program note: in three sentences, say what one
        idea the whole play stages, name two old laws it explains
        for free, and admit honestly what no scene can show
        (how \emph{small} and how \emph{many} the real actors
        are).
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
