\begin{solution}{exo:b1:potential-capacitors:4}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:potential-capacitors:5}
Every element of the ring is at distance $\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}$: $V =
Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}$; $E_z = -\dd V/\dd z = Qz/4\pi\varepsilon_0(R^2 +
z^2)^{3/2}$. Disk: $\dd q = 2\pi\sigma a\,\dd a$ at $\sqrt{a^2 + z^2}$: $V =
\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\displaystyle\int_0^R\dfrac{a\,\dd a}{\sqrt{a^2 + z^2}} =
\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\bigl(\sqrt{z^2 + R^2} - z\bigr)$, and $-\dd V/\dd z =
\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(1 - \dfrac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}}\Bigr)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:potential-capacitors:6}
$V(r) = V(R) + \displaystyle\int_r^R\dfrac{Qr'\,\dd r'}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3} =
\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R} + \dfrac{Q(R^2 - r^2)}{8\pi\varepsilon_0R^3} = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}
\dfrac{3R^2 - r^2}{2R^3}$. Gold: $V(0) = 1.5 \times 8.99 \times 10^9 \times 1.26 \times
10^{-17}/7 \times 10^{-15} = \qty{24}{MV}$: a proton needs \qty{24}{MeV}, an alpha
($2e$) \qty{48}{MeV} to reach the centre and \qty{32}{MeV} to touch the
surface. Rutherford's \qty{5}{MeV} alphas turn back at $r$ where $2eV =
\qty{5}{MeV}$: $V = \qty{2.5}{MV}$, $r = \qty{45}{fm}$ --- far outside the
nucleus, which is why his point-charge analysis worked.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:potential-capacitors:7}
Axis: $2p/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3 = 2 \times 8.99 \times 10^9 \times 6.2 \times 10^{-30}/10^{-27} =
\qty{1.1e8}{V/m}$; perpendicular, half: \qty{5.6e7}{V/m}. \omterm{def:b1:angular-momentum:moment}{Torque} $pE =
\qty{6.2e-24}{N.m}$; energy gap $2pE = \qty{1.2e-23}{J}$ against $k_BT =
\qty{4.1e-21}{J}$: $330$ times smaller --- only a $0.1\%$ net alignment.
Sodium ion at \qty{0.3}{nm}: $E = e/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2 = \qty{1.6e10}{V/m}$, $-pE
= \qty{-1.0e-19}{J} = \qty{-0.6}{eV}$, $24\,k_BT$: the water molecules lock
onto the ion --- hydration.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:potential-capacitors:8}
$Q = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2E = 10^{-4} \times 3 \times 10^6/8.99 \times 10^9 = \qty{33}{nC}$,
$V = ER = \qty{30}{kV}$. Joined spheres: $E_1 = V/R_1 = \qty{0.2}{MV/m}$,
$E_2 = V/R_2 = \qty{20}{MV/m} > \qty{3}{MV/m}$: the small sphere ionizes the
air and leaks the charge --- the \omterm{ex:b1:potential-capacitors:point}{point effect}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:potential-capacitors:9}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:potential-capacitors:10}
(a) $E = Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$, $U = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\dfrac1{R_1} -
\dfrac1{R_2}\Bigr)$, $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R_1R_2/(R_2 - R_1)$; with $R_2 - R_1 = d \ll
R_1$, $R_1R_2 \approx R^2$ and $C \approx 4\pi R^2\varepsilon_0/d = \varepsilon_0S/d$; with $R_2 \to
\infty$, $C \to 4\pi\varepsilon_0R_1$. (b) $C/L = 2\pi\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0/\ln(b/a) = 2\pi \times 2.3
\times 8.85 \times 10^{-12}/\ln5 = \qty{79}{pF/m}$, the right order. (c) $E(a) =
U/[a\ln(b/a)] = 1000/(5 \times 10^{-4} \times 1.61) = \qty{1.2}{MV/m}$: below the
\qty{20}{MV/m} of polyethylene, safe; in air it would be marginal.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:potential-capacitors:11}
(a) $W = Q^2/2C = Q^2/8\pi\varepsilon_0R$. (b) $R = e^2/8\pi\varepsilon_0m_ec^2 = 2.31 \times
10^{-28}/(2 \times 8.19 \times 10^{-14}) = \qty{1.4e-15}{m}$ (the usual ``classical
radius'' $e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0m_ec^2 = \qty{2.8}{fm}$ drops the $\tfrac12$). (c) $W =
Q^2/2C = Q^2d/2\varepsilon_0S$ doubles: $\Delta W = Q^2d/2\varepsilon_0S = F\,d$ with $F =
Q^2/2\varepsilon_0S = \sigma^2S/2\varepsilon_0$, as found directly. (d) At fixed $U$, $W =
\varepsilon_0SU^2/2d$ halves: $\Delta W = -W/2$; the puller still works $W/2$
(integrating $F = \varepsilon_0SU^2/2x^2$ from $d$ to $2d$); both halves, $W$ in
all, flow back into the battery as the charge $CU/2$ returns to it at
\omterm{def:b1:dc-circuits:voltage}{voltage} $U$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b1:potential-capacitors:12}
(a) The sphere of radius $r$ carries $q = Qr^3/R^3$ at surface potential
$q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$; adding the shell $\dd q = 3Qr^2\,\dd r/R^3$ costs $\dd W =
q\,\dd q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r = 3Q^2r^4\,\dd r/4\pi\varepsilon_0R^6$; integrate: $W =
3Q^2/20\pi\varepsilon_0R$. (b) $Q = 92e = \qty{1.47e-17}{C}$: $W = 3 \times 2.17 \times
10^{-34}/(20\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 7.4 \times 10^{-15}) = \qty{1.6e-10}{J}
= \qty{990}{MeV}$. (c) Each fragment: charge $Q/2$, radius $R/2^{1/3}$,
energy $\tfrac14 2^{1/3}W$; two of them: $2^{1/3}W/2 = 0.63W$: released
$0.37 \times 990 = \qty{370}{MeV}$. (d) The nuclear ``surface tension'':
two fragments have more surface than one nucleus, which costs about
\qty{170}{MeV}; the balance, near \qty{200}{MeV}, appears mostly as the
\omterm{thm:b1:work-and-energy:ke}{kinetic energy} of the two fragments flying apart under their mutual
repulsion.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b1:potential-capacitors:1}
\textbf{1.} Weight $\tfrac43\pi r^3\rho g$ down, drag $6\pi\eta rv$ up,
buoyancy $\tfrac43\pi r^3\rho_{\text{air}}g$ up --- $1.2/900 = 0.13\%$ of the
weight, negligible. Terminal: $\tfrac43\pi r^3\rho g = 6\pi\eta rv_1$, $v_1 =
2\rho gr^2/9\eta$.

\textbf{2.} $v_1 = \qty{1.0e-4}{m/s}$: $r = \sqrt{9\eta v_1/2\rho g} = \sqrt{9 \times
1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times 10^{-4}/(2 \times 900 \times 9.8)} = \qty{9.6e-7}{m}$, a
micrometre; $m = \tfrac43\pi r^3\rho = \qty{3.3e-15}{kg}$.

\textbf{3.} $\tau = m/6\pi\eta r = 3.3 \times 10^{-15}/3.3 \times 10^{-10} = \qty{1e-5}{s}$;
distance $\sim v_1\tau = \qty{1}{nm}$. Never.

\textbf{4.} $\mathrm{Re} = 1.2 \times 10^{-4} \times 9.6 \times 10^{-7}/1.8 \times 10^{-5} =
6 \times 10^{-6} \ll 1$: Stokes holds.

\textbf{5.} $eE = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 3 \times 10^5 = \qty{4.8e-14}{N}$ against
$mg = \qty{3.2e-14}{N}$: comparable only for micron drops. A \qty{10}{\micro m}
drop weighs a thousand times more; one \omterm{def:b1:electrostatics-gauss:charge}{elementary charge} would change
its speed by $0.1\%$, invisible.

\textbf{6.} Brownian motion: the bombardment by air molecules jostles
the drop randomly; the timings scatter, limiting the precision and
setting a lower bound on useful drop size.

\textbf{7.} $E = U/d = 5000/0.016 = \qty{3.1e5}{V/m}$; plates much wider than
$d$, so the two-plane superposition gives a uniform field; the
equipotentials are horizontal planes, $V = Uz/d$.

\textbf{8.} $C = \varepsilon_0\pi(0.1)^2/0.016 = \qty{17}{pF}$; $Q = CU = \qty{87}{nC}$;
$W = \tfrac12CU^2 = \qty{0.22}{mJ}$.

\textbf{9.} Upper plate positive: $\vect E$ points down; the force must
point up, so $q < 0$. $|q| = mgd/U_0 = 3.3 \times 10^{-15} \times 9.8 \times 0.016/
1080 = \qty{4.8e-19}{C} = 3e$: $q = -3e$.

\textbf{10.} Rising at constant speed: $|q|E = mg + 6\pi\eta rv_2$ and $mg =
6\pi\eta rv_1$, so $|q|E = 6\pi\eta r(v_1 + v_2)$ and $|q| = 6\pi\eta r(v_1 +
v_2)d/U$. $v_2 = (|q|E - mg)/6\pi\eta r = (1.5 \times 10^{-13} - 3.2 \times 10^{-14})/
3.3 \times 10^{-10} = \qty{3.6e-4}{m/s}$: \qty{1}{mm} in \qty{2.8}{s}.

\textbf{11.} Balance is a null judgment on a drifting, jittering speck
whose charge may change meanwhile; timings over a fixed distance can be
repeated at will, $r$ is fixed once for all by $v_1$, and one drop then
yields a whole series of charges.

\textbf{12.} $|q|U = 3 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 5000 = \qty{2.4e-15}{J}$
(\qty{15}{keV}) across the gap; drag over \qty{1}{cm}: $(|q|E - mg) \times 0.01 =
\qty{1.2e-15}{J}$, turned into heat in the air.

\textbf{13.} Divide by $1.60$: $3.00$, $5.01$, $4.01$, $1.99$, $6.02$ ---
integers $3, 5, 4, 2, 6$.

\textbf{14.} $q/n$: $1.600, 1.602, 1.605, 1.595, 1.605$; mean $1.601$,
standard deviation $0.004$, uncertainty of the mean $0.004/\sqrt5 =
0.002$: $e = (1.601 \pm 0.002) \times 10^{-19}$~C.

\textbf{15.} The X-rays ionize the air; the drop picks up ions one at a
time, each carrying a whole number of $e$.

\textbf{16.} $r \propto \eta^{1/2}$ from $v_1$, $m \propto r^3 \propto \eta^{3/2}$, and
$|q| = mgd/U_0 \propto \eta^{3/2}$: $1\%$ on $\eta$ gives $1.5\%$ on $e$. Millikan's
$\eta$ was $0.4\%$ low, hence his $e$ $0.6\%$ low.

\textbf{17.} Quarks never come alone: they are confined inside hadrons,
and every free object carries an integer charge.

\textbf{18.} $N_A = F/e = 96485/1.602 \times 10^{-19} = \qty{6.02e23}{mol^{-1}}$.

\textbf{19.} $m_e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}/1.76 \times 10^{11} = \qty{9.1e-31}{kg}$: $1840$
times lighter than the hydrogen atom.

\textbf{20.} Horizontal planes between the plates, bulging round the
rims; vertical \omterm{def:b1:electrostatics-gauss:field}{field lines} fanning outward at the edges over a width of
order $d = \qty{1.6}{cm}$ --- small against the \qty{20}{cm} diameter, and
the drop is watched at the centre.

\textbf{21.} $E_p = qUz/d + mgz$, linear in $z$: no minimum; a stationary
drop requires the slope to vanish, $q = -mgd/U$, and the equilibrium is
then indifferent --- any residual drift persists, which is why the
balance is hard to judge.

\textbf{22.} $2.4 \times 10^{-15}$ against $2.2 \times 10^{-4}$~J: $10^{-11}$. The drop
does not disturb the field.

\textbf{23.} Field up, force on the negative drop down: it falls at $(mg
+ |q|E)/6\pi\eta r = (3.2 \times 10^{-14} + 1.5 \times 10^{-13})/3.3 \times 10^{-10} =
\qty{5.6e-4}{m/s}$.

\textbf{24.} $F = 10^4 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 3.1 \times 10^5 = \qty{5e-10}{N}$
against $\qty{9.8e-9}{N}$: $5\%$ of the weight --- not liftable, and with
$10^4$ charges a change of one $e$ is invisible. Oil mist gives drops
that are small, non-evaporating and spherical.

\textbf{25.} Measured: $v_1$, $v_2$ and $U$ (with $d$, $\eta$, $\rho$ known);
deduced $r$, $m$, then $q$, found to be always an integer multiple of
$e = (1.601 \pm 0.002) \times 10^{-19}$~C ($0.1\%$); from it $N_A = F/e$ and
$m_e = e/(e/m_e)$.
\end{solution}
