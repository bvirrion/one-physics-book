\chapter{Fluid Statics}\label{ch:b1:fluid-statics}

A submarine at three hundred meters carries, on each square meter of
its hull, the weight of a loaded truck. A dam holds back a lake with a
wall that is thick at the bottom and thin at the top, for a reason
that has nothing to do with how long the lake is. A ship of a hundred
thousand tonnes floats because it pushes aside a hundred thousand tonnes
of water, and sinks a hand's breadth deeper when it sails from the sea
into a river. Every one of these is a consequence of a single relation
--- pressure increases downward in a fluid at rest at the rate $\rho g$ ---
and this chapter derives it, applies it to liquids and to the atmosphere,
and deduces from it the force on a wall and the law of Archimedes.

\section{Pressure in a fluid at rest}

\begin{proposition}[Isotropy of pressure]\label{prop:b1:fluid-statics:isotropy}
\index{pressure!in a fluid}
In a fluid at rest, the force exerted by the fluid on a small surface
$\dd S$ placed at a point $M$ is $\dd\vect F = P(M)\,\dd S\,\vect n$, normal to
the surface and directed toward it, with a pressure $P(M)$ that depends
on the point but \emph{not} on the orientation of the surface.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Partial proof]
A fluid at rest transmits no tangential force (otherwise it would flow):
the force is normal. Consider a tiny triangular prism of fluid at $M$
with faces of different orientations: it is in equilibrium under the
pressure forces on its faces and its weight; the weight scales as the
volume (third power of the size), the pressure forces as areas (second
power), so for a small enough prism only the pressure forces count, and
their balance in every direction requires the same $P$ on every face.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental relation of fluid statics]\label{thm:b1:fluid-statics:fundamental}
\index{hydrostatic relation}
In a fluid at rest in the uniform gravity $\vect g = -g\vect e_z$ ($z$
upward), the pressure depends only on $z$ and
\[
\frac{\dd P}{\dd z} = -\rho g ,
\]
$\rho$ being the fluid's density at that level. Pressure increases
downward; surfaces of equal pressure (\emph{isobars}) are horizontal,
and so is the free surface of a liquid.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Take a horizontal slab of fluid of area $S$ between $z$ and $z + \dd z$:
it is pushed up by $P(z)S$ from below, down by $P(z + \dd z)S$ from above,
and pulled down by its weight $\rho gS\,\dd z$. At rest:
$P(z)S - P(z + \dd z)S - \rho gS\,\dd z = 0$. Horizontal slabs of fluid
have no horizontal force from gravity, so horizontally $P$ is uniform.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Incompressible fluid]\label{cor:b1:fluid-statics:incompressible}
\index{hydrostatic pressure}
In a liquid of uniform density $\rho$, the pressure at depth $h$ below a
point where it is $P_0$ is
\[
P = P_0 + \rho gh .
\]
Water: \qty{1}{bar} more every \qty{10}{m}. Two points of a connected
liquid at the same level are at the same pressure; the free surfaces of
communicating vessels stand at one level; a pressure applied anywhere
to an enclosed liquid is transmitted undiminished to every point
(Pascal's principle).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Integrate $\dd P/\dd z = -\rho g$ with $\rho$ constant; the rest is the
horizontality of isobars and the uniqueness of $P$ at a level.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Manometers and presses]\label{ex:b1:fluid-statics:manometer}
\index{manometer}\index{hydraulic press}
A U-tube of mercury ($\rho = \qty{13600}{kg/m^3}$) whose columns differ by
$h = \qty{760}{mm}$ measures a pressure difference $\rho gh = \qty{1.013e5}{Pa}$
--- the atmosphere, which holds a \qty{10.3}{m} column of water. A
\omterm{ex:b1:fluid-statics:manometer}{hydraulic press}: a force $F_1$ on a piston of area $S_1$ raises the
pressure by $F_1/S_1$ throughout; on the large piston $S_2$ it yields
$F_2 = F_1S_2/S_1$ --- fifty times more for fifty times the area, at the
price of fifty times less travel (energy is conserved: $F_1d_1 = F_2d_2$).
\end{example}

\section{The atmosphere}

\begin{proposition}[Isothermal atmosphere]\label{prop:b1:fluid-statics:atmosphere}
\index{barometric formula}\index{scale height}
For a \omterm{def:b1:kinetic-theory:model}{perfect gas} of molar mass $M$ at uniform temperature $T$ in uniform
gravity,
\[
P(z) = P_0\,\eu^{-z/H}, \qquad H = \frac{RT}{Mg} ,
\]
the \emph{\omterm{prop:b1:fluid-statics:atmosphere}{scale height}}: about \qty{8}{km} for air; the pressure halves
every $H\ln2 \approx \qty{5.5}{km}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\rho = PM/RT$ (equation of state), so $\dd P/\dd z = -(Mg/RT)P$: a
first-order linear equation, $P = P_0\eu^{-Mgz/RT}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Thin air]\label{ex:b1:fluid-statics:altitude}
With $T = \qty{260}{K}$ (an average over the lower atmosphere), $H =
\qty{7.6}{km}$: $P = 0.52P_0$ at \qty{5000}{m}, $0.31P_0$ at the summit of
Everest --- close to the measured $0.54$ and $0.33$; the real atmosphere
cools with altitude, which the Year 2 volume takes into account. The
model also tells why the sea is different: water is eight hundred times
denser and nearly incompressible, so its pressure rises linearly, a bar
per ten meters, with no \omterm{prop:b1:fluid-statics:atmosphere}{scale height}.
\end{example}

\section{Forces on walls}

\begin{proposition}[Force on a vertical wall]\label{prop:b1:fluid-statics:wall}
\index{center of pressure}
A liquid of depth $h$ against a vertical rectangular wall of width $L$
(atmospheric pressure acting on both faces and cancelling) exerts the
horizontal force
\[
F = \tfrac12\rho gh^2L ,
\]
equivalent to a single force applied at the \emph{\omterm{prop:b1:fluid-statics:wall}{center of pressure}},
at depth $2h/3$ --- the resultant acts low, where the pressure is
greatest.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At depth $x$ the gauge pressure is $\rho gx$; on the strip of height
$\dd x$ the force is $\rho gxL\,\dd x$; integrate from $0$ to $h$. Its
moment about the surface line is $\int_0^h\rho gx^2L\,\dd x = \tfrac13\rho gh^3L$;
dividing by $F$ gives the \omterm{def:b1:angular-momentum:moment}{lever arm} $2h/3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A dam]\label{ex:b1:fluid-statics:dam}
$h = \qty{20}{m}$, $L = \qty{100}{m}$: $F = \tfrac12 \times 1000 \times 9.81 \times
400 \times 100 = \qty{2.0e8}{N}$ --- twenty thousand tonnes, applied
\qty{13}{m} below the surface, the same whether the lake behind is a pond
or a fjord: the \emph{hydrostatic paradox}.
\end{example}

\section{Archimedes' theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Archimedes]\label{thm:b1:fluid-statics:archimedes}
\index{Archimedes' theorem}\index{buoyancy}\index{center of buoyancy}
A body immersed in a fluid at rest receives from the pressure forces a
resultant --- the \emph{buoyancy} --- equal and opposite to the weight of
the fluid it displaces,
\[
\vect\Pi = -\rho_{\text{fluid}}V_{\text{immersed}}\,\vect g ,
\]
applied at the \omterm{def:b1:systems-of-points:cm}{center of mass} of the displaced fluid (the \emph{\omterm{thm:b1:fluid-statics:archimedes}{center of
buoyancy}}).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The pressure forces on the body's surface depend only on the shape of
that surface and on the fluid outside. Replace the body, in thought, by
fluid at rest filling the same volume: that fluid is in equilibrium
under its weight and the same pressure forces, so the pressure forces
balance its weight --- they sum to $-\rho V\vect g$, applied through its
\omterm{def:b1:systems-of-points:cm}{center of mass}. Remove the thought experiment; the pressure forces are
unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Floating]\label{cor:b1:fluid-statics:floating}
\index{floating}
A body of density $\rho_b$ floats in a fluid of density $\rho_f > \rho_b$
with the fraction $\rho_b/\rho_f$ of its volume immersed; if $\rho_b >
\rho_f$ it sinks, with an apparent weight $(\rho_b - \rho_f)Vg$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Weight $\rho_bVg$ equals buoyancy $\rho_fV_{\text{imm}}g$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Three buoyancies]\label{ex:b1:fluid-statics:buoy}
A hydrometer is a weighted tube that sinks until it displaces its own
weight: the denser the liquid, the less it sinks --- a density gauge read
on its stem. A \qty{2800}{m^3} balloon of hot air at \qty{373}{K} displaces
\qty{3.4}{t} of cold air and weighs \qty{2.6}{t} of hot air: \qty{0.7}{t}
of lift (\cref{pb:b1:kinetic-theory:1}). A ship of \qty{100000}{t}
displaces \qty{97600}{m^3} of sea water; in fresh water ($\qty{1000}{kg/m^3}$)
it must displace $2.5\%$ more and sinks deeper --- the Plimsoll marks on
its hull.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Stability]\label{rem:b1:fluid-statics:stability}
\index{metacenter}
A floating body tilted by a small angle is restored if the buoyancy,
now applied at the shifted center of the new immersed volume, produces
a righting moment --- which happens when the \emph{metacenter} (the point
where the buoyancy's line of action meets the body's axis) lies above
$G$. Ballast low in the hull lowers $G$ and steadies the ship; a
top-heavy boat capsizes.
\end{remark}

\section{Problem: The submarine}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a steel hull three hundred meters
down: the pressure on its plates, the water it must swallow to sink and
spit out to rise, the diver who leaves it, and the tanker that passes
overhead}]
\label{pb:b1:fluid-statics:1}
Sea water: $\rho = \qty{1025}{kg/m^3}$; fresh water \qty{1000}{kg/m^3};
$P_0 = \qty{1.013e5}{Pa}$; $g = \qty{9.81}{m/s^2}$. The submarine's hull
encloses $V = \qty{3000}{m^3}$; with empty ballast tanks its mass is
$m_0 = \qty{2800}{t}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Pressure on the hull.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Absolute pressure at \qty{300}{m}, in \omterm{def:b1:kinetic-theory:pressure}{pascals} and in bars.
  \item Force on a circular hatch of diameter \qty{0.80}{m} at that depth;
        compare with the weight of a truck.
  \item Force on one square meter of hull at the keel if the hull is
        \qty{10}{m} tall and the top is at \qty{300}{m}: how different from
        the top?
  \item Water is slightly compressible ($\chi_T = \qty{4.6e-10}{Pa^{-1}}$):
        by what fraction is sea water denser at \qty{300}{m} than at the
        surface? Does it matter for the pressure computed above?
  \item The crew inside breathes air at \qty{1}{atm}: why does the hull
        need to be a thick cylinder, and why does a cylinder resist
        better than a flat box?
  \item A thin cylindrical shell of radius $R$ and wall thickness $e$
        under an external overpressure $\Delta P$ carries a compressive
        stress $\Delta P\,R/e$ in its wall. For $R = \qty{5.0}{m}$ and
        $e = \qty{3.0}{cm}$ at \qty{300}{m}, compute it and compare with the
        yield stress of the steel, about \qty{600}{MPa}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Sinking and floating.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item At the surface with empty tanks, what volume of the hull emerges?
  \item What mass of sea water must the ballast tanks take in for the
        submarine to hover (neutral buoyancy)?
  \item Once neutrally buoyant at \qty{300}{m}, the hull is compressed by
        $0.1\%$ by the pressure. Compute the resulting net force and say
        whether the equilibrium is stable with respect to depth.
  \item The boat sails neutrally buoyant from the sea into a fresh-water
        estuary. Net force? Which way does it go, and what must the crew
        do?
  \item A \qty{20}{t} torpedo is fired. What must the ballast system do
        immediately, and by how much?
  \item Why does a submarine use compressed air to empty its tanks, and
        why is the air's pressure the limit on how deep it can blow them?
  \item Estimate the volume of air at \qty{1}{atm} needed to empty
        \qty{275}{m^3} of tanks at \qty{300}{m} (Boyle's law, $PV$
        constant).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The diver.}
A diver leaves the surface with \qty{6.0}{L} of air in her lungs.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Absolute pressure at \qty{10}{m} and \qty{30}{m}.
  \item If she descends holding her breath, what is her lung volume at
        \qty{30}{m}?
  \item A scuba diver breathes air at the ambient pressure at \qty{30}{m}
        and ascends while holding her breath: volume her lungs would
        need at the surface, and the lesson.
  \item A snorkel \qty{1.0}{m} long: pressure difference between the
        water on the diver's chest and the air in her lungs; force on a
        chest of \qty{0.05}{m^2}; can she inhale?
  \item The air tank holds \qty{12}{L} at \qty{200}{bar}: how many liters
        of air at \qty{30}{m} does that make, and for how long at
        \qty{20}{L} per minute?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The tanker overhead.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A tanker of \qty{100000}{t} floats in sea water: volume of water
