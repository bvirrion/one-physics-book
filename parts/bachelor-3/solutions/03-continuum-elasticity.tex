\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:b3:continuum-elasticity} --- Continuum Mechanics and Elasticity}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:1}
(a) $\unit{Pa} = \unit{N/m^2} = \unit{kg.m^{-1}.s^{-2}}$; strain is a
pure number. (b) $\varepsilon = \sigma/E = \num{e-3}$. (c) Doubling
the length is $\varepsilon = 1$: a thousand times beyond ``small'',
and rubber's response there is strongly nonlinear (and of a different,
entropic origin) --- \omterm{thm:b3:continuum-elasticity:hooke}{Hooke's law} only opens the curve. (d) $w =
\sigma^2/2E$: steel $(\num{2e8})^2/2(\num{2e11}) =
\qty{1e5}{J/m^3}$; rubber $(\num{e6})^2/2(\num{2e6}) =
\qty{2.5e5}{J/m^3}$ --- the soft material stores \emph{more} per unit
volume at working stress, which is why slingshots are rubber, not
steel.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:2}
(a) $\varepsilon_{ij} = \alpha\delta_{ij}$: isotropic dilation,
$\delta V/V = 3\alpha$. (b) $\varepsilon_{xy} = \varepsilon_{yx} =
\gamma/2$, rest zero: pure shear, volume unchanged. (c)
$\varepsilon_{ij} = 0$: rigid rotation, no deformation. (d)
$\operatorname{diag}(\varepsilon, -\nu\varepsilon, -\nu\varepsilon)$:
the \omterm{def:b3:continuum-elasticity:stress}{traction} test --- stretch along $x$, Poisson contraction across.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:3}
(a) With only $\sigma_{zz}(z)$ and weight: $\partial_z\sigma_{zz} =
\rho g$ (taking compression positive downward), $\sigma_{zz} = \rho
gh$ at the base. (b) $h_{\max} = \sigma_{\text{c}}/\rho g =
\num{2e8}/(2700 \times 9.81) \approx \qty{7.6}{km}$. (c) Everest is at
the crushing limit of its own base --- Earth's mountains are as tall
as rock strength allows, and no taller. (d) A cone of height $h$ loads
its base with only $\rho gh/3$ (a third of the column: the mass grows
with the section), so a mountain-shaped pile can stand about three
times taller than a column.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:4}
(a) $-\nu\varepsilon = \num{-3e-4}$. (b) $\delta V/V = (1 -
2\nu)\varepsilon = \num{4e-4}$. (c) $\nu = 1/2$: incompressible
deformation --- rubber. (d) For $\nu > 1/2$ the bulk modulus $K =
E/3(1 - 2\nu)$ would be negative: squeezing from all sides would
\emph{grow} the volume, and the material would release energy by
collapsing --- no stable solid can do it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:5}
(a) On a half-cylinder of unit length, the pressure pushes with $p
\times 2R$ (projected area) and the two cut walls pull back with
$2\sigma_\theta t$: $\sigma_\theta = pR/t$. (b) On a cross-section,
$p\pi R^2 = \sigma_z\,2\pi Rt$: $\sigma_z = pR/2t$ --- half. A
sausage splits \emph{lengthwise}: the bigger hoop stress tears the
skin along the axis. (c) $\sigma_\theta = \num{2e7} \times
0.09/0.005 = \qty{360}{MPa}$: a factor $2$ below yield --- which is
why cylinders are proof-tested and inspected. (d) A sphere carries
$pR/2t$ in every direction: hemispherical ends halve the stress and
avoid the corners where flat ends would concentrate it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:6}
(a) $G = E/2(1 + \nu) = \qty{1.0}{MPa}$. (b) $\sigma_{xy} = F/S =
150/\num{e-2} = \qty{15}{kPa}$; $\gamma = \sigma_{xy}/G = 0.015$;
displacement $\gamma h = \qty{0.3}{mm}$. (c) A rubber network changes
\emph{shape} by mere reorientation of its chains (easy), but changing
\emph{volume} means packing the chains closer, as hard as compressing
a liquid: $G \ll K$, hence $\nu \to 1/2$. (d) Softness in shear: the
mount lets the ground shake horizontally underneath while transmitting
little force, yet remains stiff enough in compression to hold the
building's weight.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:7}
(a) $k = ES/L = \num{1.2e9} \times \num{8e-5}/20 = \qty{4.8}{kN/m}$.
(b) $mg(h + x) = \tfrac12 kx^2$: $2400x^2 - 785x - 3140 = 0$, $x =
\qty{1.3}{m}$. (c) $F = kx \approx \qty{6.3}{kN}$; deceleration $F/m
\approx \qty{79}{m/s^2} \approx 8g$ --- the reason ropes are made
deliberately stretchy. (d) Part of the absorbed energy went into
breaking fibres and plastic rearrangement: the rope now sits on a
degraded stress--strain curve, stiffer and weaker, and the next fall
would be harder in both senses.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:8}
(a) The top of a tube of radius $r$ turns by the arc $r\theta$ over
the length $L$: $\gamma = r\theta/L$. (b) $\Gamma = \int_0^a r\,(G r\theta/L)\,
2\pi r\,\dd r = (\pi Ga^4/2L)\,\theta$. (c) By $2^4 = 16$. (d) $C =
\pi \times \num{4e10} \times (\num{2.5e-5})^4/2 =
\qty{2.5e-8}{N.m/rad}$; $T = 2\pi\sqrt{I/C} = 2\pi\sqrt{\num{5e-4}/
\num{2.5e-8}} \approx \qty{900}{s}$ --- a quarter of an hour per
swing: sensitivity enough to feel the gravity of lead spheres.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:9}
(a) $\mu = E/2(1+\nu) = \qty{78}{GPa}$, $\lambda = E\nu/(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)
= \qty{107}{GPa}$: $c_{\text{P}} = \sqrt{262\times10^9/7850} =
\qty{5.8}{km/s}$, $c_{\text{S}} = \qty{3.1}{km/s}$. (b) The thin rod
gives $\sqrt{E/\rho} = \qty{5.0}{km/s}$: in the rod the sides bulge
freely (Poisson release), softening the response; in the bulk the
surrounding matter forbids it. (c) $c_{\text{S}} = 0$;
$c_{\text{P}} = \sqrt{\num{2.2e9}/1000} = \qty{1.5}{km/s}$ --- the
sound speed of water. (d) P moves the ground along the ray
(near-vertical for a deep source), S across it: the three components
of a seismometer separate the polarisations, and the P/S delay then
dates the distance.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:10}
(a) An arc at radius $R + y$ has length $(R + y)\alpha$ against
$R\alpha$ at the neutral surface: $\varepsilon = y/R$, $\sigma =
Ey/R$. (b) $M = \int y\sigma\,\dd S = (E/R)\int y^2\,\dd S = EI/R$;
for the rectangle $I = bh^3/12$. (c) Flat: $I = bh^3/12$; on edge:
$hb^3/12$ --- ratio $(b/h)^2 = 25$ for $b/h = 5$: same wood, twenty-five
times stiffer, purely by geometry. (d) $I = 0.50 \times
0.035^3/12 = \qty{1.8e-6}{m^4}$, $EI = \qty{2.1e4}{N.m^2}$; $\delta =
FL^3/3EI = 736 \times 1.8^3/(3 \times \num{2.1e4}) \approx
\qty{7}{cm}$: exactly the pleasant give of a diving board.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:11}
(a) In the bowed configuration the axial load $P$ acts with lever arm
$y(x)$ about the section at $x$: $M = -Py$, and $M = EIy''$ gives
$EIy'' = -Py$. (b) $y = A\sin(x\sqrt{P/EI})$ with $y(L) = 0$: nonzero
$A$ first at $\sqrt{P/EI}\,L = \pi$, i.e.\ $P_{\text{c}} =
\pi^2EI/L^2$; below it the straight column is the only solution, above
it bowing costs no force. (c) $I = 0.03 \times 0.0015^3/12 =
\qty{8.4e-12}{m^4}$: $P_{\text{c}} = \pi^2 \times 12\times10^9 \times
\num{8.4e-12}/1^2 \approx \qty{1}{N}$ --- the weight of an apple: a
metre rule buckles under a finger. (d) $I$ grows as material moves
away from the axis: a tube puts all its section at large $y$,
maximising $I$ (hence $P_{\text{c}}$) for a given weight of material
--- the design of bones, bamboo and bicycle frames.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:b3:continuum-elasticity:12}
(a) One chain per area $a^2$; stretching by strain $\varepsilon$
stretches each spring by $\delta = \varepsilon a$, force $\kappa
\varepsilon a$ per chain, stress $\kappa\varepsilon/a$: $E =
\kappa/a$. (b) $\rho = m/a^3$, so $E/\rho = \kappa a^2/m$ and $c =
a\sqrt{\kappa/m}$. (c) $c = \num{2.5e-10}\sqrt{50/\num{e-25}} \approx
\qty{5.6}{km/s}$. (d) Right on the measured few km/s of metals: the
stiffness of everything solid --- and the speed of every earthquake
wave --- is the stiffness of the chemical bond.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:b3:continuum-elasticity:1}
\textbf{1.} $\mu = E/2(1+\nu) = \qty{30}{GPa}$; $\lambda =
E\nu/(1+\nu)(1-2\nu) = \qty{30}{GPa}$: for $\nu = 1/4$, $\lambda =
\mu$.
\textbf{2.} $c_{\text{P}} = \sqrt{3\mu/\rho} = \sqrt{\num{9e10}/2700}
= \qty{5.8}{km/s}$; $c_{\text{S}} = \sqrt{\mu/\rho} =
\qty{3.3}{km/s}$; ratio $\sqrt3$.
\textbf{3.} $\varepsilon \sim 2/\num{5e4} = \num{4e-5}$; $\sigma =
E\varepsilon \approx \qty{3}{MPa}$ --- the measured ``stress drop'' of
real earthquakes is indeed a few MPa.
\textbf{4.} $w = \tfrac12 E\varepsilon^2 \approx \qty{60}{J/m^3}$;
over $\num{5e13}{}\,\unit{m^3}$: $\sim\qty{3e15}{J}$, about
three-quarters of a megaton --- a large earthquake.
\textbf{5.} From below, P moves the ground vertically --- buildings
are built to carry vertical loads; S moves it \emph{horizontally},
the direction in which buildings are weakest.
\textbf{6.} Only the \omterm{prop:b3:continuum-elasticity:waves}{P wave}: it is a compression wave; the \omterm{prop:b3:continuum-elasticity:waves}{S wave} is
pure shear, at constant volume.
\textbf{7.} $t_{\text{P}} = d/c_{\text{P}}$, $t_{\text{S}} =
d/c_{\text{S}}$: $\Delta t = d(1/c_{\text{S}} - 1/c_{\text{P}})$,
inverted as stated.
\textbf{8.} $1/(1/3.33 - 1/5.77)\,\unit{km/s} \approx
\qty{7.9}{km}$ per second of delay --- the field seismologist's
``eight kilometres per second''.
\textbf{9.} $d \approx 7.9 \times 28 \approx \qty{220}{km}$.
\textbf{10.} Three (in general): each station knows a circle of
possible epicentres; two circles cross in two points, the third
decides.
\textbf{11.} $t_{\text{P}} = 80/5.8 = \qty{14}{s}$, $t_{\text{S}} =
80/3.3 = \qty{24}{s}$: about \qty{10}{s} of warning between the jolt
and the destructive shaking.
\textbf{12.} S arrives at $200/3.3 = \qty{60}{s}$. The P reaches
\qty{20}{km} at \qty{3.5}{s}; a radio message is practically
instantaneous, so the city can have almost a minute of warning ---
current systems achieve tens of seconds.
\textbf{13.} $2R_{\text{E}}/c \approx 12742/10 \approx \qty{1270}{s}$,
some twenty minutes.
\textbf{14.} That \omterm{prop:b3:continuum-elasticity:waves}{elastic waves} cross it at all: the deep Earth is not
molten through --- it transmits, and (\omterm{prop:b3:continuum-elasticity:waves}{S waves} crossing the mantle)
even shears, like a solid.
\textbf{15.} Two radii and the angle $\Delta$ between them: the chord
is $2R_{\text{E}}\sin(\Delta/2)$.
\textbf{16.} $2 \times 6371 \times \sin30^\circ = \qty{6371}{km}$;
$t \approx \qty{640}{s} \approx \qty{11}{min}$.
\textbf{17.} Beyond $103^\circ$ every ray must pass through a deep
central region; if that region is \emph{fluid} ($\mu = 0$), it carries
no shear wave: the \omterm{prop:b3:continuum-elasticity:waves}{S waves} die there. Fluids are the state of matter
that cannot resist shear.
\textbf{18.} A fluid still carries compression: \omterm{prop:b3:continuum-elasticity:waves}{P waves} cross the
core, but slower --- so they refract sharply at its boundary, and the
bent rays leave an annular shadow instead of a clean cut.
\textbf{19.} A liquid core sits at the centre of the Earth.
\textbf{20.} The chord's closest approach to the centre is
$R_{\text{E}}\cos(\Delta/2)$; grazing means
$R_{\text{E}}\cos(\Delta^*/2) = R_{\text{c}}$, i.e.\ $\Delta^* =
2\arccos(R_{\text{c}}/R_{\text{E}})$.
\textbf{21.} $R_{\text{c}} = 6371\cos(51.5^\circ) \approx
\qty{3970}{km}$.
\textbf{22.} $+14\%$ too large. Speed grows with depth, so real rays
curve back toward the surface: a ray whose deepest point just touches
the core comes up at a \emph{smaller} angle than the straight chord
through the same depth --- so from the observed $103^\circ$, the
straight-ray inversion places the tangent point too shallow, i.e.\
overestimates the core.
\textbf{23.} Weak P energy inside the shadow means something inside
the liquid core bends rays back out: a distinct \emph{inner core}
(Lehmann, 1936).
\textbf{24.} Shear waves exist only in solids: a \omterm{prop:b3:continuum-elasticity:waves}{P wave} converting to
a shear wave inside the inner core and back (the phase called PKJKP)
would prove it solid --- its detection, long sought, is the accepted
evidence.
\textbf{25.} Two speeds in rock ($5.8$ and \qty{3.3}{km/s}), one
missing wave beyond $103^\circ$, and the chord geometry yield a liquid
core of $\approx\qty{4000}{km}$ --- within $15\%$ of the modern
\qty{3480}{km}: \omterm{thm:b3:continuum-elasticity:hooke}{Hooke's law}, read at planetary scale.
\end{solution}
