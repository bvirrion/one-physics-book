\chapter{Phase Transitions}\label{ch:b3:phase-transitions}

Heat water by one degree, and nothing much happens --- until, at one
sharply defined temperature, the liquid tears itself into vapour.
Cool iron through \qty{1043}{K} and, with no change of composition,
it spontaneously magnetises. Nothing in a single molecule or a
single spin announces these revolutions: they are \emph{collective}
--- $10^{23}$ agents, each interacting with a few neighbours,
conspiring to change their society's phase all at once. The Year 1
volume catalogued the transitions; this chapter explains their
mechanism with the tools of the last five: free-energy competition,
\omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{order parameters}, the breaking of symmetry, and the astonishing
discovery that near a \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical point} the microscopic details cease
to matter --- a magnet and a boiling fluid share the same \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:landau}{critical
exponents}. The ideas of this chapter now run from pressure cookers
to superconductors to the Higgs field of
\cref{ch:b3:particle-physics}.

\section{Order parameters and free-energy competition}

\begin{definition}[Phases and order parameters]\label{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}
\index{phase transition}\index{order parameter}\index{symmetry breaking}
A \emph{phase transition} is a non-smooth change of a system's
equilibrium state as a control parameter ($T$, $P$, $B$) crosses a
boundary. Its bookkeeper is the \emph{order parameter}: a quantity
zero in the disordered phase and nonzero in the ordered one --- the
magnetisation $m$ of a ferromagnet, the density difference
$\rho_{\text{liq}} - \rho_{\text{gas}}$ of a fluid, the condensate
fraction of \cref{ch:b3:quantum-statistics}. Transitions come in
two kinds: \emph{first-order} (order parameter jumps; latent heat;
phases coexist --- boiling, melting) and \emph{continuous} (order
parameter grows from zero; no latent heat; wild fluctuations ---
the Curie point, the \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical point}, superfluid helium). When the
ordered state must \emph{choose} among equivalent options --- a
magnet's north has to point somewhere --- the transition
\emph{breaks a symmetry} that the underlying laws respect: a theme
that will return, at the highest stakes, with the Higgs mechanism.
The engine behind every case is the \omterm{prop:b3:canonical-ensemble:machine}{free energy} $F = E - TS$
(\cref{prop:b3:canonical-ensemble:machine}): energy favours order,
entropy favours disorder, and $T$ sets the exchange rate --- phase
boundaries are where the ledger ties.
\end{definition}

\section{The liquid--gas transition: van der Waals}

\begin{proposition}[Van der Waals isotherms]\label{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}
\index{van der Waals equation}\index{critical point}
The real-gas equation of the Year 1 volume,
\[
\Big(P + \frac{aN^2}{V^2}\Big)(V - Nb) = Nk_{\text{B}}T ,
\]
encodes attraction ($a$) and hard cores ($b$). Above a critical
temperature $T_{\text{c}} = 8a/27bk_{\text{B}}$ its isotherms are
monotonic: one fluid phase. Below it they develop a loop containing
a mechanically \emph{unstable} stretch ($\partial P/\partial V >
0$): the fluid there splits into coexisting liquid and gas, at the
pressure fixed by equality of \omterm{thm:b3:grand-canonical:distribution}{chemical potentials} (Maxwell's
equal-area rule). The coexistence region closes at the \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical
point} ($P_{\text{c}}, V_{\text{c}}, T_{\text{c}}$), where liquid
and gas become \emph{indistinguishable}: approaching it, the
density difference vanishes, the compressibility diverges, and
density fluctuations grow to optical size --- the critical
opalescence of \cref{pb:b3:perturbation-theory:1}.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{proposition}[Clausius--Clapeyron]\label{prop:b3:phase-transitions:clapeyron}
\index{Clausius--Clapeyron relation}
Along any first-order coexistence line, equality of \omterm{thm:b3:grand-canonical:distribution}{chemical
potentials} on both sides
(\cref{def:b3:grand-canonical:mu}) forces the line's slope to be
\[
\frac{\dd P}{\dd T} = \frac{L}{T\,\Delta v} ,
\]
with $L$ the latent heat and $\Delta v$ the volume change per
particle (or per kilogram, consistently). Everything about
coexistence lines is in this one formula: water's boiling line
climbs at $\approx\qty{3.6}{kPa/K}$ near \qty{100}{\celsius}
(pressure cookers, altitude cooking ---
\cref{pb:b3:phase-transitions:1}); and water's \emph{melting} line
tilts backwards ($\Delta v < 0$: ice floats), so pressure melts
ice --- a rarity among substances with consequences from glaciers
to skating rinks.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On the line, $\mu_1(T, P) = \mu_2(T, P)$; move along it:
$-s_1\dd T + v_1\dd P = -s_2\dd T + v_2\dd P$ (per particle, using
$\dd\mu = -s\,\dd T + v\,\dd P$). Solve for $\dd P/\dd T$ and use
$L = T(s_2 - s_1)$.
\end{proof}

\section{The ferromagnet: mean-field theory}

\begin{theorem}[Mean-field Ising transition]\label{thm:b3:phase-transitions:meanfield}
\index{Ising model}\index{mean-field theory}\index{Curie temperature}
Model a magnet as spins $s_i = \pm1$ on a lattice, neighbours
coupled by the exchange energy $-J\,s_is_j$
(\cref{exo:b3:identical-particles:12}), each spin having $q$
neighbours. Replace each spin's neighbours by their average $m =
\langle s\rangle$ (the \emph{mean-field} approximation): a spin
then sits in the effective field $qJm$, and
\cref{exo:b3:canonical-ensemble:11} gives the self-consistency
condition
\[
m = \tanh\!\Big(\frac{T_{\text{c}}}{T}\,m\Big) , \qquad
k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{c}} = qJ .
\]
Above $T_{\text{c}}$ the only solution is $m = 0$; below it two
symmetric solutions $\pm m(T)$ appear and are the stable ones: the
magnet \emph{must} magnetise, choosing a direction at random ---
spontaneous \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{symmetry breaking}. Near $T_{\text{c}}$, $m \propto
(T_{\text{c}} - T)^{1/2}$, and above it the susceptibility obeys
the Curie--Weiss law $\chi \propto 1/(T - T_{\text{c}})$:
divergent at the transition --- an infinitely obliging magnet.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Partial proof]
A spin in field $B_{\text{eff}} = qJm/\mu$ has $\langle s\rangle =
\tanh(\beta qJm)$; demanding $\langle s\rangle = m$ gives the
stated equation. Graphically: the line $y = m$ cuts the curve $y =
\tanh(T_{\text{c}}m/T)$ only at the origin when the curve's initial
slope $T_{\text{c}}/T < 1$, and also at $\pm m^* \neq 0$ when
$T < T_{\text{c}}$. Expanding $\tanh$ for small
$m$ gives $m^2 = 3(1 - T/T_{\text{c}})$, the square-root growth;
adding a small applied field and expanding likewise gives
Curie--Weiss (\cref{exo:b3:phase-transitions:6}).
\end{proof}

\section{Landau's viewpoint and universality}

\begin{proposition}[Landau theory]\label{prop:b3:phase-transitions:landau}
\index{Landau theory}\index{critical exponents}\index{universality}
Near a continuous transition, expand the \omterm{prop:b3:canonical-ensemble:machine}{free energy} in the small
\omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{order parameter}, keeping only what symmetry allows ($m \to -m$
here):
\[
F(m) = a\,(T - T_{\text{c}})\,m^2 + b\,m^4 , \qquad a, b > 0 .
\]
Above $T_{\text{c}}$: a single well at $m = 0$. Below: the origin
becomes a summit and two symmetric wells appear at $m = \pm
\sqrt{a(T_{\text{c}} - T)/2b}$ --- the same square root as mean
field, now from symmetry alone. This is why wildly different
systems behave identically near their \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical points}:
\emph{universality}. Measured exponents (e.g.\ $m \propto |T -
T_{\text{c}}|^\beta$ with $\beta \approx 0.33$ for both fluids
\emph{and} uniaxial magnets) differ from the mean-field $1/2$
because near $T_{\text{c}}$ fluctuations rage on all scales ---
their taming, the renormalisation group (Wilson, Nobel 1982),
showed that only dimensionality and symmetry, never microscopic
details, decide the exponents. The double-well picture itself has
a career beyond magnets: give the \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{order parameter} the role of a
field filling space, and its symmetry-breaking well is the Higgs
mechanism (\cref{ch:b3:particle-physics}).
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Reading a transition]\label{met:b3:phase-transitions:method}
(1) Identify the \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{order parameter} and whether it jumps
(first-order: latent heat, coexistence, hysteresis, nucleation) or
grows continuously (\omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical point}: divergences, opalescence,
universality). (2) First-order lines: Clausius--Clapeyron for the
slope; $\mu$-equality for coexistence. (3) Continuous: mean-field
first ($\tanh$ or Landau) for the phase diagram's shape; trust its
\emph{topology}, not its exponents. (4) Below $T_{\text{c}}$,
remember the choice: broken symmetry means domains, defects and
history-dependence. (5) Estimates: $k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{c}} \sim$
(coupling per neighbour) $\times$ (number of neighbours).
\end{method}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:1}
Classify (first-order or continuous), naming the \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{order parameter}
and the broken symmetry if any: (a) boiling at \qty{1}{atm}; (b)
the Curie point; (c) the superfluid $\lambda$ point; (d) boiling
\emph{exactly at} the \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical point}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:2}
Latent heats as entropy. (a) Water: $L_{\text{vap}} =
\qty{2.26e6}{J/kg}$ at \qty{373}{K}: compute $\Delta S$ per
molecule in units of $k_{\text{B}}$. (b) The same for melting
($L_{\text{fus}} = \qty{3.3e5}{J/kg}$ at \qty{273}{K}). (c) Most
liquids vaporise with $\Delta S \approx 10$--$11\,k_{\text{B}}$
(Trouton's rule): what does the number roughly count (the
$\ln$ of what volume ratio)? (d) Why is melting's entropy jump so
much smaller than boiling's?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:3}
Van der Waals critical constants ($V_{\text{c}} = 3Nb$,
$k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{c}} = 8a/27b$, $P_{\text{c}} = a/27b^2$ ---
accept). (a) Show $P_{\text{c}}V_{\text{c}}/Nk_{\text{B}}T_{\text{
c}} = 3/8$, a universal number for the model; the measured values
cluster near $0.29$ --- comment. (b) For CO$_2$
($T_{\text{c}} = \qty{304}{K}$, $P_{\text{c}} = \qty{73.8}{bar}$):
extract $a$ and $b$. (c) From $b$, estimate the molecular
diameter. (d) Why does room-temperature CO$_2$ liquefy under
pressure while room-temperature N$_2$ ($T_{\text{c}} =
\qty{126}{K}$) never does?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:4}
\omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{Order parameters}, quickly: give one for (a) a ferromagnet; (b)
the liquid--gas transition; (c) \omterm{thm:b3:quantum-statistics:bec}{Bose--Einstein condensation}; (d)
an antiferromagnet (two sublattices --- what quantity orders while
the total magnetisation stays zero?).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:5}
Mean field by hand. (a) Derive $m = \tanh(\beta qJm)$ from a spin
in the average field of its $q$ neighbours. (b) Show a nonzero
solution exists exactly when $T < qJ/k_{\text{B}}$. (c) Expand to
third order and derive $m(T) \approx \sqrt{3(1 -
T/T_{\text{c}})}$. (d) Iron: $T_{\text{c}} = \qty{1043}{K}$, $q =
8$: extract $J$ in eV and check the exchange scale of
\cref{exo:b3:identical-particles:12}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:6}
Curie--Weiss. Add a small field: $m = \tanh[\beta(qJm + \mu B)]$.
(a) Linearise above $T_{\text{c}}$ and derive $\chi = \partial
m/\partial B \propto 1/(T - T_{\text{c}})$. (b) Compare with the
free-spin Curie law: what does plotting $1/\chi$ against $T$ give
in each case, and how does the intercept diagnose ferromagnetic
coupling? (c) Nickel's $1/\chi$ line hits zero at \qty{631}{K}:
interpret. (d) For an antiferromagnet the intercept is
\emph{negative}: what sign of $J$ does that betray?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:7}
Clausius--Clapeyron at work. (a) Water at \qty{100}{\celsius}
($\Delta v = \qty{1.67}{m^3/kg}$): compute $\dd P/\dd T$. (b)
Use $\dd\ln P/\dd T = Lm_{\text{w}}/k_{\text{B}}T^2$ to estimate
the boiling temperature at $P = \qty{0.7}{atm}$ (a \qty{3000}{m}
summit). (c) Melting: with $\Delta v = \qty{-9.1e-5}{m^3/kg}$ and
$L = \qty{3.3e5}{J/kg}$, compute $\dd T/\dd P$ and the pressure
to depress ice's melting point by \qty{5}{K}. (d) A
\qty{70}{kg} skater on \qty{30}{mm^2} of blade: the pressure and
its melting-point shift --- does pressure-melting explain
skating? (What does: friction and surface premelting.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:8}
Landau computations. (a) Minimise $F = a(T - T_{\text{c}})m^2 +
bm^4$ below $T_{\text{c}}$. (b) Compute $F$ at the minimum and
show the heat capacity has a finite \emph{jump} $\Delta C =
a^2T_{\text{c}}/2b$ at the transition. (c) Sketch $C(T)$:
mean-field step versus the measured near-logarithmic peak of
helium's $\lambda$ point --- what do real fluctuations add? (d)
Add a field term $-hm$: show the transition rounds off ---
symmetry must be exact for it to break.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:9}
Nucleation. A droplet of radius $r$ in supersaturated vapour
gains bulk \omterm{prop:b3:canonical-ensemble:machine}{free energy} $-\tfrac43\pi r^3\,n\,\delta\mu$ but pays
surface tension $4\pi r^2\sigma$. (a) Show the barrier peaks at
$r^* = 2\sigma/n\,\delta\mu$. (b) Explain: why do clean
supersaturated vapours and superheated liquids persist
(metastability), and what do dust, ions and scratches provide?
(c) Cloud chambers (\cref{ch:b3:particle-physics}'s ancestor
detectors) and bubble chambers run \emph{on} this physics: which
role does the passing particle's ion trail play? (d) Why are
``boiling chips'' put in laboratory flasks?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:10}
No transition in one dimension. Consider a 1D Ising chain of $N$
spins, all up, and insert one ``domain wall'' (all spins beyond
some bond flipped). (a) Energy cost of the wall: $2J$,
independent of position. (b) Entropy gain: the wall can sit at
any of $\sim N$ bonds --- $\Delta S = k_{\text{B}}\ln N$. (c)
Show $\Delta F = 2J - k_{\text{B}}T\ln N < 0$ for any $T > 0$ at
large $N$: walls always proliferate, and long-range order is
impossible --- the 1D \omterm{thm:b3:phase-transitions:meanfield}{Ising model} has $T_{\text{c}} = 0$. (d) In
2D, a wall is a line of length $\ell$ costing $2J\ell$ but with
$\sim3^\ell$ shapes: redo the estimate and show order
\emph{survives} below a finite $T_{\text{c}}$ --- the
Peierls argument behind Onsager's exact $2.269\,J/k_{\text{B}}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:11}
Universality audited. Mean field predicts $\beta = 1/2$, $\gamma
= 1$ ($\chi \sim |t|^{-\gamma}$), $\delta = 3$ ($m \sim h^{1/3}$
at $T_{\text{c}}$). Measured, for both the liquid--gas \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical
point} \emph{and} three-dimensional uniaxial magnets: $\beta
\approx 0.326$, $\gamma \approx 1.237$, $\delta \approx 4.79$.
(a) What justifies comparing a fluid with a magnet at all (what
do their \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{order parameters} share --- a single sign choice)? (b)
Why does mean field fail near $T_{\text{c}}$ (what diverges,
invalidating the neglect of fluctuations)? (c) Why does it fail
\emph{less} in higher dimensions (neighbour count versus
fluctuation reach; mean field becomes exact above four
dimensions)? (d) State the renormalisation-group moral in two
sentences: what is being averaged scale by scale, and why only
symmetry and dimension survive the averaging.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:phase-transitions:12}
From magnets to the Higgs. Promote the \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{order parameter} to a field
$\phi(\vect r)$ with Landau energy density $\alpha(T -
T_{\text{c}})\phi^2 + b\phi^4$ plus a gradient term. (a) Below
$T_{\text{c}}$ the field sits at $\pm\phi_0$: \omterm{prop:b3:lagrangian-mechanics:modes}{small oscillations}
\emph{along} the well have a restoring curvature --- compute
$F''(\phi_0)$ and interpret it, in field language, as a
\emph{mass}. (b) For a \emph{complex} \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{order parameter} (a circle
of minima --- the ``Mexican hat''), oscillations \emph{around}
the rim cost no potential energy: a massless mode --- name the
condensed-matter analogues (spin waves, superfluid phonons). (c)
In the electroweak theory, the vacuum itself sits in such a
broken-symmetry well; particles acquire mass from the field's
nonzero value, and the radial oscillation \emph{is} the Higgs
boson: map each element onto (a)--(b). (d) In one sentence: what
did condensed-matter physics teach particle physics here (the
export, via Anderson, of \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{symmetry breaking})?
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The kitchen phase diagram}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- pressure cookers, mountain
tea, and the end of boiling}]\label{pb:b3:phase-transitions:1}
Water's phase diagram hangs, unnoticed, over every stove. This
problem walks its three lines with Clausius--Clapeyron in hand ---
from why a pressure cooker halves cooking times, through why tea
disappoints at altitude, to the strange world beyond the \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical
point} where boiling itself ceases to exist. Data: $L_{\text{vap}}
= \qty{2.26e6}{J/kg}$, $L_{\text{fus}} = \qty{3.34e5}{J/kg}$;
water vapour at \qty{373}{K}, \qty{1}{atm}: $\Delta v_{\text{vap}}
= \qty{1.67}{m^3/kg}$; melting: $\Delta v = \qty{-9.1e-5}{m^3/kg}$;
molecular mass $m_{\text{w}} = \qty{3.0e-26}{kg}$; \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical point}:
$T_{\text{c}} = \qty{647}{K}$, $P_{\text{c}} = \qty{221}{bar}$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The boiling line.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What defines ``boiling'' at a given pressure (which two
        \omterm{thm:b3:grand-canonical:distribution}{chemical potentials} tie)?
  \item Compute the slope $\dd P/\dd T$ of the vaporisation line
        at \qty{100}{\celsius}.
  \item A pressure cooker holds \qty{2}{atm}: integrate the slope
        (or use the exponential form with $Lm_{\text{w}}/
        k_{\text{B}} \approx \qty{4900}{K}$) to find its boiling
        temperature.
  \item With the Arrhenius kitchen rule of
        \cref{exo:b3:canonical-ensemble:9} (rates double per
        \qty{10}{K}), estimate the speed-up over open-pot
        cooking.
  \item At La Paz (\qty{3600}{m}, $P \approx \qty{0.65}{atm}$):
        boiling temperature, and why tea brews badly.
  \item Why does adding salt raise the boiling point (whose $\mu$
        does the solute lower --- recall
        \cref{exo:b3:grand-canonical:8})?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The melting line.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute $\dd T/\dd P$ for melting and note the sign.
  \item Which everyday observation encodes $\Delta v < 0$ (what
        does ice do in your glass), and why is that anomaly rare
        among substances?
  \item A glacier \qty{300}{m} thick: the pressure at its base
        and the melting-point depression --- does basal
        pressure-melting matter for glacier flow?
  \item Revisit the skater of
        \cref{exo:b3:phase-transitions:7}(d): what actually
        lubricates the blade?
  \item If ice sank (positive slope, like almost every other
        solid), what would winter do to lakes and their fish ---
        trace the consequence chain.
  \item The negative slope ends at high pressure where other ice
        crystal forms take over (ice III, V, VI): what does the
        existence of a dozen ices say about the free-energy
        landscape of one simple molecule?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The end of the line.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The boiling line terminates at ($T_{\text{c}},
        P_{\text{c}}$): what happens to the latent heat and the
        density difference as the end is approached?
  \item Beyond the \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical point} one can carry water from
        ``liquid-like'' to ``gas-like'' with \emph{no} transition
        at all: describe the path in the $(P, T)$ plane.
  \item Approaching the \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical point}, the fluid turns milky:
        connect this opalescence to
        \cref{exo:b3:microcanonical-ensemble:12} and
        \cref{pb:b3:perturbation-theory:1}.
  \item Supercritical CO$_2$ ($T_{\text{c}} = \qty{304}{K}$,
        $P_{\text{c}} = \qty{74}{bar}$) dissolves like a liquid
        and penetrates like a gas: name the industrial use that
        decaffeinates coffee, and why the solvent leaves no
        residue.
  \item Supercritical water (in power-plant boilers above
        \qty{221}{bar}): why does eliminating boiling ---
        bubbles, films, burnout --- appeal to boiler engineers?
  \item Deep-ocean hydrothermal vents emit water at
        \qty{400}{\celsius} that does not boil: check against the
        local pressure ($\sim\qty{300}{bar}$ at \qty{3}{km}) and
        the phase diagram.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Reading the whole map.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Sketch (in words) water's $(P, T)$ diagram: the three
        lines, the triple point ($\qty{0.01}{\celsius}$,
        \qty{611}{Pa}), the \omterm{prop:b3:phase-transitions:vdw}{critical point}.
  \item Below \qty{611}{Pa}, ice sublimes without melting: which
        appliance (freeze-drying) and which planet's polar caps
        (Mars, CO$_2$ and H$_2$O) live on this fact?
  \item Why was the triple point of water --- exactly
        \qty{273.16}{K} by definition until 2019 --- such a good
        temperature standard (how many coexisting phases pin how
        many \omterm{def:b3:lagrangian-mechanics:coordinates}{degrees of freedom})?
  \item Each line of the map is a first-order transition; only
        its endpoint is critical: restate the distinction with
        \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{order parameters} and latent heat.
  \item Humid air's dew line is the same physics run backwards:
        explain dew, fog and the morning mist on a cold window
        with one $\mu$-crossing sentence.
  \item Every pot lid is an experiment: droplets condense on it
        and rain back --- identify the miniature water cycle's
        two \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{phase transitions} and the latent-heat shuttle that
        makes a covered pot boil sooner.
  \item Summarise the named result: one slope formula, $\dd P/
        \dd T = L/T\Delta v$, explains the pressure cooker's
        \qty{121}{\celsius}, the mountain's \qty{88}{\celsius}
        tea, ice floating backwards up its melting line, and the
        supercritical kettle where boiling ends at
        $\qty{647}{K}, \qty{221}{bar}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
