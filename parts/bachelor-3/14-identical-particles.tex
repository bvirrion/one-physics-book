\chapter{Identical Particles}\label{ch:b3:identical-particles}

Every electron in the universe is strictly, perfectly identical to
every other --- not similar the way two coins from one mint are, but
indistinguishable in principle: no tag, no scratch, no history can
mark one. Quantum mechanics takes this seriously, and the
consequences run the world. Swapping two \omterm{def:b3:identical-particles:exchange}{identical particles} must
change nothing observable, which leaves the many-particle wave
function only two options: stay the same (\emph{bosons}) or change
sign (\emph{fermions}). From the minus sign alone follow the Pauli
exclusion principle, the shell structure and the whole \omterm{prop:b3:identical-particles:periodic}{periodic
table}, the stiffness of matter underfoot, and the pressure that holds
up dead stars; from the plus sign follow the gregariousness of
photons --- hence the laser --- and, three chapters ahead,
Bose--Einstein condensation. Rarely in physics has one sign carried
so much.

\section{The symmetrisation postulate}

\begin{definition}[Exchange]\label{def:b3:identical-particles:exchange}
\index{identical particles}\index{exchange operator}
For two identical particles with state space spanned by products
$\ket{a}\otimes\ket{b}$ (particle 1 in $\ket a$, particle 2 in
$\ket b$ --- positions, spins and all), the \emph{exchange operator}
$\hat P_{12}$ swaps the roles: $\hat P_{12}\ket a\otimes\ket b =
\ket b\otimes\ket a$. It is Hermitian, squares to the identity, and
commutes with any \omterm{def:b3:hamiltonian-mechanics:hamiltonian}{Hamiltonian} that treats the particles identically:
its \omterm{def:b3:quantum-formalism:observable}{eigenvalues} $\pm1$ are conserved labels of the world.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Bosons and fermions]\label{thm:b3:identical-particles:postulate}
\index{boson}\index{fermion}\index{spin-statistics theorem}
States of \omterm{def:b3:identical-particles:exchange}{identical particles} are not merely allowed but
\emph{required} to be eigenstates of every exchange: totally
\emph{symmetric} for one class of particles --- \emph{bosons} ---
and totally \emph{antisymmetric} (a sign change under every swap)
for the other --- \emph{fermions}. Which class a species belongs to
is fixed by its spin (the \emph{spin--statistics theorem}, proved
only in relativistic quantum field theory and admitted here):
\[
\text{integer spin} \Rightarrow \text{boson} , \qquad
\text{half-integer spin} \Rightarrow \text{fermion} .
\]
Photons, pions and $^4$He atoms are bosons; electrons, protons,
neutrons and $^3$He are fermions.
A composite behaves as the sum of its parts: an even number of
fermions bound together is a boson ($^4$He: 2p + 2n + 2e), an odd
number a fermion ($^3$He) --- with consequences as visible as two
different liquid heliums.
\end{theorem}
\admitted

\begin{corollary}[The Pauli exclusion principle]\label{cor:b3:identical-particles:pauli}
\index{Pauli exclusion principle}
Two fermions can never occupy the same single-particle state: putting
$\ket a = \ket b$ in the antisymmetric combination
$(\ket a\otimes\ket b - \ket b\otimes\ket a)/\sqrt2$ gives the zero
vector --- no such state exists. For electrons (spin $\tfrac12$),
each orbital admits at most two, of opposite spin projection.
\end{corollary}

\begin{example}[Counting differently]\label{ex:b3:identical-particles:counting}
Two particles among two states $a, b$: classical (distinguishable)
counting gives four configurations; bosons have three ($aa$, $bb$,
and \emph{one} mixed state); fermions exactly one (the mixed,
antisymmetric one). Statistical physics will inherit these counts
wholesale (\cref{ch:b3:quantum-statistics}); already here they
dissolve a nineteenth-century embarrassment --- the entropy of
``mixing'' two samples of the same gas
(\cref{exo:b3:identical-particles:10}).
\end{example}

\section{The periodic table}

\begin{proposition}[Shells, and why chemistry exists]\label{prop:b3:identical-particles:periodic}
\index{periodic table}\index{aufbau principle}
Fill the hydrogen-like orbitals $(n, l, m, m_s)$ with one electron
each, lowest energies first, in the level order set by penetration
and screening ($1s$, $2s$, $2p$, $3s$, $3p$, $4s$, $3d$, \dots ---
\cref{exo:b3:hydrogen-atom:8}). Pauli's principle makes the filling
\emph{saturate}: $2$ electrons close the first shell, $8$ the
second and third rows, and the table of elements acquires its
periods. Chemistry is the physics of whichever electrons the
exclusion principle left outermost: full shells (noble gases) are
inert; one electron beyond a full shell (alkalis) or one hole short
of one (halogens) is reactive in opposite ways. Without the minus
sign, every electron would sink into $1s$, every atom would be a
shrunken helium-like ball, and there would be no bonds, no
molecules, and no chemists to regret it.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{The exchange interaction}

\begin{proposition}[Symmetry of the spatial pair state]\label{prop:b3:identical-particles:exchange-force}
\index{exchange interaction}
For two electrons, the total state (space $\times$ spin) must be
antisymmetric: a \emph{singlet} (antisymmetric spin,
\cref{exo:b3:spin-two-level:11}) forces a \emph{symmetric} spatial
wave function, a \emph{triplet} an antisymmetric one. The two differ
physically: in the antisymmetric spatial state the electrons can
never coincide ($\psi(\vect r, \vect r) = 0$) and keep apart, while
the symmetric state lets them bunch. Since proximity costs Coulomb
energy, the spin arrangement acquires an energy difference --- the
\emph{exchange energy} --- of electrostatic size (electron-volts!),
though no magnetic force of that strength exists: spins align or
anti-align because of geometry plus repulsion. This is the engine of
Hund's rule, of helium's two spectra, of the covalent bond, and ---
scaled up through a crystal --- of ferromagnetism itself
(\cref{ch:b3:electromagnetism-in-matter}).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Partial proof]
The symmetry bookkeeping is
\cref{thm:b3:identical-particles:postulate} plus the spin
combinations; the vanishing at coincidence is antisymmetry read at
$\vect r_1 = \vect r_2$. The energy consequence is first-order
\omterm{thm:b3:perturbation-theory:corrections}{perturbation theory} in the repulsion: the direct term is common to
both symmetries and the cross (``exchange'') integral enters with
opposite signs (\cref{exo:b3:identical-particles:6}).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The two heliums in one atom]\label{ex:b3:identical-particles:helium}
Helium's excited states $1s\,2s$ come in two families that barely
intercombine: \emph{para}helium (singlet, symmetric space) and
\emph{ortho}helium (triplet, antisymmetric space), the triplet lying
$\approx\qty{0.8}{eV}$ \emph{lower} --- its exchange hole spares
Coulomb repulsion. The ground state is necessarily para ($1s^2$
forces the symmetric spatial state); and the lowest ortho state,
with no lower triplet to decay to and spin flips forbidden, lives
for hours --- a metastable atom used as a workhorse in atomic
beams. Nineteenth-century spectroscopists catalogued ``two
heliums''; the resolution is one minus sign.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The covalent bond]\label{ex:b3:identical-particles:bond}
Bring two \omterm{thm:b3:hydrogen-atom:levels}{hydrogen atoms} together: the two electrons can enter the
symmetric spatial combination of the two atomic orbitals (spins
singlet) or the antisymmetric one (triplet). The symmetric state
piles charge \emph{between} the protons, where it attracts both:
binding --- the covalent bond of H$_2$, \qty{4.5}{eV} deep. The
antisymmetric state evacuates the midplane: purely repulsive. Every
line of organic chemistry is bookkeeping of paired-spin electrons
shared symmetrically --- the exclusion principle, run in reverse, is
the glue of molecules.
\end{example}

\section{Bosons: the more, the merrier}

\begin{proposition}[Bosonic enhancement]\label{prop:b3:identical-particles:enhancement}
\index{stimulated emission!bosonic origin}
Transitions that add a boson to a state already holding $n$ of them
have amplitudes enhanced by $\sqrt{n + 1}$ --- rates by $n + 1$
(the ladder algebra of \cref{ch:b3:harmonic-oscillator}, since a
bosonic mode \emph{is} an oscillator). The ``$1$'' is spontaneous
emission; the ``$n$'' is \emph{stimulated} emission, proportional
to the crowd already present: the avalanche behind every laser
(Year 2 volume), now revealed as pure exchange symmetry. The same
statistics, applied to atoms cooled until their wave packets
overlap, predicts a stampede into a single quantum state ---
Bose--Einstein condensation, kept for
\cref{ch:b3:quantum-statistics}.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{method}[Handling identical particles]\label{met:b3:identical-particles:method}
(1) Decide the statistics from the spin (composites: add the
fermion count). (2) For two electrons, factor space $\times$ spin
and pair singlet with symmetric space, triplet with antisymmetric.
(3) Count states, never particles-with-names: list occupations.
(4) Energy questions with repulsion: direct integral once, exchange
integral with the symmetry's sign. (5) Remember the two slogans ---
fermions: at most one per state, an exchange hole around each;
bosons: rates boosted by occupancy.
\end{method}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:1}
(a) Show $\hat P_{12}$ is Hermitian and $\hat P_{12}^2 = \mathbb 1$:
what are its possible \omterm{def:b3:quantum-formalism:observable}{eigenvalues}? (b) Show $[\hat P_{12}, \hat H] =
0$ for \omterm{def:b3:identical-particles:exchange}{identical particles} and conclude the symmetry class is
conserved. (c) Why can a state of two \omterm{def:b3:identical-particles:exchange}{identical particles} not
simply ``be'' $\ket a\otimes\ket b$ with no symmetry? (d) Classify:
$^1$H, $^2$H (deuterium), $^4$He, $^3$He, a hydrogen molecule, a
photon.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:2}
(a) Write the normalised antisymmetric state built from orthogonal
orbitals $\ket a, \ket b$. (b) Show it vanishes when $a = b$. (c)
For three fermions in $a, b, c$: write the fully antisymmetric
combination (six terms, alternating signs --- a $3\times3$
determinant) and check one swap flips its sign. (d) Why does the
determinant form make Pauli's principle automatic?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:3}
Two particles share three orthogonal states. Count the two-particle
states for (a) distinguishable particles; (b) bosons; (c) fermions
(spinless). (d) Repeat (c) for electrons with spin: how many states
now?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:4}
(a) Give the ground configurations of carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and
neon. (b) State Hund's rule and apply it to nitrogen's three $2p$
electrons. (c) Justify Hund's rule in one sentence from
\cref{prop:b3:identical-particles:exchange-force}. (d) Which
element's full shells make it the least reactive substance known,
and which single missing electron makes fluorine the most avid?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:5}
Two non-interacting particles in a box of levels $E_n = n^2E_1$.
(a) Ground energy for two bosons; for two spinless fermions; for
two electrons. (b) First excited level of each. (c) The fermionic
ground state costs more: this ``Fermi promotion'' is a pressure in
embryo --- explain in one sentence how it stops matter from
collapsing. (d) Generalise: $N$ spinless fermions in the box ---
show the ground energy grows as $N^3$, not $N$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:6}
With orthogonal real orbitals $\varphi_a, \varphi_b$ and pair
states $\psi_\pm \propto \varphi_a(1)\varphi_b(2) \pm
\varphi_b(1)\varphi_a(2)$: (a) show $\psi_-(\vect r, \vect r) = 0$;
(b) derive
$\langle(\vect r_1 - \vect r_2)^2\rangle_\pm =
(\text{common terms}) \mp 2\,|\bra{a}\hat{\vect r}\ket{b}|^2$, and
identify which symmetry keeps the particles farther apart; (c) for
a repulsive interaction $V(|\vect r_1 - \vect r_2|)$, argue the
sign of $E_+ - E_-$; (d) connect to helium's ortho--para ordering.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:7}
Helium's ladder. (a) Why must the ground state be a spin singlet?
(b) The $1s2s$ triplet lies \qty{0.80}{eV} below the $1s2s$
singlet: express this as an exchange integral and compare its size
with magnetic spin--spin energies ($\sim\mu_{\text{B}}^2\mu_0/4\pi
a_0^3$, evaluate it). (c) Why is the $2\,^3S$ state metastable
(which two selection rules must both be broken)? (d) Its measured
life is $\approx\qty{8000}{s}$: compare with the \qty{1.6}{ns} of
hydrogen $2p$ and marvel responsibly.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:8}
Ortho- and para-hydrogen. The two protons of H$_2$ are fermions:
the \emph{total} nuclear-spin $\times$ rotational state must be
antisymmetric under their exchange; exchanging the protons also
reverses the molecular axis, multiplying the rotational state by
$(-1)^J$. (a) Show nuclear triplet (ortho) pairs with odd $J$,
singlet (para) with even $J$. (b) At high temperature, what
ortho:para ratio do the spin multiplicities dictate? (c) The
lowest state of ortho ($J = 1$) lies $2B \approx \qty{15}{meV}$
above para's $J = 0$, and spin conversion takes days: what happens
inside a tank of freshly liquefied hydrogen as the ortho fraction
slowly converts, and why must liquefier plants catalyse the
conversion \emph{before} storage? (d) Which everyday cryogenic
fuel programme (rockets!) meets this exact problem?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:9}
Bosonic stampede. A mode holds $n$ photons; matrix elements for
adding one scale as $\sqrt{n+1}$. (a) Show emission rates into the
mode scale as $n + 1$ and absorption as $n$. (b) In a cavity where
one mode holds $10^{10}$ photons and its neighbours hold none,
compare an excited atom's emission rates into each. (c) Explain in
two sentences how this makes laser light \emph{grow} in a single
mode. (d) Where in \cref{prop:b3:harmonic-oscillator:coherent-motion}
did the $\sqrt{n+1}$ already appear?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:10}
Gibbs's paradox, resolved by identity. Two equal volumes $V$ of
ideal gas, $N$ molecules each, same temperature, are joined. (a) If
the gases are \emph{different} (say argon and neon), the entropy
rises by $2Nk_{\text{B}}\ln2$ --- recall why (each gas doubles its
volume). (b) If they are the \emph{same} gas, opening the tap
changes nothing macroscopic: what absurdity would follow if the
mixing entropy appeared anyway (consider closing and reopening)?
(c) Show that treating the molecules as distinguishable
\emph{does} produce the spurious entropy, and that dividing the
state count by $N!$ --- legitimate only because molecules are
identical --- removes it. (d) Moral, in one sentence: classical
statistical physics silently borrows a quantum fact; the next
chapters will borrow it loudly.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:11}
Pauli holds up the dead. A white dwarf packs about
$\qty{e9}{kg/m^3}$, i.e.\ electron density $n_{\text{e}} \approx
\qty{3e35}{m^{-3}}$ (one electron per two nucleon masses). (a)
Compute the Fermi energy from
\cref{exo:b3:schrodinger-three-dimensions:8}
--- and notice the non-relativistic formula strains. (b) Compare
$E_{\text{F}}$ with $k_{\text{B}}T$ at $T = \qty{e7}{K}$: in what
sense is this white-hot star \emph{cold}? (c) Explain in two
sentences how exclusion, not heat, supplies the pressure that
balances gravity. (d) What must happen as mass is added and
$E_{\text{F}}$ turns relativistic --- which famous limit does that
foreshadow (\cref{ch:b3:astrophysics})?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:identical-particles:12}
From exchange to magnets. Model two neighbouring electrons in a
solid by \cref{prop:b3:identical-particles:exchange-force}: their
energy is $E_{\text{singlet}}$ or $E_{\text{triplet}} =
E_{\text{singlet}} - 2J$ with exchange constant $J$. (a) Show this
is reproduced by the effective spin \omterm{def:b3:hamiltonian-mechanics:hamiltonian}{Hamiltonian} $\hat H =
-2J\,\hat{\vect S}_1\cdot\hat{\vect S}_2/\hbar^2$ (use
$\hat{\vect S}_1\cdot\hat{\vect S}_2 = \tfrac12[\hat S^2 - \hat
S_1^2 - \hat S_2^2]$). (b) For $J > 0$ neighbouring spins align:
what macroscopic phenomenon does a lattice of such pairs produce?
(c) Estimate the ordering temperature $k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{C}}
\sim J$ for $J \approx \qty{0.1}{eV}$ and compare with iron's
\qty{1043}{K}. (d) Why could no \emph{magnetic} dipole--dipole
interaction (recall its size from
\cref{exo:b3:identical-particles:7}b) explain a thousand-kelvin
magnet --- and what does, in one sentence?
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The logic of the periodic table}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- three quantum rules build all
of chemistry}]\label{pb:b3:identical-particles:1}
Hand a physicist the hydrogen orbitals $(n, l, m)$, the electron's
spin, and the Pauli principle, and the \omterm{prop:b3:identical-particles:periodic}{periodic table} follows ---
periods, families, sizes, the zigzag of ionisation energies, even
the colour of gold. This problem builds it. Data: hydrogenic
levels $-13.6\,Z_{\text{eff}}^2/n^2$ eV; measured first ionisation
energies (eV): H $13.6$, He $24.6$, Li $5.4$, Be $9.3$, B $8.3$,
C $11.3$, N $14.5$, O $13.6$, F $17.4$, Ne $21.6$, Na $5.1$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The three rules.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item State the three ingredients: the orbital catalogue
        $(n, l, m)$ with its degeneracies, the spin doubling, and
        the exclusion principle. How many electrons fit in $n = 1$,
        $2$, $3$?
  \item Why does the \emph{energy order} of subshells in a
        many-electron atom differ from hydrogen's pure-$n$ order
        (recall penetration and screening,
        \cref{exo:b3:hydrogen-atom:8})?
  \item Write the filling order through $4s$ and $3d$, and give
        the configurations of Na ($Z = 11$), Cl ($17$), K ($19$),
        Fe ($26$).
  \item Row lengths: explain the observed $2, 8, 8, 18$ of the
        table's first four periods from the filling order (why is
        the third row 8, not 18?).
  \item What single feature makes the noble gases chemically
        silent?
  \item Define the effective charge $Z_{\text{eff}}$ seen by a
        valence electron, and explain why it is roughly $1$ for
        sodium's outer electron while helium's electrons each see
        nearly the full $Z = 2$ minus a fraction of one another's
        screening.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Reading the second row.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item From the data, ionisation energy climbs from Li to Ne but
        dips at B and at O: locate both dips.
  \item Explain the boron dip (which subshell opens at B, and why
        is $2p$ costlier to hold than $2s$?).
  \item Explain the oxygen dip (what must two of oxygen's $2p$
        electrons do for the first time, and which rule's comfort
        is lost?).
  \item Estimate helium's ionisation energy naively as
        $13.6 \times Z^2 = \qty{54.4}{eV}$: why is the measured
        \qty{24.6}{eV} so much smaller, and what number
        $Z_{\text{eff}} \approx 1.34$ is the fit telling you?
  \item Lithium's \qty{5.4}{eV} against hydrogen's \qty{13.6}{eV}:
        what pair of effects (screening; higher $n$) conspires?
  \item Why does atomic \emph{size} shrink across a row
        (Li $\to$ Ne) though electrons are being added?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Families and bonds.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Alkalis: one $s$ electron over a noble core --- derive
        their family traits (low ionisation, $+1$ ions, violent
        water chemistry) from the configuration.
  \item Halogens: one hole in a $p$ shell --- derive theirs
        (electron affinity, $-1$ ions, diatomic molecules).
  \item Carbon's four: why is a $2s^22p^2$ atom tetravalent in
        practice (what near-degeneracy lets it promote and
        hybridise --- recall the Stark hybrids of
        \cref{ex:b3:perturbation-theory:stark})?
  \item Use \cref{ex:b3:identical-particles:bond}: why do two
        closed-shell atoms (two heliums) form \emph{no} covalent
        bond?
  \item Transition metals: the $4s$/$3d$ near-degeneracy makes ten
        columns of similar chemistry: why does filling an
        \emph{inner} $d$ shell change the chemistry so little?
  \item Predict, from configurations alone, which of K, Ca, Sc
        first shows magnetism-ready unpaired $d$ electrons.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Beyond the rules of thumb.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Hund's rule assigns nitrogen three parallel $2p$ spins:
        which measurable atomic property (its magnetic moment)
        does this fix, and which chapter-14 mechanism pays for the
        alignment?
  \item The table's very existence requires strict identity of
        electrons: what would ``slightly distinguishable''
        electrons do to Pauli's principle and to shell closure?
  \item In heavy atoms the inner electrons move at $Z\alpha c$:
        for gold ($Z = 79$), estimate $v/c$ for the $1s$ pair and
        state qualitatively what relativity does to the $6s$
        orbital's energy --- the accepted explanation of gold's
        colour and mercury's liquidity.
  \item The $4s$ subshell fills before $3d$ yet ionises
        \emph{first} in transition metals, and chromium and copper
        borrow an $s$ electron to reach $3d^5$ and $3d^{10}$: what
        do these anomalies say about how close the two subshells'
        energies run?
  \item Two isotopes of an element are chemically all but
        identical: from the three rules, why does chemistry care
        only about $Z$ and hardly at all about the nucleus's mass?
  \item Period 7 ends near $Z \approx 118$ and superheavy
        chemistry departs from the table's naive column logic:
        name the two competing scales (shell structure versus
        relativistic and Coulomb effects at large $Z$).
