\chapter{Particle Physics}\label{ch:b3:particle-physics}

% Provenance (not for print): second half of the "physique subatomique"
% module common to the surveyed third-year curricula: standard model
% inventory, interactions, conservation laws, detectors.

Strip matter of its structure, layer by layer --- molecules, atoms,
nuclei, nucleons --- and the peeling stops, abruptly, at a short list:
six \emph{\omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{quarks}}, six \emph{\omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{leptons}}, a handful of force carriers,
and one shy scalar found in 2012. Everything you have ever touched is
three entries of that list; the rest lived in the first microsecond of
the universe and live again, briefly, wherever
$E = mc^2$ is paid in full --- cosmic-ray collisions and accelerator
rings. This chapter takes inventory: the particles, the four
interactions that move them, the conservation laws
(\cref{ch:b3:lagrangian-mechanics}'s Noether currents, now running the
subatomic bookkeeping), and the detectors that photograph it all. It
ends in your own attic, counting muons made ten kilometres overhead.

\section{The inventory}

\begin{definition}[Leptons, quarks, hadrons]\label{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}
\index{lepton}\index{quark}\index{hadron}\index{baryon}\index{meson}\index{antiparticle}
Matter's building blocks are spin-$\tfrac12$ fermions
(\cref{ch:b3:spin-two-level}, \cref{ch:b3:identical-particles}) in
two families of six. \emph{Leptons} feel no strong force: the
electron, muon and tau (charge $-e$, each heavier than the last)
and their three neutrinos --- nearly massless, nearly
undetectable. \emph{Quarks} come in six flavours --- up, down,
strange, charm, bottom, top --- with charges $+\tfrac23e$ (u, c,
t) and $-\tfrac13e$ (d, s, b), and they are never seen alone:
\emph{confinement} binds them into \emph{hadrons} --- three-quark
\emph{baryons} (proton $=$ uud, neutron $=$ udd) and
quark--antiquark \emph{mesons} (pion, kaon). Each particle has an
\emph{antiparticle} of identical mass and opposite charges
(\cref{exo:b3:particle-physics:8}). Ordinary matter uses only the
first generation --- u, d, e, $\nu_{\text{e}}$; the two heavier
copies differ, as far as anyone knows, only in mass. ``Who
ordered that?'' physics still asks.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Four interactions]\label{rem:b3:particle-physics:forces}
\index{strong interaction}\index{weak interaction}\index{electroweak}
Every process is one of four forces at work, each carried by its
boson. The \emph{strong} force (gluons) grips \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{quarks} with a
potential that \emph{grows} with distance --- pull two \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{quarks}
apart and the stretched field snaps into a fresh
quark--antiquark pair (\cref{exo:b3:particle-physics:1}): hence
confinement, and a residue of it glues nuclei
(\cref{ch:b3:nuclear-physics}). \emph{Electromagnetism} (photon,
massless: infinite range) runs chemistry and light. The
\emph{weak} force (W and Z, a massive
$\approx80$--$\qty{91}{GeV}$: range $10^{-3}\,\unit{fm}$,
hence ``weak'') is the only flavour-changer --- $\beta$ decay is
a d \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{quark} becoming a u
(\cref{prop:b3:particle-physics:conservation}) --- and the only
force neutrinos answer to. \emph{Gravity} is $10^{36}$ times
feebler than electromagnetism between two protons and plays no
laboratory role --- yet, unscreened and long-ranged, it will run
all of \cref{ch:b3:astrophysics}. Electromagnetism and the weak
force unify at high energy into one \emph{electroweak}
interaction --- split apart, at our cold energies, by the Higgs
field's \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{symmetry breaking} (\cref{exo:b3:particle-physics:9}).
\end{remark}

\section{The bookkeeping: conservation laws}

\begin{proposition}[Conservation laws of particle reactions]\label{prop:b3:particle-physics:conservation}
\index{baryon number}\index{lepton number}\index{conservation laws!particle}
A reaction happens only if it balances the books. Always
conserved: energy--momentum
(\cref{ch:b3:relativistic-dynamics}), angular momentum, and
electric charge --- Noether's theorem
(\cref{thm:b3:lagrangian-mechanics:noether}) underwriting each.
Conserved in every reaction yet observed: \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:particle-physics:conservation}{baryon number}}
$B$ (\omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{baryons} $+1$, antibaryons $-1$) and \emph{\omterm{prop:b3:particle-physics:conservation}{lepton number}}
$L$ (per family, to excellent accuracy). Thus the proton, the
lightest \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{baryon}, has nowhere to decay --- matter's stability is
a bookkeeping theorem --- and $\beta$ decay must ship an
antineutrino:
\[
\text{n} \;\to\; \text{p} + \text{e}^- + \bar\nu_{\text{e}}
\qquad (\text{quark level: } \text{d} \to \text{u} +
\text{e}^- + \bar\nu_{\text{e}}) ,
\]
balancing $L$ (and explaining the electron's continuous energy
spectrum, which nearly cost physics its faith in energy
conservation before Pauli invented the neutrino to save it).
Flavours (strangeness, charm) are conserved by strong and
electromagnetic processes but \emph{broken by the weak}: strange
particles are born copiously in pairs, then die slowly and alone
--- the $10^{13}$ gap between $\qty{e-23}{s}$ strong lifetimes
and $\qty{e-10}{s}$ weak ones is the fingerprint of which force
signed the decay.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\section{Making and seeing particles}

\begin{remark}[Accelerators and detectors]\label{rem:b3:particle-physics:machines}
\index{collider}\index{particle detector}
Two reasons to accelerate: \emph{resolution} --- a probe sees no
detail finer than its wavelength $\lambda = h/p$
(\cref{ch:b3:crystalline-solids} used ångströms; femtometres
cost \unit{GeV}s) --- and \emph{creation}: collision energy
becomes new mass, and colliding two beams head-on spends it all
(a fixed target wastes most of it carrying the debris forward:
\cref{exo:b3:particle-physics:4}). Circular colliders recycle
the same particles through the same accelerating gap while
magnets steer them --- paying synchrotron radiation
$\propto\gamma^4$, which is why the ring that found the Higgs
collides heavy protons, not featherweight electrons. Around the
crossing point sit onion layers, each asking one question: a
\emph{tracker} bends charged paths in a magnet (momentum); an
electromagnetic \emph{calorimeter} swallows electrons and
photons (energy); a hadronic one swallows pions and protons;
and only muons punch through to the outermost chambers ---
identity by depth of burial. Neutrinos leave as missing
momentum: bookkeeping detects what nothing can stop.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The Higgs, and the open ledger]\label{rem:b3:particle-physics:higgs}
\index{Higgs boson}\index{dark matter}
The Standard Model's symmetry forbids bare masses for its
particles; the \emph{Higgs field}, filling all space with a
nonzero condensate --- the double-well choice of
\cref{ch:b3:phase-transitions}, promoted to the vacuum itself
--- breaks the symmetry and grants masses through its couplings:
heavier particle, stronger grip. Its own quantum, the \omterm{rem:b3:particle-physics:higgs}{Higgs
boson} (\qty{125}{GeV}, found 2012), completed the table. The
open ledger is longer: neutrinos oscillate between flavours and
so must have mass the model never wrote in
(\cref{exo:b3:particle-physics:10}); galaxies rotate as if held
by five times more matter than shines
(\cref{ch:b3:astrophysics}); and the universe contains matter
but almost no antimatter, an asymmetry the model's tiny
CP-violation cannot yet pay for. The list is the syllabus of
whatever comes next.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:1}
\omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{Quark} arithmetic. (a) Verify the charges of p $=$ uud and
n $=$ udd. (b) The pion $\pi^+$ is $\text{u}\bar{\text{d}}$:
check its charge. (c) Propose \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{quark} contents for $\pi^-$ and for
the $\Delta^{++}$ (charge $+2e$). (d) Why does pulling a \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{quark}
out of a proton yield a \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{meson} shower instead of a free \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{quark}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:2}
Sorting. For each of e$^-$, $\nu_\mu$, $\pi^+$, p, n,
$\mu^-$: (a) \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{lepton}, \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{baryon} or \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{meson}? (b) which feel the strong
force? (c) which are stable in isolation? (d) which can a
magnetic field not deflect?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:3}
Allowed or forbidden? Judge, naming the violated law if any:
(a) $\text{n} \to \text{p} + \text{e}^- + \bar\nu_{\text{e}}$;
(b) $\text{p} \to \text{n} + \text{e}^+ + \nu_{\text{e}}$ (for
a \emph{free} proton);
(c) $\text{p} \to \text{e}^+ + \gamma$;
(d) $\mu^- \to \text{e}^- + \gamma$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:4}
The price of creation. Producing a proton--antiproton pair from
a proton beam on a hydrogen target requires (by
\cref{ch:b3:relativistic-dynamics}'s invariant-mass argument) a
beam kinetic energy of $6m_{\text{p}}c^2$. (a) Evaluate in GeV.
(b) In a collider, two beams of what kinetic energy suffice for
the same pair? (c) Compute the ``waste'' ratio and explain where
the fixed-target energy goes. (d) Why did the antiproton's 1955
discovery machine need $\sim\qty{6}{GeV}$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:5}
The muon, twice over. The muon ($m = \qty{106}{MeV}$,
$\tau = \qty{2.2}{\micro s}$) is the electron's heavy twin. (a)
What distance is $c\tau$? (b) Cosmic muons born at
\qty{15}{km} reach the ground: what $\gamma$ does survival
demand, and what energy is that? (c) Describe the same survival
from the muon's own frame. (d) Why does the muon decay to an
electron but never to a proton, and why is its decay
\emph{slow} by particle standards?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:6}
Inside $\beta$ decay. (a) Write neutron decay at the \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{quark}
level and check all conserved quantities. (b) The emitted
electron's energy spectrum is continuous up to an endpoint:
explain how this once threatened energy conservation and how
Pauli's neutrino rescued it. (c) The W boson weighs
\qty{80.4}{GeV}: estimate the weak force's range
$\hbar/m_{\text{W}}c$ ($\hbar c = \qty{197}{MeV.fm}$). (d) Use
that range to explain in one sentence why the ``weak'' force is
weak at low energy yet electroweak-strong at high.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:7}
Strange bookkeeping. Kaons and $\Lambda$ hyperons carry a
flavour, \emph{strangeness}, conserved by the strong force but
not the weak. (a) Explain why cosmic-ray collisions produce
strange particles only in pairs. (b) The $\Lambda$ lives
$\qty{2.6e-10}{s}$ --- compare with the $\qty{e-23}{s}$ of
strong decays and conclude which force kills it. (c) What ratio
of timescales separates the two, and to what everyday ratio is
that comparable (one second against what)? (d) How did this
``strange'' behaviour --- copious birth, reluctant death ---
force the invention of a new quantum number?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:8}
Antimatter, cashed. (a) Why must
$\text{e}^+ + \text{e}^- \to \gamma$ (one photon) be forbidden,
while two photons are fine? (b) Compute the energy released by
annihilating one gram of antimatter with one of matter, and
compare with \cref{exo:b3:nuclear-physics:9}'s kilogram of
fissioned uranium. (c) PET scanners
(\cref{exo:b3:nuclear-physics:12}) run on positrons: where do
medicine's positrons come from? (d) Making antiprotons costs
$\sim10^{6}$ times their \omterm{def:b3:relativistic-dynamics:momentum-energy}{rest energy} in wall-plug power: comment
on antimatter as a fuel.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:9}
The Higgs as a \omterm{def:b3:phase-transitions:order}{phase transition}. (a) Restate, in the language of
\cref{exo:b3:phase-transitions:12}, what it means that the
vacuum sits in a broken-symmetry well. (b) Why do the W and Z
get masses while the photon stays massless (what part of the
symmetry survives)? (c) The \omterm{rem:b3:particle-physics:higgs}{Higgs boson} is the field's radial
oscillation: what plays this role in the magnet of
\cref{thm:b3:phase-transitions:meanfield}? (d) Above
$\sim10^{15}\,\unit{K}$ the symmetry restores: where and when
was the universe last that hot (\cref{ch:b3:astrophysics})?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:10}
Neutrinos by the billion. (a) The Sun makes two neutrinos per
$4\text{p} \to {}^4\text{He}$ cycle (\qty{26.7}{MeV}): from the
solar constant \qty{1360}{W/m^2}, compute the neutrino flux at
Earth. (b) How many cross your thumbnail
(\qty{1}{cm^2}) per second, and roughly how many will interact
with your body in a lifetime (take an interaction
probability of order $10^{-22}$ per metre of flesh per neutrino as
given)? (c) Early
detectors caught only a third of the predicted
$\nu_{\text{e}}$: state the resolution (oscillations) and its
structural consequence for the Standard Model. (d) Why does a
neutrino detector live under a mountain?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:11}
Machine numbers. LHC protons: $E = \qty{6.8}{TeV}$ each. (a)
Compute $\gamma$ and $1 - v/c \approx 1/2\gamma^2$. (b) What
wavelength $\lambda \approx hc/E$ probes structure, and compare
with the proton's \qty{0.8}{fm} radius. (c) Head-on collision
energy: \qty{13.6}{TeV} --- how many proton rest masses could
that create? (d) Synchrotron loss scales as $\gamma^4$:
compute the electron-to-proton $\gamma$ ratio at fixed energy
and justify protons for the ring, electrons for precision
machines.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b3:particle-physics:12}
Messengers from the edge. The highest-energy cosmic ray ever
recorded carried $\sim\qty{3e20}{eV}$ --- a single proton. (a)
Convert to joules and name a macroscopic object with that
kinetic energy. (b) Compute its $\gamma$, and the width of the
Galaxy ($\sim10^{5}$ light-years) in its own frame. (c) Above
$\sim\qty{5e19}{eV}$, protons scatter on the cosmic microwave
background (\cref{ch:b3:photons-phonons}) --- explain
qualitatively why the universe is opaque to them. (d) Why do
such energies, far beyond any accelerator, still teach us less
than the LHC does? (Think flux and control.)
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The attic muon telescope}

\begin{problem}\label{pb:b3:particle-physics:1}
\emph{The attic muon telescope.} For the science fair you build a
cosmic-ray telescope: two $20\times20$\,\unit{cm} scintillator paddles,
one \qty{30}{cm} above the other, each read by a photomultiplier,
wired in coincidence --- a count only when \emph{both} fire
within \qty{100}{ns}. Sea-level muon flux through a horizontal
surface: about 1 per \unit{cm^2} per minute.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Counting the sky.}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Where do your muons start? Trace the chain: primary
        proton, upper atmosphere, pions, muons --- naming which
        force governs each step.
  \item Why do the pions themselves almost never reach the
        ground ($\tau_\pi = \qty{26}{ns}$), while their daughter
        muons do?
  \item Predict your single-paddle rate from the quoted flux.
  \item The paddle alone actually clicks far more often ---
        electronics noise and ambient radioactivity
        (\cref{ch:b3:nuclear-physics}). Explain how the
        two-paddle coincidence kills almost all of it.
  \item What accidental-coincidence rate do two independent
        \qty{100}{Hz} noise sources produce in a \qty{100}{ns}
        window ($R_{\text{acc}} = R_1R_2\Delta t$)? Compare with
        the real muon rate.
  \item Your coincidence rate settles near 100 per minute ---
        well below the single-paddle 400. Explain: which muons
        does the two-paddle geometry accept?
  \item Tilt the telescope: the rate falls roughly as
        $\cos^2\theta$ of the zenith angle. Give the physical
        reason (what grows with $\theta$?).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Relativity, verified in the attic.}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Muons: $\tau = \qty{2.2}{\micro s}$. Compute $c\tau$ and
        conclude what a \emph{classical} muon born at
        \qty{15}{km} could do.
  \item Your muons average $E \approx \qty{4}{GeV}$
        ($m_\mu c^2 = \qty{106}{MeV}$): compute $\gamma$.
  \item Dilated lifetime, and the corresponding range: does the
        attic rate make sense now?
  \item Retell the survival from the muon's frame
        (\cref{ch:b3:relativistic-kinematics}).
  \item For $\gamma = 38$, compute the surviving fraction from
        \qty{15}{km} ($t_{\text{proper}} = L/\gamma v$, survival
        $\eu^{-t/\tau}$).
  \item Your telescope is therefore \emph{which} classic
        experiment, rebuilt with school parts? State what a null
        result (classical decay) would have predicted for your
        counter.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- What a muon is.}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Place the muon in the Standard Model table: family,
        generation, charge, spin.
  \item It decays: $\mu^- \to \text{e}^- + \bar\nu_{\text{e}} +
        \nu_\mu$. Check charge and both \omterm{def:b3:particle-physics:zoo}{lepton} family numbers.
  \item Why is the decay electron's energy spectrum continuous,
        and what is its endpoint ($\approx m_\mu c^2/2$)?
  \item Why is $\mu^- \to \text{e}^- + \gamma$ --- never
        observed --- so interesting to today's experiments?
        (What would its discovery break?)
  \item A muon stopping in your paddle decays at rest: describe
        the delayed second flash and how it hands you $\tau$
        directly.
  \item Muons are ``heavy electrons'': give one consequence for
        atoms --- what happens to a muonic hydrogen's \omterm{thm:b3:hydrogen-atom:levels}{Bohr
        radius} (\cref{ch:b3:hydrogen-atom}), scaled by
        $m_\mu/m_{\text{e}} \approx 207$?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Up the chain.}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A $\qty{e15}{eV}$ primary proton spawns a shower of
        millions. Estimate the average energy per shower
        particle if $10^{6}$ particles share it.
  \item Big observatories replace your paddles with hundreds of
        water tanks kilometres apart, watching for
        \emph{Cherenkov} light (\cref{pb:b3:nuclear-physics:1}):
        what do muons do in the water, and why tanks \emph{far
        apart}?
  \item Your scintillator-and-photomultiplier is the same
        principle as the calorimeters at the great colliders:
        state that shared principle in one sentence
        (particle $\to$ light $\to$ electrons $\to$ count).
  \item Bubble-chamber photographs once did the seeing: charged
        tracks curling in a magnetic field. What two quantities
        does the curl hand you, and which particle leaves no
        track at all?
  \item The record cosmic ray of
        \cref{exo:b3:particle-physics:12} hit with the energy of
        a served tennis ball in one proton. What does its
        existence say about nature's accelerators versus ours?
  \item Summarise the fair poster in four lines: born of a
        proton ten kilometres up, delivered by \omterm{prop:b3:relativistic-kinematics:dilation}{time dilation},
        identified by two coincident flashes, and explained by a
        table with twelve seats.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
