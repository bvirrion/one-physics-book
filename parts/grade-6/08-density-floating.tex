\chapter{Density: Why Things Float}\label{ch:g6:density-floating}

In your very first year, a tiny coin sank while a great log
floated, and we promised that the ``why'' was a treasure of this
course. You have earned it. The key was forged over two chapters
--- \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} for the stuff, \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume} for the room --- and today the two
turn together in the lock.

\section{The mass of one cubic centimetre}

\begin{example}[Fair comparison at last]\label{ex:g6:density-floating:fair}
Comparing a whole log with a whole coin was never fair: different
sizes, different shapes. The fair contest takes \emph{equal
\omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volumes}}: one \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{cubic centimetre} of each contestant. One
\unit{cm^3} of water has a \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} of \qty{1}{g}. One \unit{cm^3}
of oak: about \qty{0.6}{g}. Of stone: about \qty{2.5}{g}. Of
iron: \qty{7.9}{g}. Equal rooms, wildly unequal stuff --- this is
the number the floating game was waiting for.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Density]\label{def:g6:density-floating:density}
The \emph{density}\index{density} of a substance is the \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} of
one \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{cubic centimetre} of it, in \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:units}{grams}. Water's density is
\qty{1}{g} per \unit{cm^3}; oak's about $0.6$; iron's $7.9$. To
find a density, no new instrument is needed: \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{measure} a sample's
\omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} and its \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume}, then divide --- share the \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:units}{grams} equally
among the \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{cubic centimetres}, and the answer is each \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{centimetre}'s
share.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Density is a signature]\label{prop:g6:density-floating:signature}
The \omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density} of a pure substance is the same for every piece of
it, large or small: a splinter of oak and a whole \omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beam} both carry
about \qty{0.6}{g} in each \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{cubic centimetre}. \omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{Density} therefore
identifies substances, as \omterm{def:g4:changes-of-state:melting-point}{melting points} do: \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{measure} a mystery
object's \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} and \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume}, divide, and compare with the table ---
the substance often confesses on the spot.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Measuring a density]\label{met:g6:density-floating:measure}
For a pebble --- or a crown:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{measure} its \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} with the balance: say \qty{75}{g};
  \item \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{measure} its \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume} by the rise of water: say
        \qty{30}{cm^3};
  \item divide the \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:units}{grams} by the \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{cubic centimetres}: $75 \div 30 =
        2.5$ --- each \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{cubic centimetre} carries \qty{2.5}{g};
  \item consult the table: \omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density} $2.5$ --- our pebble is
        ordinary stone.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{The floating rule}

\begin{proposition}[Why things float]\label{prop:g6:density-floating:rule}
An object \omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{floats} in water if its \omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density} is \emph{less} than
water's --- less than \qty{1}{g} per \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{cubic centimetre} --- and
\omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{sinks} if its \omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density} is greater. The water, so to speak, compares
the intruder with itself, room for room: lighter-than-me-per-room
rides on top; heavier-per-room goes down. The same rule governs
any \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid}, each with its own \omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density} as the referee.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Six years of puzzles, settled]\label{ex:g6:density-floating:settled}
The log ($0.6$): \omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{floats}, however huge --- every \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{cubic centimetre}
of it underbids water. The coin (bronze, near $9$): \omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{sinks},
however small. The plastic duck, the cork, the pencil: under $1$,
floaters all. Ice, at $0.9$ --- water's own swollen \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:solid}{solid} ---
\omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{floats} on its own \omterm{def:g3:solids-liquids-gases:liquid}{liquid} by a whisker, which is why ponds wear
lids and icebergs sail. And the plasticine boat? Shaped as a
bowl, the \emph{boat-plus-air} parcel holds much \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} per room:
the parcel's \omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density} drops below $1$, and down at the docks the
same trick launches ten-thousand-tonne ships of steel.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The liquid tower]\label{ex:g6:density-floating:tower}
Pour honey, then water tinted with ink, then oil gently down a
tall glass's side: three layers, honey ($1.4$) at the bottom, oil
($0.9$) on top, refusing to mix their ranks. Drop in guests: a
grape ($1.05$) \omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{sinks} through the oil, \omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{sinks} through the water,
and rests on the honey --- denser than two floors, lighter than
the third. The tower is the floating rule performed as theater.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Reading the iceberg]\label{rem:g6:density-floating:iceberg}
Ice's $0.9$ against water's $1.0$ does more than \omterm{def:g1:floating-and-sinking:words}{float} the berg:
it sets the waterline. Nine tenths of an iceberg's bulk rides
\emph{below} the surface --- the famous hidden \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} that sank
proud ships. Exactly \emph{why} the fraction below matches the
\omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density} ratio is a beautiful law of the ancient bathtub scientist
himself, proved honestly in the High School volume; the table of
densities already whispers the answer.
\end{remark}

\section{The crown, at last}

\begin{example}[Archimedes closes the case]\label{ex:g6:density-floating:crown}
Now finish the story begun two chapters ago. The crown's \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass}:
easy, the balance gives it. Its \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume}: impossible for a lumpy
masterpiece --- until the bathtub's lesson gave the rise method.
\omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{Mass} divided by \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume}: the crown's \emph{\omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density}} --- and
\omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density} is a signature. Pure gold signs $19.3$; silver signs
$10.5$; a secret blend signs something in between. Legend says
the goldsmith's crown confessed a \omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density} well short of gold's,
and the fraud was exposed without a scratch on the crown. The
weekend problem hands you his numbers.
\end{example}

\section{Problem: The King's Crown}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the case of the king's crown,
reopened with your instruments; the goldsmith's trial, solved by
division}]\label{pb:g6:density-floating:1}
Reopen antiquity's most famous fraud case with modern numbers.
Signatures: gold $19.3$, silver $10.5$ (\omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:units}{grams} per \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{cubic
centimetre}).

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The evidence.}
The king gave the goldsmith \qty{1930}{g} of pure gold. The
finished crown, on the balance: \qty{1930}{g} exactly.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} matches perfectly. Why does this alone prove
        nothing about the fraud? (What could the goldsmith have
        done and still matched it?)
  \item What \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume} should \qty{1930}{g} of \emph{pure gold}
        occupy? (Share the \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:units}{grams}: how many \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{cubic centimetres} at
        \qty{19.3}{g} each?)
  \item Archimedes lowers the crown into a brim-full vessel and
        collects the overflow: \qty{130}{cm^3}. What is the
        crown's true \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume}?
  \item Compare the two \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volumes}. What does the comparison already
        shout?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The signature.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Compute the crown's \omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density} from its \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} and its
        measured \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume} (one decimal is enough).
  \item Set the three signatures side by side: gold, the crown,
        silver. Where does the crown fall?
  \item Explain, in one sentence to the king, why the crown
        cannot be pure gold.
  \item The goldsmith protests: ``Perhaps the balance erred!''
        Grant him a generous error of \qty{30}{g} either way.
        Does any \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} between \qty{1900}{g} and \qty{1960}{g}
        rescue a pure-gold crown of \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume} \qty{130}{cm^3}?
        (Check the \omterm{def:g6:density-floating:density}{density} range.)
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- How much was stolen?}
Suppose the goldsmith kept some gold and replaced it, \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:units}{gram} for
\omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:units}{gram}, with silver.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Why does replacing gold by silver, \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:units}{gram} for \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:units}{gram}, keep
        the \omterm{def:g3:measuring-mass:mass}{mass} right but \emph{swell} the \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume}?
  \item Test a guess: a crown of \qty{965}{g} gold $+$
        \qty{965}{g} silver. \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{Volume} of the gold share? Of the
        silver share? (One decimal.)
  \item Total the guessed crown's \omterm{def:g6:mass-and-volume:volume}{volume}, and judge the guess
        against the measured \qty{130}{cm^3}: too much silver, or
        too little?
  \item The court needs a verdict sentence: state what was
        proved beyond doubt (pure gold or not), what the method
        was, and why the crown never needed to be harmed ---
        Archimedes' true triumph.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
