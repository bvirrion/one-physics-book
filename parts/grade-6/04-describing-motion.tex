\chapter{Describing Motion: Trajectory and Speed}\label{ch:g6:describing-motion}

A falling leaf, a \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{high-speed} train, a carousel horse, a penalty
kick: the world never sits still. To study motion, physics asks
two tidy questions of every moving thing: \emph{along what path?}
and \emph{how fast along it?} Path and pace --- \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:trajectory}{trajectory} and
\omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed} --- are this chapter's pair of spectacles.

\section{Trajectory: the path taken}

\begin{definition}[Trajectory]\label{def:g6:describing-motion:trajectory}
The \emph{trajectory}\index{trajectory} of a moving object is the
line traced by its journey --- the path it draws through space.
Three shapes cover most of everyday life:
\emph{straight}\index{straight trajectory} (the elevator, the
falling apple), \emph{circular}\index{circular trajectory} (the
carousel horse, the tip of a clock hand), and \emph{curved} in
freer ways (the thrown ball's arch, the swallow's swoop).
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Whose trajectory?]\label{ex:g6:describing-motion:whose}
Be careful to name \emph{which point} you follow. The carousel
horse's nose draws a circle; the whole carousel's center draws
nothing at all --- it stays put. A rolling wheel's hub glides in a
straight line while a point on its rim traces graceful hops. One
machine, many trajectories: physics follows one point at a time.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Moving compared to what?]\label{rem:g6:describing-motion:relative}
A passenger dozing on the train draws no \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:trajectory}{trajectory} at all for the
passenger opposite --- and a hundred-kilometre line across the map
for a cow watching from the field. Both descriptions are honest:
motion is always \emph{motion compared to something}, and the
describer must say what. For now we quietly compare everything to
the ground; the full power of choosing other viewpoints is a
celebrated story kept for the High School volume.
\end{remark}

\section{Uniform or varied?}

\begin{definition}[Uniform and varied motion]\label{def:g6:describing-motion:uniform}
A motion is \emph{uniform}\index{uniform motion} when it covers
equal distances in equal times --- the steady walk of a metronome
listener, the cruise of a train between stations. It is
\emph{varied}\index{varied motion} when the pace changes:
speeding up (the sprinter off the blocks), slowing down (the bus
braking), or both by turns (city traffic's endless stop and go).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Reading the dotted film]\label{prop:g6:describing-motion:dots}
Photograph a moving object at equal ticks of time --- many quick
snapshots on one \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{image} --- and its motion signs its own name in
dots:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item dots evenly spaced: \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:uniform}{uniform motion};
  \item dots spreading apart along the way: speeding up;
  \item dots crowding together: slowing down.
\end{enumerate}
Equal times sit between every pair of dots, so the gaps between
dots compare the distances --- the whole diagnosis at a glance.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[Making a dotted film]\label{met:g6:describing-motion:film}
Any phone camera can produce these portraits:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item film the moving object --- a friend cycling past, a ball
        rolling off a ramp --- holding the camera still;
  \item step through the video, pausing at equal steps (every
        half-second of playback, say);
  \item at each pause, mark the object's position on a tracing
        over the screen, or note it against fence posts in the
        background;
  \item lay out your marks in a row and read them with
        \cref{prop:g6:describing-motion:dots}.
\end{enumerate}
One warning from the professionals: hold the camera still --- a
drifting camera adds its own motion to the film and muddles whose
\omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:trajectory}{trajectory} is whose (exactly the warning of
\cref{rem:g6:describing-motion:relative}).
\end{method}

\section{Putting numbers on the pace}

\begin{example}[Speed by proportionality]\label{ex:g6:describing-motion:proportion}
A tram in \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:uniform}{uniform motion} covers \qty{9}{km} in $20$ minutes. What
\omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed} is that, in the familiar kilometres-per-hour? Proportionality
answers without any new machinery: an hour is three helpings of
$20$ minutes, so the tram covers $3 \times 9 = \qty{27}{km}$ in
one hour --- its \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed} is $27$ kilometres per hour. In a table:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|cc}
minutes & $20$ & $60$ \\
\hline
kilometres & $9$ & $27$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:uniform}{Uniform motion} \emph{is} proportionality between distance and
time --- your mathematics course and your physics course have just
shaken hands.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The pace board]\label{ex:g6:describing-motion:paces}
Everyday paces, for judging any answer: strolling, about $4$
kilometres per hour; brisk walking, $6$; easy cycling, $15$; a
city bus with stops, $20$; a car on the open road, $90$; a
\omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{high-speed} train, up to $300$. When a calculation hands you a
walker doing $40$ kilometres per hour, the pace board sends it
back.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Speed at an instant]\label{rem:g6:describing-motion:instant}
A \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:uniform}{varied motion} has no single \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed} --- the braking bus is fast,
then slow, then still. A car's speedometer needle answers a
subtler question: \emph{how fast right now?} Defining that
\omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{now-speed} precisely is genuinely deep --- it needs mathematics
you will meet in the last year of high school, and it opened one
of the greatest chapters in all of science. For this year: \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:uniform}{uniform
motions} get one honest number; \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:uniform}{varied motions} get a story.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:1}
Give the \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:trajectory}{trajectory}'s shape: a raindrop down a still windowpane;
\ the tip of the minute hand; \ a basketball's shot toward the
hoop; \ a skier's slalom.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:2}
On a rolling bicycle, what \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:trajectory}{trajectory} does the hub of the wheel
draw? And the valve on the rim? (One word and one sketch each.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:3}
Uniform or varied: a train cruising between stations; \ the same
train pulling away from the platform; \ a parachutist drifting
down at a steady rate; \ a puck sliding and slowing on rough ice?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:4}
A dotted film shows gaps of \qty{2}{cm}, \qty{2}{cm}, \qty{2}{cm},
\qty{2}{cm} between snapshots. Diagnose the motion. Another shows
\qty{1}{cm}, \qty{2}{cm}, \qty{4}{cm}, \qty{7}{cm}: diagnose that
one.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:5}
Why must the camera hold still when making a dotted film? Which
remark of the chapter is at stake?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:6}
A ferry in \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:uniform}{uniform motion} covers \qty{12}{km} in $30$ minutes.
Build the proportionality table and give its \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed} in kilometres
per hour.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:7}
A cyclist claims a steady $14$ kilometres per hour. How far do
they ride in $30$ minutes? In $15$ minutes? (Tables, not
formulas.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:8}
Against the pace board of
\cref{ex:g6:describing-motion:paces}, judge these claims: a
stroller covering \qty{12}{km} in one hour; \ a city bus covering
\qty{10}{km} in $30$ minutes; \ a \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{high-speed} train covering
\qty{75}{km} in $15$ minutes.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:9}
The dozing passenger of
\cref{rem:g6:describing-motion:relative}: describe their motion
--- \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:trajectory}{trajectory} and pace --- first for the passenger opposite,
then for the cow in the field. Why are both descriptions honest?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:10}
A metro line runs \qty{9}{km} end to end. The timetable allows
$18$ minutes including six one-minute station stops. During the
actual riding time, is the metro's average pace above or below
$45$ kilometres per hour? (Find the riding time first, then use a
table.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:11}
Design a dotted film to settle a family argument: does the dog
run at a steady pace when fetching, or sprint-and-coast? List
your steps, what you will \omterm{def:g6:measurement-in-science:measuring}{measure}, and what verdict each pattern
of dots would give.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:describing-motion:12}
A ball rolls off a table's edge. Sketch its \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:trajectory}{trajectory} from
table-edge to floor as seen by someone standing in the room.
Then argue --- viewpoints again! --- what \omterm{def:g6:describing-motion:trajectory}{trajectory} a tiny
observer riding a cart that rolls under the falling ball at just
the ball's forward pace would see. (One of the two sees a curve;
the other, something far simpler. This puzzle returns, solved, in
the High School volume.)
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Dotted Film of Line 7}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a transit engineer reads the
dotted films of a tram line; diagnosing the ride, timing the line,
catching the timetable's lie}]\label{pb:g6:describing-motion:1}
A transit engineer has filmed tram runs with a roadside camera
that marks the tram's position at every tick (one tick $=$ $10$
seconds), and hands you the films.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Reading the films.}
Film A, between two stations, shows gaps (in \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres}):
$50, 50, 50, 50, 50$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Diagnose the motion on film A.
  \item How far did the tram travel during film A's $50$ seconds?
  \item Film B, leaving a station, shows gaps
        $10, 25, 40, 50, 50$. Tell the ride's story in words.
  \item On film B, during which tick-intervals is the motion
        (nearly) uniform?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The line's true pace.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item On its uniform stretch, the tram covers \qty{50}{m} every
        $10$ seconds. Build the table up to one minute: how far
        per minute?
  \item Continue to the hour: what is the tram's cruising \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed}
        in kilometres per hour?
  \item The whole line is \qty{12}{km}. At cruising \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed} without
        stops, how many minutes end to end?
  \item The timetable allows $50$ minutes end to end. How many
        minutes does the real line lose to stations, red \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights}
        and slow zones altogether?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The advertisement on trial.}
The company's poster boasts: ``Line 7 --- clear across town at
$18$ kilometres per hour, twice a walker's best!''
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Check the poster's arithmetic against the timetable:
        \qty{12}{km} in $50$ minutes --- build the table toward
        one hour. Is ``$18$ kilometres per hour'' honest for the
        \emph{whole} journey?
  \item A pedant objects: ``No number of that kind is ever on the
        speedometer --- the tram is either cruising at $18$ or
        standing at $0$!'' Explain what a whole-journey \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed}
        really describes, and why it is still the fair number for
        a journey of many paces.
  \item The engineer proposes skipping two stations to save four
        minutes. What would the new end-to-end time be, and ---
        table again --- the new whole-journey \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed} in kilometres
        per hour? (Round sensibly.)
  \item Write the engineer's one-sentence report: which number
        should commuters trust for planning, the cruising \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed}
        or the whole-journey \omterm{def:g5:speed-distance-time:speed}{speed}, and why?
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
