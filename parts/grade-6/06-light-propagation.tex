\chapter{Rectilinear Propagation of Light}\label{ch:g6:light-propagation}

``\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{Light} travels in straight lines'' has served you for two years
--- lighting \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadows}, aiming \omterm{def:g5:mirrors-reflection:reflection}{mirrors}, aligning three cards and a
candle. This year the old law gets its formal name, its drawing
rules, and its most magical consequence: a camera with no lens, no
glass, no moving parts --- just a hole.

\section{The law, stated properly}

\begin{proposition}[Rectilinear propagation of light]\label{prop:g6:light-propagation:law}
In clear \omterm{def:g2:air-around-us:air}{air} --- and in any clear, well-mixed material such as
still water or glass --- \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} propagates in straight lines. This
is the law of \emph{rectilinear propagation}\index{rectilinear
propagation}: \emph{rectilinear} is simply Latin-flavored for
``straight-lined'', and \emph{propagation} names the traveling of
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} from place to place.
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}[Ray and beam]\label{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}
A \emph{light ray}\index{light ray} is the physicist's drawing of
one thread of \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}: a straight line with an arrow for its
direction of travel. Real lamps pour out countless threads
together: a bundle of rays is a \emph{beam}\index{beam}. Three
beam shapes cover practice: \emph{diverging} (rays spreading from
a point --- any small lamp), \emph{parallel} (rays side by side,
neither meeting nor parting --- sunlight, having traveled so far),
and \emph{converging} (rays closing toward a point --- \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}
gathered by instruments you will meet in two years).
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The law at work, everywhere]\label{ex:g6:light-propagation:sightings}
Builders sight along a straight plank's edge to judge it --- eye,
edge and far end must line up, and \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}'s straightness is the
judge's oath. Surveyors set poles in a line by eye; archers and
photographers ``line up the shot''; and the three-cards experiment
of two years ago now has a formal name: a test of \omterm{prop:g6:light-propagation:law}{rectilinear
propagation}. Every one of these trades a straight line of \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}
for a straight line of matter.
\end{example}

\section{Shadows, constructed}

\begin{method}[Constructing a shadow with a ruler]\label{met:g6:light-propagation:shadow}
For a small lamp $S$, a ball, and a screen behind it:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item draw the ray from $S$ that just grazes the ball's top
        edge, and prolong it straight to the screen;
  \item likewise the ray grazing the bottom edge (and, in three
        dimensions, all around);
  \item between the two graze-points on the screen lies the
        \emph{shadow}\index{shadow}: the zone no straight ray from
        $S$ can reach;
  \item the space between ball and screen bounded by the grazing
        rays is the \emph{\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} cone}: an eye inside it cannot
        see the lamp.
\end{enumerate}
No guessing anywhere: two ruler lines fix the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}'s exact size
and place.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[Bigger, smaller, sharper]\label{ex:g6:light-propagation:sizes}
The construction predicts what the toy-and-torch games of your
first year found by play: slide the ball toward the lamp and the
grazing rays fan wider --- giant \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}; slide it toward the
screen and they land tighter --- true-size \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}. And the
prediction is exact: with the ball halfway from lamp to screen,
the grazing rays spread twice the ball's width --- a \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}
exactly twice the ball's size. Proportionality, drawn in \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}.
\end{example}

\section{The camera with no lens}

\begin{method}[Building a pinhole camera]\label{met:g6:light-propagation:pinhole}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item take a box (a shoebox serves); in the middle of one end,
        make a clean pin-sized hole;
  \item replace the opposite end with tracing paper --- the
        screen;
  \item drape a cloth over your head and the screen end, aim the
        hole at a bright scene --- the window, a sunlit street;
  \item look at the tracing paper: the scene appears, in color,
        moving as the scene moves --- \emph{upside down}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\begin{proposition}[Why the image is upside down]\label{prop:g6:light-propagation:inverted}
\omterm{prop:g6:light-propagation:law}{Rectilinear propagation} builds the picture and inverts it in the
same stroke. From the scene's top, rays travel straight through
the hole --- and straight lines through a low hole from a high
point must continue \emph{downward}: the top lands at the screen's
bottom. From the scene's bottom, upward through the hole to the
screen's top. Every point of the scene sends its thread through
the one little gate, the threads cross at the gate, and the
picture assembles reversed --- top for bottom, left for right.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[An old and honorable box]\label{rem:g6:light-propagation:history}
The pinhole room --- \emph{camera obscura}, ``dark chamber'' ---
is centuries older than photography: astronomers watched eclipses
safely on its screen, and painters traced its \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{images} to get
perspective right. Every modern camera, and your own eye, keeps
its architecture: a small gate for the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}, a screen for the
\omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{image} --- and yes, the \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{image} on the back of your eye stands upside
down too; your brain politely turns it over. One straight-line law,
patiently followed, explains the family camera and half the
history of picture-making.
\end{remark}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:1}
Say the law of \omterm{prop:g6:light-propagation:law}{rectilinear propagation} in your own words, and
unpack both long words.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:2}
Name the three \omterm{def:g6:light-propagation:ray}{beam} shapes, with one real source or situation
each. Which shape does sunlight arrive in, and why?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:3}
A builder sights along a shelf's edge to check it is straight.
What plays the ruler in this test? Which law is trusted?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:4}
In the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} construction, what defines the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}'s edge on the
screen? What is true for an eye placed inside the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} cone?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:5}
Using \cref{ex:g6:light-propagation:sizes}: the ball sits halfway
between lamp and screen. The ball is \qty{6}{cm} wide --- how wide
is its \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}? And if the ball moves close to the screen?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:6}
In the pinhole camera, where does \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} from the scene's top land
on the screen? Trace the reason in one sentence.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:7}
A friend waves their right hand in front of your pinhole camera.
On the screen, which side waves, and which way up? (Careful ---
two reversals.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:8}
Give two historical or modern users of the camera obscura, and the
job it did for each.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:9}
Construct (sketch with a ruler): lamp at the left, a coin-shaped
blocker, screen at the right --- then move the \emph{lamp} twice
as far from the coin, screen fixed. What happens to the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}'s
size, by your own drawing? Reconcile with the fan of grazing
rays.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:10}
On a sunny day, tree \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadows} are crisp; under an overcast sky,
\omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadows} almost vanish. Explain both skies with rays and sources
--- what kind of ``lamp'' is a whole gray cloud layer?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:11}
The pinhole camera's \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{image} brightens when the hole is widened ---
but blurs. Explain both effects with threads through the gate:
what does a wide gate let each scene-point paint on the screen?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:light-propagation:12}
Under a leafy tree during a partial solar eclipse, the ground
fills with hundreds of little \omterm{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}{crescents}. Assemble the explanation
from this chapter: what are the gaps between leaves, what is each
bright patch on an ordinary day, and why do the patches turn
\omterm{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}{crescent} when the Sun does? (Next year's eclipse chapter will be
proud of you.)
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Shadow Theater}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the family shadow theater;
puppets sized by proportion, a pinhole finale; light's straight
lines run the show}]\label{pb:g6:light-propagation:1}
A bedsheet screen, one small bright lamp, cardboard puppets:
opening night is Saturday, and the stage crew works with rulers.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Setting the stage.}
The lamp stands \qty{4}{m} from the screen.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A puppet held against the screen throws a \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} of
        exactly its own size. Why?
  \item The dragon puppet is \qty{20}{cm} tall. Held halfway ---
        \qty{2}{m} from lamp and screen --- how tall is its
        \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}?
  \item For the finale the dragon must tower \qty{80}{cm} tall.
        The crew slides it toward the lamp: at $1$ \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metre} from the
        lamp, the grazing rays spread four times the puppet's
        size. Check: does \qty{80}{cm} match, and what fraction
        of the lamp--screen distance is the puppet standing at?
  \item Why must the theater's lamp be small and bare, not a
        broad frosted globe? (What would the globe do to the
        dragon's crisp edges?)
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Stagecraft with rays.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The wolf puppet must grow menacingly during its scene
        without the puppeteer's arm appearing. Which way is the
        puppet moved, and what happens to its \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}'s edges as
        it grows? (Edges soften slightly --- accept and explain
        with the lamp's small-but-not-point size.)
  \item Two puppets, held at the same distance, must not \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow}
        each other. What must be true of the straight lines from
        the lamp through each puppet?
  \item A hand slips between lamp and dragon at a third of the
        way from the lamp. Whose \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadow} appears bigger on
        the screen, hand or dragon, and why?
  \item The villain must vanish instantly. Rays in hand, give two
        ways: one moving the puppet, one using a second, nearer
        blocker.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The pinhole finale.}
For the last scene, the crew turns the theater itself into a
camera obscura: streetlight outside, pinhole in the shutter,
screen-sheet across the room.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The streetlamp hangs high outside. Where does its \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{image}
        land on the sheet --- high or low? Why?
  \item A late cyclist rides past from left to right. Which way
        does the cyclist's \omterm{prop:g5:mirrors-reflection:image}{image} glide across the sheet?
  \item The audience wants a brighter picture, so the crew
        enlarges the pinhole to a coin-sized opening. What
        becomes of the show, and why?
  \item Closing line of the program notes: in one sentence,
        name the single law that ran the whole evening --- both
        theaters, all \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadows}, and the upside-down street.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
