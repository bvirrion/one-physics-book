\chapter{The Sun--Earth--Moon System}\label{ch:g6:sun-earth-moon}

Three worlds run our sky: the Sun that gives the day, the Earth
that carries us, the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} that keeps us company. You know each
one; now we chart the \emph{system} --- who circles whom, how
fast, how far --- and pay an old debt: the promised secret of the
\omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons} is in this chapter.

\section{Three motions, three clocks}

\begin{definition}[Axis and orbit]\label{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}
The Earth's \emph{axis}\index{axis} is the imaginary line through
its poles around which it spins, like a top's spindle. An
\emph{orbit}\index{orbit} is the closed path one heavenly body
travels around another --- very nearly a circle for the Earth
around the Sun, and for the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} around the Earth.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The three motions]\label{prop:g6:sun-earth-moon:motions}
The whole calendar hangs on three steady motions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the Earth \emph{spins} on its \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{axis}: one turn in $24$
        \omterm{ex:g2:measuring-time:clock}{hours} --- the day, parading Sun and \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars} across our sky;
  \item the Earth \emph{\omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbits}} the Sun: one lap in about $365$
        days and a quarter --- the year, sliding the Sun's arc up
        and down through the \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons};
  \item the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} \emph{\omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbits}} the Earth: one lap in about a
        month --- and, as its sunlit half faces us by turns,
        parading its phases through your old \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} diary.
\end{enumerate}
Three motions, three clocks: day, month, year --- humankind's
oldest timekeepers, all still running.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[The quarter day and the leap year]\label{ex:g6:sun-earth-moon:leap}
The Earth's lap takes $365$ days \emph{and about a quarter} ---
nature owes no loyalty to round numbers. Calendars pay the debt by
saving the quarters: four years of quarter-days make one whole
day, added to the calendar every fourth year --- the $366$-day
\emph{leap year}, with its extra day at February's end. Miss this
bookkeeping for a century and the calendar drifts weeks away from
the \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons} --- as older calendars, painfully, discovered.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The system, to scale]\label{ex:g6:sun-earth-moon:scale}
Numbers for the notebook: the Earth is about \qty{12800}{km}
across; the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}, about a quarter of that, \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbits} about
\qty{384000}{km} away --- thirty Earths laid side by side. The
Sun is about $109$ Earths across and sits $150$ million
kilometres away. Shrink the Earth to a \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{centimetre} marble and the
model says: \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}, a peppercorn $30$ \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{centimetres} away; Sun, a ball
over a \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metre} wide, standing more than a hundred \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres} down the
street. Our whole neighborhood is mostly emptiness --- as the park
scale model of the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:family}{Solar System} already whispered.
\end{example}

\section{The debt repaid: why seasons}

\begin{proposition}[The tilt makes the seasons]\label{prop:g6:sun-earth-moon:tilt}
The Earth's \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{axis} is not upright on its \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbit}: it leans, by about a
quarter of a right angle --- and it keeps leaning \emph{the same
way} all around the year's lap, like a faithful \omterm{def:g4:magnets-and-compass:compass}{compass} of the
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}. So for half the lap, our half of the world leans
\emph{toward} the Sun: the Sun rides high, days run long ---
summer. Half a lap later, the same unchanged lean points us
\emph{away}: low Sun, short days --- winter. The \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons} are not
the Sun changing, nor the distance: they are a tilted \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}
carrying its unchanged lean around its \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{star}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{method}[The globe and the lamp]\label{met:g6:sun-earth-moon:globe}
See the whole machine on a table:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item set a lamp mid-table as the Sun, and carry a tilted globe
        (or a ball on a slanted skewer) slowly around it,
        \emph{keeping the skewer pointing at the same corner of
        the room} the whole way;
  \item at each quarter of the lap, stop and look at your home's
        latitude: watch how much of its daily spin-circle lies in
        the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light};
  \item on one side of the lamp, your hemisphere basks tilted-in
        --- long lit arcs, high lamp: summer; opposite, tilted-out
        --- short arcs, low lamp: winter;
  \item at the two stations between, neither pole leans in: day
        and night share equally --- spring and autumn.
\end{enumerate}
The one discipline that makes it work: never let the skewer swing
--- the \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{axis}' faithfulness \emph{is} the \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons}.
\end{method}

\begin{example}[The old clues, accounted for]\label{ex:g6:sun-earth-moon:clues}
Every observation of your \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:sundial}{sundial} years now reports to the tilt.
Summer's high arc: our hemisphere leans in, so the Sun stands
taller at noon. Winter's stubby arc and long \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:shadow}{shadows}: leaning out,
the Sun skims low. Opposite \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons} across the equator ---
December beach holidays in the south while the north shovels snow:
when one hemisphere leans in, the other must lean out. Even the
equal days of spring and autumn fall out of the lamp-and-globe
walk. One quarter-of-a-right-angle lean runs the whole show.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Not distance --- and the proof]\label{rem:g6:sun-earth-moon:notdistance}
The Earth's \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbit} is so nearly circular that sunward distance
changes little --- and, delightfully, we ride slightly
\emph{closer} to the Sun in the northern winter. The \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons} care
about the \emph{slant} of the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}, not the length of the trip:
leaning in, sunlight strikes steep and concentrated; leaning out,
the same \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} spreads thin across the ground. A torch held
straight-on versus slanted against a wall shows the difference in
one second --- steep \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} bites, grazing \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} barely warms.
\end{remark}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:9}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:10}

no lean at all. Describe a year: what happens to \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons}, to the

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:11}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:12}
In midsummer, regions near the North Pole keep the Sun above the
horizon at midnight --- the famous ``midnight sun'' --- and in

with the tilted spin-circles of
\cref{met:g6:sun-earth-moon:globe}: what does the lean do to a
polar town's daily circle in June, and in December?
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The Sports-Field Model}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the class builds the
Sun--Earth--Moon system on the sports field; marbles, peppercorns
and a very long walk; the tilt joins the model}]\label{pb:g6:sun-earth-moon:1}
The class builds the system to scale: the Earth is a marble
\qty{1}{cm} across. Handy true numbers: Earth \qty{12800}{km}
across; \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} \qty{3200}{km} across, orbiting \qty{384000}{km}
away; Sun $109$ Earths across, $150$ million kilometres away.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Casting the parts.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item In the model, \qty{1}{cm} stands for how many kilometres?
  \item The \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} is about a quarter of the Earth's width. How
        wide is the model \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} --- and what kitchen item plays it
        well?
  \item The true \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} distance is $30$ Earth-widths. Where does
        the model \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} stand from the marble?
  \item The model Sun is $109$ marbles across. How wide is that,
        in \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{centimetres} --- and what playground object is about
        that size?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The long walk.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The Sun's distance is $150$ million km. In the model ---
        divide by the answer to question 1 --- how many
        \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{centimetres} is that, and how many \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres}?
  \item Will the model fit on a \qty{100}{m} sports field? Where
        must the Sun-ball stand?
  \item A pupil walks the Earth--Sun stretch at $1$ \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metre} per
        second. About how many minutes does the walk take?
  \item Standing at the marble and looking back at the
        metre-wide Sun-ball $117$ \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metres} away, the class is
        surprised: it looks about the same size as the
        \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{quarter-centimetre} Moon-grain $30$ \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{centimetres} from the
        marble. Check the two ratios --- and connect this
        coincidence to something that happens, rarely and
        gloriously, in the real sky.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Making it run.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item To animate the model honestly, how long should the
        marble take to circle the Sun-ball, and the pea to circle
        the marble, if one real day is played by one second?
        (Count roughly: a year as $365$ days, a Moon-lap as $27$
        days.)
  \item The marble must also spin. At one real day per second,
        how fast is that in the model --- and why will nobody see
        it on a \qty{1}{cm} marble?
  \item A skewer through the marble plays the \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{axis}. At what slant,
        and pointing how, must the skewer be carried around the
        whole lap?
  \item Write the model's honest disclaimer: name one thing the
        sports-field model shows truly, and one thing no
        field-sized model can show at once (sizes and distances
        together? motions? \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light}?). Justify with your numbers.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
