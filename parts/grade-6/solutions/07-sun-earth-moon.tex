\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g6:sun-earth-moon} --- The Sun--Earth--Moon System}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:1}
The Earth spins on its \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{axis} --- the day. The Earth \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbits} the Sun
--- the year. The \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbits} the Earth --- the month.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:2}
The imaginary line through the poles around which the Earth spins.
It leans by about a quarter of a right angle --- and keeps leaning
the same way all around the \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbit}: lean plus faithfulness make the
\omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:3}
The Earth's lap takes $365$ days and a quarter; four saved
quarters make the leap year's extra day. Without it the calendar
would drift through the \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons} --- about a day every four years,
weeks in a century.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:4}
About $30$ Earths to the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}; about $109$ Earths across the
Sun's face.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:5}
The skewer plays the \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{axis}, and the real \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{axis} keeps its lean fixed
against the \omterm{def:g4:solar-system:star}{stars}. Let it swing --- always leaning toward the lamp,
say --- and the model shows an endless summer: the \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons}
vanish with the faithfulness.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:6}
The opposite one: when our hemisphere leans toward the Sun, the
other must lean away --- one tilt cannot favor both ends at once.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:7}
Straight-on, the torch's \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} lands steep and concentrated ---
a small, bright, biting patch. Slanted, the same \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} smears
across a long stretch of wall, thin and feeble. Leaning in gives
us the steep, concentrated kind.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:8}
The \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon}'s own \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbit} around the Earth: as it circles, we view its
one sunlit half from changing angles --- \omterm{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}{crescent} to full and
back. Motion three runs the phase show.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:9}
First: the northern winter happens while the Earth rides slightly
\emph{closer} to the Sun --- distance points the wrong way.
Second: the two hemispheres have opposite \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons} at the same
moment and the same distance --- no shared distance could do
that; only the tilt, favoring one end and disfavoring the other,
can.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:10}
With no lean, every station of the lap looks alike: the Sun
climbs to the same noon height all year, days and nights stay
equal everywhere, and the year loses its \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:year}{seasons} --- one long
eternal spring, the \omterm{def:g3:sun-days-seasons:sundial}{sundial}'s arc frozen, the calendar's chief
drama canceled.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:11}
While the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} lapped its $27$ days, the Earth advanced along its
own \omterm{def:g6:sun-earth-moon:axisorbit}{orbit} --- so the Sun's direction shifted, and the \omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} needs
about two and a half extra days of travel to catch up to the same
lineup with the Sun that makes a \omterm{ex:g2:moon-in-the-sky:shapes}{full moon}. Lap and lineup are
different finishing posts.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g6:sun-earth-moon:12}
A polar town's daily circle is small and, thanks to the June
lean, can lie \emph{entirely} in the lit half: the town spins
around without ever leaving daylight --- midnight sun. In
December the same lean tips its little circle entirely into the
dark half: whole spins without a \omterm{def:g1:day-and-night:daynight}{sunrise}. The tilt does gently to
every latitude what it does absolutely at the poles.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:g6:sun-earth-moon:1}
\textbf{1.} \qty{12800}{km} per \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{centimetre}.
\textbf{2.} About \qty{0.25}{cm} --- a peppercorn (or a grain).
\textbf{3.} $30 \times 1 = \qty{30}{cm}$ from the marble.
\textbf{4.} $109$ cm --- about a \omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{metre}: a hula hoop or a large
beach ball.
\textbf{5.} $150000000 \div 12800 \approx \qty{11700}{cm}$ ---
about \qty{117}{m}.
\textbf{6.} Barely not: the Sun-ball must stand some \qty{17}{m}
beyond the far goal line of a \qty{100}{m} field.
\textbf{7.} About $117$ seconds --- roughly two minutes.
\textbf{8.} Sun: $109 \div 11700 \approx 0.009$; Moon-grain:
$0.25 \div 30 \approx 0.008$ --- nearly equal: the two look the
same size from the marble. So too in our sky --- and when the
\omterm{def:g2:moon-in-the-sky:moon}{Moon} passes exactly before the Sun, it covers it almost exactly:
a total solar eclipse, nature exploiting the coincidence.
\textbf{9.} The marble's lap: about $365$ seconds --- six
minutes of walking; the grain's lap around the marble: about
$27$ seconds.
\textbf{10.} One full spin per second --- and on a smooth
\omterm{def:g2:measuring-length:units}{centimetre} marble with no markings, a spin leaves nothing for
the eye to catch: painting a dot on it is the honest fix.
\textbf{11.} Slanted by about a quarter of a right angle from
upright --- and carried around the whole lap pointing always at
the same distant corner of the field, never turning to face the
Sun-ball.
\textbf{12.} For example: the model shows distances and sizes
\emph{together}, truly --- the great emptiness is its triumph.
What it cannot show at once is the motions at true pace (a
six-minute ``year'' is already a wild speed-up; the real marble
would creep) --- nor, of course, the \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{light} and warmth: no
metre-wide ball \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} a sports field the way the Sun \omterm{def:g1:light-and-shadows:source}{lights} a
\omterm{def:g4:solar-system:planet}{planet}.
\end{solution}
