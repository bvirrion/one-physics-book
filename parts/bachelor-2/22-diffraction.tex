\chapter{Fraunhofer Diffraction and Spatial Filtering}\label{ch:b2:diffraction}

Squint at a street lamp through the gap between two fingers and the
lamp stretches into a band of fringes; look at a star through the
finest telescope and you see not a point but a small disc ringed with
faint circles. Light passing through an opening spreads --- the
narrower the opening, the wider the spread --- and no lens can focus it
tighter than this spread allows. This is \emph{\omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction}}, the reason
a microscope cannot see a virus, a telescope's resolving power grows
with its diameter, and a laser beam cannot stay parallel. This chapter
computes the \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction} pattern of an aperture in the far field, the
limit it sets on every optical instrument, the way it combines with
interference, and the idea --- Abbe's --- that turns the focal plane of a
lens into a place where an image can be filtered.

\section{The Huygens--Fresnel principle and the Fraunhofer regime}

\begin{definition}[Huygens--Fresnel principle]\label{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}
\index{Huygens--Fresnel principle}\index{diffraction}\index{Fraunhofer diffraction}
Every point of a \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{wavefront} (or of an aperture lit by a wave) acts as a
secondary source emitting a spherical wavelet, in phase with the wave
there; the wave beyond is the coherent sum of the wavelets. For an
aperture $\Sigma$ lit by a plane wave of amplitude $a$, the amplitude
reaching a far point is $\underline s(M) = K\iint_\Sigma\eu^{-\iu\varphi(P, M)}\dd S$, with
$\varphi(P, M)$ the phase of the path from the aperture point $P$ to $M$
($K$ a constant). \emph{Fraunhofer} (far-field) diffraction is the case
where $M$ is at infinity --- or in the focal plane of a lens --- so that
the rays from all points of the aperture toward $M$ are parallel, in
the direction $\vect u$, and
\[
\varphi(P, M) = \varphi_0 - \frac{2\pi}\lambda\,\vect{OP}\cdot\vect u , \qquad
\underline s(\vect u) \propto \iint_\Sigma\eu^{\iu(2\pi/\lambda)\vect{OP}\cdot\vect u}\dd S .
\]
The pattern is observed at infinity, or at the focus $F'$ of a lens,
where the direction $\vect u$ of small angles $(\alpha, \beta)$ maps to the
point $(f\alpha, f\beta)$.
\end{definition}
\admitted

\begin{remark}[When is it far enough?]\label{rem:b2:diffraction:fresnelnumber}
\index{Fresnel number}
For an aperture of size $D$ seen at distance $L$ without a lens, the
rays are parallel enough when the quadratic term of
\cref{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:12} is negligible, $D^2/L \ll \lambda$ ---
the \emph{\omterm{rem:b2:diffraction:fresnelnumber}{Fresnel number}} $D^2/\lambda L \ll 1$. A \qty{0.1}{mm} slit at
\qty{500}{nm} needs $L \gg \qty{2}{cm}$; a \qty{1}{cm} aperture, $L \gg \qty{200}{m}$ ---
hence the lens, which brings infinity to its focal plane. Closer in
(Fresnel \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction}) the patterns are more complex; the shadow of a
straight edge, for instance, has fringes along its lit side.
\end{remark}

\section{The single slit and the circular aperture}

\begin{proposition}[Diffraction by a slit]\label{prop:b2:diffraction:slit}
\index{single-slit diffraction}\index{sinc function}
A slit of width $b$ (long along $y$) lit at normal incidence sends in
the direction $\theta$ (in the plane perpendicular to the slit) the
intensity
\[
I(\theta) = I_0\,\operatorname{sinc}^2\Bigl(\frac{\pi b\sin\theta}\lambda\Bigr) , \qquad \operatorname{sinc}x = \frac{\sin x}x :
\]
a bright central maximum of angular half-width $\lambda/b$ (first zeros at
$\sin\theta = \pm\lambda/b$), containing $90\%$ of the light, and secondary maxima
at about $\sin\theta = \pm1.43\lambda/b$, $\pm2.46\lambda/b$, \dots, of relative
intensities $4.7\%$, $1.6\%$, \dots --- the narrower the slit, the wider
the pattern. In the focal plane of a lens of focal length $f$ the
central maximum is $2\lambda f/b$ wide.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\underline s(\theta) \propto \int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\eu^{\iu kx\sin\theta}\dd x = b\,\operatorname{sinc}(kb\sin\theta/2)$
with $k = 2\pi/\lambda$; square. Zeros where $b\sin\theta = m\lambda$, $m \ne 0$: the
wavelets from the two halves of the slit then cancel pairwise.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Circular aperture; the Airy disc]\label{prop:b2:diffraction:airy}
\index{Airy disc}\index{Rayleigh criterion (resolution)}\index{resolving power (instrument)}
A circular aperture of diameter $D$ gives a central bright disc, the
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:airy}{Airy disc}}, surrounded by faint rings; the first dark ring is at
\[
\sin\theta = 1.22\,\frac\lambda D ,
\]
and the disc holds $84\%$ of the light. Every instrument with an
entrance pupil of diameter $D$ --- eye, camera, telescope, microscope
objective --- turns a point source into an \omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:airy}{Airy disc} of that angular
radius; two points are \emph{resolved} (Rayleigh's criterion) if their
angular separation exceeds $1.22\lambda/D$: the resolving power of the
instrument.
\end{proposition}
\admitted

\begin{example}[Eye, telescope, microscope]\label{ex:b2:diffraction:instruments}
The eye ($D = \qty{3}{mm}$, $\lambda = \qty{550}{nm}$): $1.22\lambda/D = \qty{2.2e-4}{rad}$, about
\qty{45}{''} --- a \qty{1}{mm} detail at \qty{4}{m}, which is indeed about the
acuity of a good eye: evolution matched the retina's cells to the
\omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction} limit. A \qty{2.4}{m} telescope: \qty{0.06}{''}, a thousand times
better, if the atmosphere lets it (it does not, from the ground:
\qty{1}{''} of "seeing", whence space telescopes and adaptive optics). A
radio dish of \qty{100}{m} at $\lambda = \qty{21}{cm}$: \qty{9}{'}, worse than the eye;
interferometers across continents restore the resolution. A
microscope objective of aperture angle $\alpha$ resolves $0.61\lambda/n\sin\alpha$
--- about \qty{0.2}{\micro m} in the visible, whatever the magnification: the
limit that drove microscopy to electrons and to X-rays.
\end{example}

\section{Diffraction and interference together}

\begin{proposition}[Young's slits of finite width; the grating's envelope]\label{prop:b2:diffraction:combined}
\index{envelope (diffraction)}\index{missing orders}
Two slits of width $b$ whose centres are $a$ apart give
\[
I(\theta) = 4I_0\,\operatorname{sinc}^2\Bigl(\frac{\pi b\sin\theta}\lambda\Bigr)\cos^2\Bigl(\frac{\pi a\sin\theta}\lambda\Bigr) :
\]
Young's fringes (spacing $\lambda/a$ in $\sin\theta$) under the single-slit
envelope (width $2\lambda/b$); $a/b$ fringes fill the central lobe, and the
fringes at $a\sin\theta = p\lambda$ that fall on a zero of the envelope
($b\sin\theta = m\lambda$) are \emph{missing}. A grating of $N$ slits of width
$b$ likewise has its principal maxima modulated by the same envelope ---
which the blaze of \cref{ch:b2:gratings} tilts toward the order in use.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The aperture integral splits into a sum over the two slits of the same
integral shifted by $\pm a/2$: $\underline s \propto b\,\operatorname{sinc}(\pi b\sin\theta/\lambda)
\times2\cos(\pi a\sin\theta/\lambda)$. For $N$ slits the second factor is the
$N$-wave sum.
\end{proof}

\section{The Fourier plane and spatial filtering}

\begin{proposition}[The lens as a Fourier analyser]\label{prop:b2:diffraction:fourier}
\index{Fourier plane}\index{spatial frequency}\index{spatial filtering}\index{Abbe's theory}
A transparency of transmittance $t(x, y)$ in the front focal plane of a
lens, lit by a plane wave, produces in the back focal plane the
amplitude
\[
\underline s(X, Y) \propto \iint t(x, y)\,\eu^{-\iu2\pi(xX + yY)/\lambda f}\dd x\,\dd y
\]
--- its two-dimensional Fourier transform, each point $(X, Y)$ of the
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:fourier}{Fourier plane}} collecting the light diffracted at the angles
$(X/f, Y/f)$, i.e.\ the \emph{spatial frequencies} $(X/\lambda f, Y/\lambda f)$ of
the object: fine details (high frequencies) far from the axis, coarse
ones near it. A second lens re-forms the image from that plane; whatever
is blocked or altered there is filtered out of the image
(\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:fourier}{spatial filtering}}, \omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:fourier}{Abbe's theory} of the microscope): a pinhole
on the axis keeps only the low frequencies (smoothing, "cleaning" a
laser beam); a stop on the axis removes the uniform background and
keeps the edges (dark-field, strioscopy); a \omterm{prop:b2:plane-waves-polarization:plates}{quarter-wave plate} on the
axis turns phase objects, invisible otherwise, into intensity contrast
(phase contrast, Zernike).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Justification]
In the Fraunhofer integral, $\vect{OP}\cdot\vect u = x\alpha + y\beta$ with the
direction mapped to $(X, Y) = (f\alpha, f\beta)$; the transmittance weights
each secondary source. (The Fourier transform itself is studied in the
Year 3 mathematics volume; here it is only the name of this integral.)
For a periodic object of period $d$ the transform is the set of grating
orders at $X = p\lambda f/d$: the image can show the period only if at least
the orders $\pm1$ pass through the lens --- Abbe's condition, which is
the microscope's resolution limit again.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Cleaning a beam, seeing the invisible]\label{ex:b2:diffraction:filtering}
A laser beam carries dust and ripples: focus it through a pinhole of
a few micrometres --- the size of the central \omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:airy}{Airy disc} of the clean
Gaussian beam --- and everything else, which lies outside, is blocked;
the re-collimated beam is smooth: a \emph{spatial filter}. A living
cell is transparent; it changes only the phase of the light. Blocking
the axial spot makes its edges bright on black; Zernike's plate
shifts the undiffracted light by a quarter wave so that it interferes
with the diffracted light in intensity: the cell's interior appears,
and biology got its microscope (1953 Nobel prize).
\end{example}

\begin{method}[Diffraction estimates]\label{met:b2:diffraction:method}
(1) Angular spread $\sim\lambda/b$ for an aperture of size $b$ ($1.22\lambda/D$ for
a circle); the pattern's size at distance $L$ or focal length $f$ is
$\lambda L/b$, $\lambda f/b$. (2) Check the Fraunhofer condition $b^2/\lambda L \ll 1$ or
use a lens. (3) Combine: \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction} envelope $\times$ interference
fringes. (4) Resolution: two points need $\Delta\theta > 1.22\lambda/D$; a periodic
object needs its first orders to enter the pupil. (5) The \omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:fourier}{Fourier
plane} picture: what to block to remove which detail.
\end{method}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:1}
A slit of \qty{0.10}{mm} lit by a He--Ne laser (\qty{633}{nm}); screen at
\qty{2.0}{m}. Width of the central maximum; position of the first two
secondary maxima; \omterm{rem:b2:diffraction:fresnelnumber}{Fresnel number} --- is the screen far enough? Same
slit with a lens of $f = \qty{50}{cm}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:2}
Resolving power (Rayleigh) of: the eye (\qty{3}{mm}, \qty{550}{nm}); a
\qty{10}{cm} amateur telescope; the \qty{2.4}{m} Hubble mirror; a \qty{64}{m}
radio dish at \qty{6}{cm}; a \qty{50}{mm} camera lens at $f/8$ (pupil \qty{6}{mm}):
size of the \omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:airy}{Airy disc} on the sensor, compared with a \qty{4}{\micro m} pixel.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:3}
Two slits of width $b = \qty{0.05}{mm}$, centres $a = \qty{0.25}{mm}$ apart, $\lambda =
\qty{500}{nm}$, lens $f = \qty{1}{m}$: fringe spacing; width of the central
envelope; number of fringes in it; which orders are missing?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:4}
A laser beam of diameter \qty{2}{mm} at \qty{633}{nm} propagates to the Moon
(\qty{3.8e8}{m}): \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction} angle $\sim1.22\lambda/D$, diameter of the spot;
with a \qty{3.5}{m} telescope as emitter; why are lunar ranging returns so
faint (the retroreflector, \qty{1}{m}, sends the light back with its own
\omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction})?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:5}
\emph{The slit in detail.} (a) Carry out the Fraunhofer integral for
the slit and obtain $\operatorname{sinc}^2$. (b) Show the secondary maxima
are at $\tan x = x$ ($x = \pi b\sin\theta/\lambda$), the first near $x = 1.43\pi$, and
compute its relative intensity. (c) Fraction of the energy in the
central lobe (admit $\int_{-\pi}^\pi\operatorname{sinc}^2 = 0.903\pi$ and
$\int_{-\infty}^\infty\operatorname{sinc}^2 = \pi$). (d) The slit is lit at incidence
$\theta_0$: show the pattern is the same, centred on the direction $\theta_0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:6}
\emph{Microscope.} An objective of \omterm{prop:b2:guided-waves:fibre}{numerical aperture} $n\sin\alpha = 1.4$
(oil immersion) at \qty{500}{nm}: smallest resolved distance $0.61\lambda/n\sin
\alpha$; with blue light (\qty{400}{nm}); in the ultraviolet (\qty{250}{nm}); why
does a $1000\times$ eyepiece not help (the eye's own limit at \qty{25}{cm} is
\qty{0.1}{mm})? What do electron microscopes change ($\lambda = \qty{4}{pm}$ at
\qty{100}{keV})?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:7}
\emph{Rectangular and circular.} (a) A rectangular aperture $b \times h$:
show the pattern is $\operatorname{sinc}^2(\pi b\sin\theta_x/\lambda)\operatorname{sinc}^2(\pi h
\sin\theta_y/\lambda)$: a cross, wider along the narrower side. (b) A square of
\qty{0.2}{mm} with $f = \qty{1}{m}$: size of the central square. (c) For a
circle of the same width the first zero is at $1.22\lambda/D$ instead of $\lambda/b$:
why further out (think of the light concentrated near the centre of a
disc)? (d) A telescope's secondary mirror is held by four vanes: what do
they add to the image of a bright star?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:8}
\emph{Abbe.} A grating of period $d = \qty{2}{\micro m}$ is observed with a
microscope objective of aperture angle $\alpha$ in air, $\lambda = \qty{550}{nm}$.
(a) Angles of its orders $\pm1$, $\pm2$. (b) Minimum $\sin\alpha$ for the image to
show the period (orders $\pm1$ must enter). (c) With $\sin\alpha = 0.4$: what
does the image show --- is it a faithful grating? (d) Oblique
illumination lets the zero order and \emph{one} first order through:
minimum $\sin\alpha$ then, and the gain.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:9}
\emph{Spatial filter.} A \qty{2}{mm} laser beam ($\lambda = \qty{633}{nm}$) is
focused by a lens of $f = \qty{20}{mm}$ through a pinhole. (a) Diameter of
the central \omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:airy}{Airy disc} at the focus; pinhole diameter to let it through
(take $1.5\times$). (b) A dust speck of \qty{50}{\micro m} on the beam
diffracts light at what angle, and where does that light land in the
focal plane --- through the pinhole or not? (c) Power lost if the beam
is Gaussian and the pinhole passes $99\%$ of it; why is the emerging
beam smooth? (d) Why does the pinhole have to be centred to a few
micrometres?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:10}
\emph{Babinet.} Two complementary screens (a slit, and an opaque
strip of the same width) are lit by a plane wave. (a) Show that, away
from the direction of the incident beam, their Fraunhofer patterns
are identical (the sum of the two amplitudes is that of the
unobstructed wave, zero off-axis). (b) The pattern of a hair of
diameter \qty{80}{\micro m} at \qty{2}{m} with a laser: fringe spacing, and the
hair's diameter from it --- a standard measurement. (c) What is seen
on the axis itself? (d) Why do small droplets in a thin cloud produce a
corona of coloured rings around the Moon, and what does the ring
radius give?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:11}
\emph{Dark field and phase contrast.} A transparent object has
$t(x) = \eu^{\iu\varphi(x)} \approx 1 + \iu\varphi(x)$ with $|\varphi| \ll 1$. (a) Intensity of
its ordinary image: show it is uniform to first order --- invisible. (b)
In the \omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:fourier}{Fourier plane} the "1" is the axial spot and $\iu\varphi$ the
diffracted light around it: if a stop blocks the axial spot, image
intensity $\propto\varphi^2$ --- dark field; its weakness. (c) If instead the
axial spot is retarded by $\pi/2$ (multiplied by $\iu$): image $\propto|1 +
\varphi|^2 \approx 1 + 2\varphi$ --- phase contrast, linear in $\varphi$. (d) Why is the
Zernike plate often also absorbing (it attenuates the axial spot)?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:diffraction:12}
\emph{Resolution of a spectrograph's slit image and the grating.} A
grating of width $W$ is the aperture of the spectrograph: show that the
\omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction} pattern of that aperture, $\operatorname{sinc}^2(\pi W\sin\theta/\lambda)$
(for the beam leaving at $\theta \approx 0$), has the angular half-width
$\lambda/W$, and that converted to wavelength through the dispersion $p/a\cos
\theta$ this is $\Delta\lambda = \lambda/pN$ --- the resolving power of
\cref{ch:b2:gratings}, seen as \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction}. (b) A telescope of
diameter $D$ and a grating of width $W$: the grating must be wider than
$D\,f_{\text{coll}}/f_{\text{tel}}$: why? (c) Explain in one sentence why
every "resolving power" in optics is $\sim$(size of the aperture)/$\lambda$.
(d) What does the slit add, and when does it dominate?
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: What can be resolved}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- the diffraction limit at four
scales: the eye, a telescope, a microscope, and the laser beam that
must be cleaned before it is used}]
\label{pb:b2:diffraction:1}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The eye.}
Pupil $D = \qty{3}{mm}$ by day, \qty{7}{mm} at night; $\lambda = \qty{550}{nm}$; the
retina's cones are \qty{2}{\micro m} apart at the fovea, the eye's focal
length is \qty{17}{mm}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Angular radius of the \omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:airy}{Airy disc} by day; its linear radius on
        the retina; compare with the cone spacing --- is the retina
        matched to the optics?
  \item Smallest separation of two points resolved at \qty{25}{cm}; at
        \qty{10}{m}; can you read a \qty{1}{mm} text at arm's length?
  \item At night the pupil opens to \qty{7}{mm}: \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction} limit then;
        why does vision nevertheless get worse (aberrations of the
        lens at full aperture, rods coarser than cones)?
  \item Two car headlights \qty{1.5}{m} apart: distance at which they merge
        into one light.
  \item A \qty{1}{mm} hole held before the eye: what does \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction} do,
        and why does a pinhole nevertheless help a short-sighted eye
        (depth of field)?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The telescope.}
A \qty{1}{m} telescope, $f = \qty{10}{m}$, with a camera of \qty{10}{\micro m}
pixels; $\lambda = \qty{550}{nm}$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{Diffraction} limit in seconds of arc; Airy radius on the
        detector; pixels per \omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:airy}{Airy disc}.
  \item The atmosphere blurs images to \qty{1}{''}: how much of the
        telescope's resolution is lost? Diameter of telescope for which
        the \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction} limit equals the seeing.
  \item Adaptive optics corrects the atmosphere at \qty{2.2}{\micro m}:
        \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction} limit there; why is it easier in the infrared?
  \item A binary star with components \qty{0.15}{''} apart: resolved with
        adaptive optics at \qty{2.2}{\micro m}? At \qty{550}{nm} from space?
  \item The Moon is \qty{3.8e5}{km} away: smallest crater this telescope
        could resolve (\omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction} only); and the footprint of a lunar
        lander (\qty{4}{m})?
  \item Light-gathering power of the telescope relative to the
        night eye (\qty{7}{mm}); what does that buy, and what not?
  \item The light of a star through the four vanes holding the
        secondary mirror: describe the image.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The microscope.}
Objective of \omterm{prop:b2:guided-waves:fibre}{numerical aperture} $\mathrm{NA} = n\sin\alpha = 0.9$ (dry) or $1.4$
(oil), $\lambda = \qty{550}{nm}$; the resolved distance is $0.61\lambda/\mathrm{NA}$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Resolved distance with each objective; number of line pairs
        per millimetre.
  \item A bacterium of \qty{1}{\micro m} with internal structure at
        \qty{0.2}{\micro m}: what is seen with each? A virus of \qty{0.1}{\micro m}?
  \item Abbe: a periodic structure of period $d$ is imaged only if its
        first orders at $\sin\theta = \lambda/d$ enter the objective: minimum $d$
        for $\mathrm{NA} = 1.4$; compare with $0.61\lambda/\mathrm{NA}$.
  \item Why does oil between the slide and the objective improve the
        resolution?
  \item Ultraviolet at \qty{250}{nm} and an electron microscope at \qty{4}{pm}:
        resolved distances in the same formula (for electrons the
        practical NA is \qty{0.01}{}): compare.
  \item A cell is transparent: in bright field its image is empty.
        Explain what the phase-contrast plate in the objective's
        \omterm{prop:b2:diffraction:fourier}{Fourier plane} does, in two sentences.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The beam.}
A laser at \qty{532}{nm}, beam diameter \qty{1.5}{mm}, is to be expanded to
\qty{30}{mm} and cleaned.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{Diffraction} half-angle of the raw beam ($\sim1.22\lambda/D$); its
        diameter after \qty{100}{m} with no optics.
  \item A lens of $f_1 = \qty{10}{mm}$ focuses it: diameter of the focal spot;
        pinhole diameter chosen ($1.5\times$ the spot).
  \item A second lens of $f_2$ recollimates at \qty{30}{mm}: $f_2$; new
        \omterm{def:b2:diffraction:huygens}{diffraction} angle; diameter after \qty{1}{km}.
  \item Dust of \qty{100}{\micro m} on the first lens diffracts at what angle;
        where does that light fall at the pinhole plane, and what
        happens to it?
  \item The pinhole passes $99\%$ of a Gaussian beam: power lost, and
        where it goes.
  \item Why must the expanded beam be \emph{larger} to stay parallel
        (relate $\lambda/D$ to the distance over which the beam doubles,
        $\sim D^2/\lambda$)?
  \item Sum up the four limits of this problem in one table: aperture,
        wavelength, $\lambda/D$, what it limits.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
