\chapter{Thermal Radiation}\label{ch:b2:thermal-radiation}

Everything warm glows. A hand, a wall, a glass of water at room
temperature radiate invisibly, at wavelengths near \qty{10}{\micro m}, a
few hundred watts per square metre; heat a poker and at
\qty{600}{\celsius} it glows dull red, at \qty{1000}{\celsius} orange, at
\qty{2500}{\celsius} --- the filament of a bulb --- white; the Sun's
surface at \qty{5800}{K} radiates yellow-white, sixty megawatts per
square metre, and that light, after eight minutes of travel, is what
keeps the Earth at a temperature where water is liquid. Radiation is
the third mode of heat transfer and the only one that crosses empty
space; it is also the place where classical physics failed and Planck,
in 1900, had to introduce the quantum. This chapter states the law he
found, draws from it the two results that one uses constantly ---
Wien's displacement and the Stefan--Boltzmann law --- and applies them
to the exchanges between bodies, to the Sun and the Earth, and to the
one-layer model of the \omterm{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:greenhouse}{greenhouse effect} that fixes our climate.

\section{Thermal radiation and the black body}

\begin{definition}[Thermal radiation, absorptivity, black body]\label{def:b2:thermal-radiation:blackbody}
Every body at temperature $T$ emits electromagnetic radiation from the
thermal agitation of its charges: its \emph{thermal
radiation}\index{thermal radiation}. Its surface also receives
radiation from its surroundings and absorbs a fraction $\alpha$ of it
(the \emph{absorptivity}, between $0$ and $1$, in general depending on
the wavelength), reflecting or transmitting the rest. A \emph{black
body}\index{black body} absorbs everything ($\alpha = 1$ at every
wavelength). A small hole in the wall of a closed cavity is the
practical black body: radiation entering it is absorbed at the walls
before it can find the way out.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Equilibrium radiation and Kirchhoff's law]\label{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:kirchhoff}
Inside a closed cavity whose walls are at $T$, the radiation is in
equilibrium with the walls and is \emph{universal}: isotropic,
unpolarised, with a spectral energy density $u_\nu(T)$ (energy per unit
volume and unit frequency) that depends on $T$ and $\nu$ only, not on
the cavity's material or shape. The radiation leaving a small hole in
such a cavity is the \emph{black-body radiation} at $T$. A surface that
absorbs the fraction $\alpha_\nu$ of the radiation it receives at the
frequency $\nu$ emits, at that frequency, the fraction
$\varepsilon_\nu = \alpha_\nu$ of what a \omterm{def:b2:thermal-radiation:blackbody}{black body} at the same
temperature would emit (its \emph{emissivity}\index{emissivity}):
\emph{good absorbers are good emitters}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Universality: connect two cavities at the same $T$ by a hole with a
filter passing one frequency; if the densities differed, energy would
flow from one to the other at equal temperatures, violating the second
law. Kirchhoff: place the surface in the cavity at its own $T$; in
equilibrium it must emit at each frequency exactly what it absorbs,
$\varepsilon_\nu u_\nu = \alpha_\nu u_\nu$.
\end{proof}

\section{Planck's law}

\begin{theorem}[Planck's law]\label{thm:b2:thermal-radiation:planck}
The spectral energy density of black-body radiation at the temperature
$T$ is
\[
u_\nu(T) = \frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}\,\frac{1}{\eu^{h\nu/k_BT} - 1},
\]
and the power radiated by a black surface per unit area and per unit
wavelength (its \emph{spectral exitance}\index{spectral exitance}) is
\[
M_\lambda(T) = \frac{2\pi hc^2}{\lambda^5}\,\frac{1}{\eu^{hc/\lambda k_BT} - 1}.
\]
Two limits: for $h\nu \ll k_BT$, $u_\nu \approx 8\pi\nu^2 k_BT/c^3$
(Rayleigh--Jeans: each mode of the cavity carries the classical energy
$k_BT$ --- a law that integrates to infinity, the ``ultraviolet
catastrophe''); for $h\nu \gg k_BT$, $u_\nu \approx (8\pi h\nu^3/c^3)
\eu^{-h\nu/k_BT}$ (Wien: the Boltzmann factor of a photon of energy
$h\nu$, \cref{ch:b2:boltzmann-factor}).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Admitted: the counting of the cavity's modes ($8\pi\nu^2/c^3$ per unit
volume and frequency, two polarizations) belongs to the Year 3 volume,
and the mean energy of a mode, $h\nu/(\eu^{h\nu/k_BT} - 1)$ instead of the
classical $k_BT$, is the quantum hypothesis: energy is exchanged with
the mode in quanta $h\nu$, so that modes with $h\nu \gg k_BT$ are
frozen out. The relation $M_\lambda\,\dd\lambda = (c/4)\,u_\nu\,\dd\nu$ is the
geometric factor computed in \cref{thm:b2:thermal-radiation:stefan}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Wien's displacement law]\label{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:wien}
The \omterm{thm:b2:thermal-radiation:planck}{spectral exitance} $M_\lambda$ peaks at
\[
\lambda_{\max}\,T = \frac{hc}{4.965\,k_B} = \qty{2.90e-3}{m.K} .
\]
A body at \qty{300}{K} radiates mostly near \qty{10}{\micro m}; the Sun
(\qty{5800}{K}) near \qty{0.5}{\micro m}, in the green, where the eye is
most sensitive --- not by chance.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With $x = hc/\lambda k_BT$, $M_\lambda \propto x^5/(\eu^x - 1)$; $\dd/\dd x = 0$
gives $5(\eu^x - 1) = x\eu^x$, i.e. $x = 5(1 - \eu^{-x})$, whose non-zero
root is $x = 4.965$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Stefan--Boltzmann law]\label{thm:b2:thermal-radiation:stefan}
A black surface at $T$ radiates, per unit area, the total power
\[
M = \int_0^\infty M_\lambda\,\dd\lambda = \sigma T^4, \qquad
\sigma = \frac{2\pi^5k_B^4}{15h^3c^2} = \qty{5.67e-8}{W/m^2/K^4} ;
\]
the energy density of the equilibrium radiation is $u = (4\sigma/c)T^4$
and its pressure $P = u/3$. A grey surface of \omterm{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:kirchhoff}{emissivity} $\varepsilon$
radiates $\varepsilon\sigma T^4$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Integrate Planck's density: $u = \int u_\nu\,\dd\nu = (8\pi h/c^3)(k_BT/h)^4
\int_0^\infty x^3\,\dd x/(\eu^x - 1)$ and the integral is $\pi^4/15$
(admitted from analysis): $u = (8\pi^5k_B^4/15h^3c^3)\,T^4$. The power
leaving a hole of unit area in the cavity wall is $cu/4$: the
radiation is isotropic, the photons moving toward the hole within the
solid angle $\dd\Omega$ at the angle $\theta$ from its normal carry the
flux $c\,u\,(\dd\Omega/4\pi)\cos\theta$, and $\int\cos\theta\,\dd\Omega/4\pi$
over the outward hemisphere equals $1/4$. Hence $M = cu/4 = \sigma T^4$.
The pressure, as for any isotropic gas of particles moving at $c$, is
$u/3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Orders of magnitude]\label{ex:b2:thermal-radiation:orders}
At \qty{300}{K}: $\sigma T^4 = \qty{460}{W/m^2}$ --- a person of
\qty{1.7}{m^2} radiates \qty{800}{W}, but receives almost as much from
the walls at \qty{293}{K}; the net loss, $\sigma A(T^4 - T_0^4) \approx
\qty{100}{W}$, is the body's whole metabolic output, which is why one
needs clothes in a room that feels mild. The Sun's surface: $\sigma
(5800)^4 = \qty{6.4e7}{W/m^2}$, total $3.9 \times 10^{26}\,\unit{W}$. A
bulb's filament at \qty{2800}{K}: \qty{3.5e6}{W/m^2}, \qty{60}{W} from half
a square centimetre of tungsten. The cosmic background, \qty{2.7}{K}:
peak at \qty{1}{mm}, $\qty{3e-6}{W/m^2}$, and $400$ photons per cubic
centimetre of the universe.
\end{example}

\section{Radiative exchanges}

\begin{proposition}[Net exchange and the radiative coefficient]\label{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:exchange}
A small grey body (\omterm{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:kirchhoff}{emissivity} $\varepsilon$, area $S$, temperature $T$)
inside large surroundings at $T_0$ loses the net power
\[
P = \varepsilon\sigma S\,(T^4 - T_0^4)
\ \approx\ 4\varepsilon\sigma T_0^3\,S\,(T - T_0) = h_{\text{r}}S(T - T_0)
\quad\text{for } |T - T_0| \ll T_0 ,
\]
where $h_{\text{r}} = 4\varepsilon\sigma T_0^3 \approx \qty{6}{W/m^2/K}$ at
room temperature --- comparable to natural convection: in a room,
radiation carries about half the heat leaving a body or a radiator.
Between two large parallel black plates the net flux density is
$\sigma(T_1^4 - T_2^4)$; for grey plates of emissivities $\varepsilon_1$,
$\varepsilon_2$ it is $\sigma(T_1^4 - T_2^4)/(1/\varepsilon_1 + 1/\varepsilon_2
- 1)$, and each thin \emph{radiation shield} interposed divides it
further --- the principle of the vacuum flask and of the gold foil on
satellites.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The body emits $\varepsilon\sigma T^4$ and absorbs the fraction $\alpha =
\varepsilon$ of the black radiation $\sigma T_0^4$ that fills the enclosure
(Kirchhoff). Linearise $T^4 - T_0^4 \approx 4T_0^3(T - T_0)$. For the
grey plates, sum the multiple reflections (a geometric series).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Frost under a clear sky, and the thermal camera]\label{ex:b2:thermal-radiation:frost}
On a clear night the sky radiates like a body at about \qty{250}{K}
(the dry upper atmosphere), while the air near the ground is at
\qty{278}{K}. A leaf or a car roof ($\varepsilon = 0.95$) balances its
radiative loss to the sky against convection from the air ($h =
\qty{5}{W/m^2/K}$): $\varepsilon\sigma(T^4 - 250^4) = h(278 - T)$ gives $T
\approx \qty{266}{K}$ --- frost at \qty{-7}{\celsius} on the grass while
the thermometer reads $+5$. A cloud (radiating at \qty{275}{K}) lifts $T$
to \qty{277}{K}: no frost. A thermal camera measures the radiance near
\qty{10}{\micro m} and converts it to a temperature assuming
$\varepsilon \approx 0.95$; a polished metal ($\varepsilon = 0.1$) mostly
reflects the room and appears at the room's temperature whatever its
own --- hence the black tape that thermographers stick on shiny parts.
\end{example}

\section{The Sun, the Earth and the greenhouse}

\begin{proposition}[Solar constant and effective temperature]\label{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:earth}
The Sun (radius $R_\odot$, surface temperature $T_\odot$) delivers at the
distance $d$ the flux density $S = \sigma T_\odot^4 (R_\odot/d)^2 =
\qty{1361}{W/m^2}$ above the Earth's atmosphere (the \emph{solar
constant}\index{solar constant}). A planet of albedo $A$ (the fraction
reflected) absorbs $(1 - A)S\pi R^2$ and, in the steady state, radiates it
from its whole surface $4\pi R^2$ as a \omterm{def:b2:thermal-radiation:blackbody}{black body} at its \emph{effective
temperature}\index{effective temperature}
\[
T_{\text{e}} = \Big(\frac{(1 - A)S}{4\sigma}\Big)^{1/4} = \qty{255}{K}
\quad\text{for the Earth } (A = 0.30) .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Energy conservation: absorbed $= $ emitted, the \omterm{prop:b2:laser:gain}{cross-section} $\pi R^2$
for the intercepted beam, the full sphere for the emission (day and
night, spread by rotation and circulation).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[One-layer greenhouse model]\label{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:greenhouse}
Surround the planet with a thin layer of atmosphere that is transparent
to sunlight but absorbs all the infrared emitted by the surface, and
radiates as a \omterm{def:b2:thermal-radiation:blackbody}{black body} at $T_{\text{a}}$ both up and down. In the
steady state,
\[
T_{\text{a}} = T_{\text{e}}, \qquad
T_{\text{s}} = 2^{1/4}\,T_{\text{e}} = \qty{303}{K} .
\]
If the layer absorbs only the fraction $\varepsilon$ of the infrared,
$T_{\text{s}}^4 = T_{\text{e}}^4/(1 - \varepsilon/2)$; the observed
\qty{288}{K} corresponds to $\varepsilon \approx 0.78$. The surface is
warmer than the \omterm{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:earth}{effective temperature} because it receives, in addition
to the Sun, the infrared that the atmosphere sends back down --- the
\emph{greenhouse effect}\index{greenhouse effect}, \qty{33}{K} on Earth,
without which the oceans would be frozen.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Balance of the layer: absorbs $\sigma T_{\text{s}}^4$, emits $2\sigma
T_{\text{a}}^4$. Balance of the planet seen from space: emits $\sigma
T_{\text{a}}^4 = (1 - A)S/4 = \sigma T_{\text{e}}^4$. Balance of the
surface: receives $(1 - A)S/4 + \sigma T_{\text{a}}^4 = 2\sigma T_{\text{e}}^4$,
emits $\sigma T_{\text{s}}^4$. For the partial layer, replace the
absorbed and emitted infrared by $\varepsilon\sigma T_{\text{s}}^4$ and
$\varepsilon\sigma T_{\text{a}}^4$ and let the fraction $1 - \varepsilon$
of the surface radiation escape directly.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why the greenhouse is selective]\label{rem:b2:thermal-radiation:selective}
Sunlight peaks at \qty{0.5}{\micro m}, the Earth's radiation at
\qty{10}{\micro m}: the two spectra hardly overlap, so a gas can be
transparent to the first and absorbing for the second. Nitrogen and
oxygen absorb neither; water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane and ozone
have vibration bands in the infrared and are the greenhouse gases. The
window between \qty{8}{} and \qty{13}{\micro m} is what the thermal camera
looks through, what the night sky's \qty{250}{K} refers to, and what
radiative cooling paints exploit. The glass of a real greenhouse does
the same (transparent to \qty{0.5}{\micro m}, opaque to \qty{10}{\micro m}),
but mostly it blocks convection --- the name is a little unfair.
\end{remark}

\begin{method}[Radiation estimates]\label{meth:b2:thermal-radiation:method}
(1) $\lambda_{\max} = \qty{2900}{\micro m.K}/T$ tells where the spectrum
lies; (2) $\varepsilon\sigma T^4$ per unit area; (3) exchanges: emitted
minus absorbed, linearised as $h_{\text{r}} = 4\varepsilon\sigma T^3$ near
room temperature; (4) a planet: $(1 - A)S/4 = \sigma T_{\text{e}}^4$, then
the layers; (5) compare with conduction and convection --- radiation
wins at high temperature ($T^4$) and in vacuum; (6) mind emissivities:
shiny metals \qty{0.05}{}, paints, skin, water, soil $\approx 0.9$--$0.98$
in the infrared whatever their visible colour.
\end{method}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:thermal-radiation:5}
\emph{The two limits.} (a) Expand Planck's $u_\nu$ for $h\nu \ll k_BT$ and

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:thermal-radiation:6}
\emph{Wien.} (a) Derive $x = 5(1 - \eu^{-x})$ and solve it numerically
to three figures. (b) Value of $\lambda_{\max}T$. (c) Show that $u_\nu$

peaks at \qty{290}{nm}; its colour to the eye.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:thermal-radiation:7}

per unit volume at $T$, and the mean photon energy in units of $k_BT$.
(d) Photons per cubic centimetre at \qty{300}{K} and at \qty{2.73}{K}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:thermal-radiation:8}
\emph{Shields.} Two large black plates at $T_1$, $T_2$. (a) Net flux.
(b) A thin black sheet is placed between them: its temperature and the
new flux. (c) $n$ sheets. (d) Grey plates of \omterm{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:kirchhoff}{emissivity} $\varepsilon$:
show the flux is $\sigma(T_1^4 - T_2^4)/(2/\varepsilon - 1)$ and compute
it for polished surfaces ($\varepsilon = 0.05$) at \qty{300}{K} and
\qty{77}{K} --- the vacuum flask.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:thermal-radiation:10}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:thermal-radiation:11}

Doubling the carbon dioxide is a forcing of about \qty{3.7}{W/m^2}:
$\Delta T$ in this model. (c) The ocean's mixed layer (\qty{50}{m} of

\section{Problem: The Earth's energy budget and the incandescent bulb}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- black bodies from the Sun to the
filament}]\label{pb:b2:thermal-radiation:1}
Data: $\sigma = \qty{5.67e-8}{W/m^2/K^4}$, $\lambda_{\max}T = \qty{2.90e-3}{m.K}$,
$h = \qty{6.63e-34}{J.s}$, $k_B = \qty{1.38e-23}{J/K}$, $c = \qty{3.00e8}{m/s}$;
Sun: $R_\odot = \qty{6.96e8}{m}$, $d = \qty{1.50e11}{m}$; Earth: $R =
\qty{6.37e6}{m}$, albedo $0.30$.

\textbf{Part I --- The Sun as a \omterm{def:b2:thermal-radiation:blackbody}{black body}.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The solar spectrum peaks at \qty{0.50}{\micro m}: surface
        temperature.
  \item Exitance of the surface and total luminosity.
  \item \omterm{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:earth}{Solar constant} at the Earth; energy density and pressure of
        sunlight there.
  \item Mean photon energy ($\approx 2.7\,k_BT$) and number of solar
        photons per second per square metre at the Earth.
  \item The Sun has radiated at this rate for $4.5 \times 10^9$ years:
        energy emitted; compare with $Mc^2$ for $M = \qty{2e30}{kg}$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Part II --- The Earth without atmosphere.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Power absorbed by the Earth; \omterm{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:earth}{effective temperature}.
  \item Why does the balance divide by $4$? What would the
        temperature be at the subsolar point of a non-rotating airless
        Earth ($A = 0.30$)?
  \item The Moon ($A = 0.12$): subsolar temperature; and why its night
        side drops to \qty{100}{K} (think of the heat stored in the
        regolith during the two-week day, \qty{1e6}{J/m^2/K} effective).
  \item Wavelength of the Earth's emission peak; compare with the
        Sun's.
  \item If more ice or cloud raised the albedo to $0.35$, by how much
        would the \omterm{prop:b2:thermal-radiation:earth}{effective temperature} fall?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Part III --- The greenhouse.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Write the three balances (space, layer, surface) of the
        one-layer model and obtain $T_{\text{s}} = 2^{1/4}T_{\text{e}}$.
  \item With a layer absorbing the fraction $\varepsilon$ of the
        infrared: $T_{\text{s}}(\varepsilon)$; the value of $\varepsilon$
        giving \qty{288}{K}.
  \item Which gases absorb, which do not, and in which wavelength
        band; what is the atmospheric window?
  \item The infrared sent back to the ground by the atmosphere,
        in \unit{W/m^2}, in the $\varepsilon = 0.78$ model; compare with
        the absorbed sunlight.
  \item A forcing of \qty{3.7}{W/m^2} (doubled carbon dioxide):
        temperature change $\Delta T = \Delta F/4\sigma T_{\text{e}}^3$
        without \omterm{def:b2:feedback-oscillators:loop}{feedbacks}.
  \item Heat capacity of a \qty{50}{m} ocean mixed layer per square
        metre and the time constant of the response.
  \item Why do clouds both cool (albedo) and warm (infrared) the
        surface; which effect wins at night?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Part IV --- The incandescent bulb.}
A \qty{60}{W} bulb has a tungsten filament at \qty{2800}{K},
$\varepsilon = 0.35$; the fraction of a \omterm{def:b2:thermal-radiation:blackbody}{black body}'s power that falls in
the visible ($0.4$--\qty{0.7}{\micro m}) is $0.08$ at \qty{2800}{K} and $0.14$
at \qty{3200}{K}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Radiating area of the filament; length of a \qty{50}{\micro m}
        wire having it; why is it coiled?
  \item Peak wavelength; visible power; luminous efficiency.
  \item Resistance of the filament in operation at \qty{230}{V};
        tungsten's resistivity is about ten times smaller at room
        temperature: current at switch-on, and a consequence.
  \item At \qty{3200}{K}: the area needed for the same \qty{60}{W} and
        the visible power; why not run every bulb at \qty{3200}{K}
        (the tungsten's evaporation rate roughly doubles every
        \qty{50}{K})?
  \item What does the halogen cycle do, and why does it allow a hotter
        filament?
  \item Where does the rest of the \qty{60}{W} go, and why does the
        bulb's glass reach \qty{150}{\celsius}?
  \item The filament's cooling time once switched off: estimate from
        its mass (\qty{20}{mg}, $c = \qty{140}{J/kg/K}$) and its radiated
        power.
  \item Summarise: what a \omterm{def:b2:thermal-radiation:blackbody}{black body}'s temperature fixes, and what
        the filament, the Sun and the Earth have in common.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
