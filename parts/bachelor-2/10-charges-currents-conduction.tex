\chapter{Charges, Currents and Conduction}\label{ch:b2:charges-currents-conduction}

Flip the switch and the lamp lights at once; yet the electrons in the
copper wire drift toward it at a fraction of a millimetre per second,
and would take hours to arrive. A lightning bolt carries thirty
thousand amperes through a channel the width of a finger; the nerve
that moves that finger carries picoamperes. The Year 1 volume treated
currents as numbers in wires, $I = U/R$; this chapter describes them as
a \emph{field} --- a \omterm{def:b2:charges-currents-conduction:densities}{current density} at every point of matter --- writes
the law that charge is never created or destroyed, and derives Ohm's
law from the jostling of electrons in a metal: the microscopic picture
behind the resistor, the fuse and the Hall sensor.

\section{Charge and current densities}

\begin{definition}[Charge density; current density]\label{def:b2:charges-currents-conduction:densities}
\index{charge density}\index{current density}\index{intensity (flux of j)}
At the \omterm{def:b2:fluid-kinematics:particle}{mesoscopic scale}, matter carries a \emph{volume charge density}
$\rho(M, t)$ (\unit{C/m^3}): the charge $\rho\,\dd\tau$ in $\dd\tau$. A surface
may carry a \emph{surface density} $\sigma$ (\unit{C/m^2}), a wire a linear
density $\lambda$ (\unit{C/m}). If the carriers of species $i$ (charge
$q_i$, number density $n_i$) move at the mean velocity $\vect v_i$, the
\emph{current density} is
\[
\vect j = \sum_in_iq_i\,\vect v_i \qquad (\unit{A/m^2}) ,
\]
and the \emph{intensity} through an oriented surface $S$ is the flux
$I = \iint_S\vect j\cdot\vect n\,\dd S$: the charge crossing $S$ per unit
time. For a single species, $\vect j = \rho_m\vect v$ with $\rho_m = nq$ the
density of mobile charge.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}
In $\dd t$ the carriers of species $i$ that cross $\dd S$ are those in
the oblique cylinder of base $\dd S$ and generator $\vect v_i\dd t$: number
$n_i\vect v_i\cdot\vect n\,\dd S\,\dd t$, charge $q_i$ times that.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[How slowly electrons drift]\label{ex:b2:charges-currents-conduction:drift}
\index{drift velocity}
Copper has one conduction electron per atom: $n = \rho_{\text{Cu}}N_A/M =
8900 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}/0.0635 = \qty{8.5e28}{m^{-3}}$. A current of
\qty{10}{A} in a \qty{1.5}{mm^2} wire is $j = \qty{6.7e6}{A/m^2}$, and the
\emph{\omterm{ex:b2:charges-currents-conduction:drift}{drift velocity}} is $v = j/ne = 6.7 \times 10^6/(8.5 \times 10^{28} \times 1.6 \times
10^{-19}) = \qty{0.5}{mm/s}$ --- two hours per metre. The lamp lights at
once because the \emph{field} that pushes the electrons is set up
along the whole wire within nanoseconds (it travels at nearly the
speed of light, as the cable of \cref{ch:b2:dispersion-wave-packets}
showed): all the electrons start together, like water in a full hose.
\end{example}

\section{Conservation of charge}

\begin{theorem}[Local conservation of charge]\label{thm:b2:charges-currents-conduction:conservation}
\index{charge conservation (local)}\index{continuity equation (charge)}
At every point,
\[
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \operatorname{div}\vect j = 0 ;
\]
integrated over a fixed volume $V$ bounded by the closed surface $\Sigma$,
$\dd Q_V/\dd t = -I_{\text{out}}$: the charge inside changes only by what
crosses the boundary. In a \emph{stationary regime} ($\partial_t\rho = 0$),
$\operatorname{div}\vect j = 0$: the flux of $\vect j$ is conserved along a tube
of current, the same intensity crosses every section of a wire, and
the currents entering a node equal those leaving (Kirchhoff's node law).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Word for word the mass balance of \cref{thm:b2:fluid-kinematics:continuity}
with $\rho$ the \omterm{def:b2:charges-currents-conduction:densities}{charge density} and $\vect j$ its current: the net outflow
of charge through the six faces of a fixed box, per unit volume, is
$\operatorname{div}\vect j$, and no charge is created. The integral form sums
the boxes (the interior faces cancel). For a tube between two sections
$S_1$, $S_2$ with no flux through its side, $I_1 = I_2$; a node is a
small volume with several wires: $\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Where the current stops: the capacitor]\label{rem:b2:charges-currents-conduction:capacitor}
A wire feeding a capacitor plate is a tube of current that ends: the
flux of $\vect j$ into the plate is $I = \dd Q/\dd t$, and charge accumulates
--- $\operatorname{div}\vect j \ne 0$ there, the regime is not stationary.
Yet the same current $I$ flows in the wire on the other side: something
must carry the balance across the gap. \cref{ch:b2:maxwell-equations}
names it --- the displacement current --- and makes the conservation of
charge the keystone of Maxwell's equations.
\end{remark}

\section{The Drude model and Ohm's law}

\begin{proposition}[Drude model; local Ohm's law]\label{prop:b2:charges-currents-conduction:drude}
\index{Drude model}\index{Ohm's law (local)}\index{conductivity}\index{resistivity}\index{mobility}
Model the conduction electrons of a metal as free particles (mass $m$,
charge $-e$, density $n$) that feel the field $\vect E$ and, on average,
lose their drift momentum by collisions with the lattice every $\tau$
seconds --- a \omterm{def:b2:rigid-body-mechanics:contact}{friction force} $-m\vect v/\tau$ on the mean velocity:
\[
m\frac{\dd\vect v}{\dd t} = -e\vect E - \frac m\tau\vect v .
\]
In a steady field the drift settles, in a few $\tau$, at $\vect v = -e\tau
\vect E/m$, and the \omterm{def:b2:charges-currents-conduction:densities}{current density} is
\[
\vect j = \gamma\,\vect E , \qquad \gamma = \frac{ne^2\tau}{m} :
\]
the \emph{local Ohm's law}, with $\gamma$ the \emph{conductivity}
(\unit{S/m}; its inverse $\rho_e = 1/\gamma$ is the \emph{resistivity},
\unit{\ohm.m}). The ratio $\mu = e\tau/m$ of the drift speed to the field is
the \emph{mobility} (\unit{m^2/(V.s)}). For a conductor of length $L$ and
uniform section $S$ carrying a uniform $\vect j$, integrating $E = j/\gamma$
along the length gives $U = EL = (L/\gamma S)I$: the resistance $R = L/\gamma S
= \rho_eL/S$ of the Year 1 volume.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With $\vect v = -e\tau\vect E/m$, $\vect j = -ne\vect v = (ne^2\tau/m)\vect E$. The
transient $\vect v(t) = \vect v_\infty(1 - \eu^{-t/\tau})$ lasts $\tau \sim
\qty{1e-14}{s}$, instantaneous on every circuit time scale. The resistance
follows from $\int\vect E\cdot\dd\vect l$ between the ends.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Copper, from the measured conductivity]\label{ex:b2:charges-currents-conduction:copper}
Copper: $\gamma = \qty{6.0e7}{S/m}$, $n = \qty{8.5e28}{m^{-3}}$: $\tau = m\gamma/ne^2 =
9.1 \times 10^{-31} \times 6 \times 10^7/(8.5 \times 10^{28} \times 2.56 \times 10^{-38}) = \qty{2.5e-14}{s}$
--- twenty-five femtoseconds between collisions. The electrons' random
speed in a metal is about \qty{1.6e6}{m/s} (a quantum effect, not the
thermal $\sqrt{3k_BT/m} \approx \qty{1e5}{m/s}$), so the mean free path is $\ell
= v\tau \approx \qty{40}{nm}$, a hundred atomic spacings: the electrons do not
collide with the atoms themselves (a perfect crystal is transparent to
them) but with its defects and thermal vibrations --- which is why
$\gamma$ falls when a metal is heated or alloyed. Mobility $e\tau/m =
\qty{4.4e-3}{m^2/(V.s)}$: \qty{4}{mm/s} per volt per metre.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Ohm's law at high frequency]\label{prop:b2:charges-currents-conduction:acdrude}
\index{complex conductivity}
For a sinusoidal field $\underline{\vect E}\,\eu^{\iu\omega t}$ the Drude equation
gives $\underline{\vect j} = \underline\gamma(\omega)\underline{\vect E}$ with
\[
\underline\gamma(\omega) = \frac{\gamma_0}{1 + \iu\omega\tau} , \qquad \gamma_0 = \frac{ne^2\tau}{m} :
\]
the conductivity is real and equal to its DC value as long as $\omega\tau
\ll 1$, i.e.\ up to about \qty{1e13}{Hz} for copper --- through the whole
radio and microwave range; in the infrared and visible ($\omega\tau \gg 1$)
it becomes imaginary, $\underline\gamma \approx ne^2/\iu m\omega$: the electrons
oscillate freely, out of phase with the field, and the metal behaves
as the plasma of \cref{ch:b2:waves-in-media}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\iu\omega m\underline{\vect v} = -e\underline{\vect E} - m\underline{\vect v}/\tau$, whence
$\underline{\vect v} = -e\tau\underline{\vect E}/m(1 + \iu\omega\tau)$ and $\underline{\vect j}
= -ne\underline{\vect v}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Conductors, semiconductors, electrolytes]\label{rem:b2:charges-currents-conduction:materials}
\index{semiconductor}\index{electrolyte}
The same law $\vect j = \sum n_iq_i\mu_i\vect E$ holds for every conductor;
what changes is $n$ and $\mu$. Metals: $n \sim 10^{29}$, $\gamma \sim 10^7$
S/m. Semiconductors: $n \sim 10^{16}$--$10^{23}$, set by doping and rising
steeply with temperature, with two kinds of carriers (electrons and
holes) --- whence thermistors and diodes. Electrolytes: ions of both
signs, $\mu \sim 10^{-8}$--$10^{-7}$ m$^2$/(V s); sea water $\gamma \approx \qty{5}{S/m}$,
tap water $10^{-2}$, pure water $10^{-5}$. Insulators (glass, polymers):
$\gamma \lesssim 10^{-12}$ S/m. The Hall effect of the Year 1 volume, in
local form $\vect E_H = -\vect j\wedge\vect B/nq$, measures $n$ and the sign
of $q$: in aluminium or zinc the carriers turn out to be positive ---
holes --- and no classical model explains that.
\end{remark}

\section{Energy: the local Joule law}

\begin{proposition}[Local Joule law]\label{prop:b2:charges-currents-conduction:joule}
\index{Joule law (local)}
The electric field delivers to the carriers, per unit volume and time,
the power $\vect j\cdot\vect E$; in an ohmic conductor this is
\[
p = \vect j\cdot\vect E = \gamma E^2 = \frac{j^2}\gamma \ \ (\unit{W/m^3}) ,
\]
all of it turned into heat by the collisions (the drift kinetic energy
is negligible and constant). Integrated over a wire, $\int j^2/\gamma\,\dd\tau
= (L/\gamma S)I^2 = RI^2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The force $q\vect E$ on each carrier works at the rate $q\vect E\cdot\vect v$;
summed over the $n\,\dd\tau$ carriers: $nq\vect v\cdot\vect E\,\dd\tau = \vect j\cdot
\vect E\,\dd\tau$ (the magnetic force does no work). In the Drude steady
state this power equals that dissipated by the friction term, i.e.\
transferred to the lattice.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A wire heating]\label{ex:b2:charges-currents-conduction:heating}
The \qty{1.5}{mm^2} wire at \qty{10}{A}: $p = j^2/\gamma = (6.7 \times 10^6)^2/6 \times 10^7 =
\qty{7.4e5}{W/m^3}$, i.e.\ \qty{1.1}{W} per metre --- easily evacuated by the air
around it. Left to itself (no cooling), copper ($c = \qty{385}{J/(kg.K)}$,
$\rho = \qty{8900}{kg/m^3}$) would warm at $p/\rho c = \qty{0.2}{K/s}$. At \qty{100}{A}
in the same wire the rate is a hundred times larger, \qty{20}{K/s}: it
melts in a minute --- the principle of the fuse, whose thin wire is
designed to melt first.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Relaxation of charge in a conductor]\label{prop:b2:charges-currents-conduction:relaxation}
\index{relaxation time (conductor)}
In an ohmic conductor a volume \omterm{def:b2:charges-currents-conduction:densities}{charge density} decays as $\rho(t) =
\rho_0\eu^{-t/\tau_r}$ with
\[
\tau_r = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{\gamma} :
\]
\qty{1.5e-19}{s} for copper, \qty{2e-12}{s} for sea water, \qty{10}{s} for glass.
Up to frequencies of order $1/\tau_r$ a conductor carries \emph{no
volume charge}: any excess charge runs to its surface. The word
"conductor" thus depends on the time scale: sea water is a conductor
for radio waves and an insulator for visible light.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Charge conservation with $\vect j = \gamma\vect E$ and Gauss's law in local
form, $\operatorname{div}\vect E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ (\cref{ch:b2:maxwell-equations}):
$\partial_t\rho = -\gamma\operatorname{div}\vect E = -(\gamma/\varepsilon_0)\rho$.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[From the local to the integral law]\label{met:b2:charges-currents-conduction:method}
To find the resistance of a conductor of arbitrary shape: (1) use the
symmetry to write $\vect j$ (stationary: its flux is conserved, so in a
tube $j \times$ section $= I$); (2) $\vect E = \vect j/\gamma$; (3) integrate
$\vect E$ along a line from one electrode to the other: $U = \int\vect E\cdot
\dd\vect l$, and $R = U/I$; (4) check with the Joule power $\int j^2/\gamma\,
\dd\tau = RI^2$. The same steps give the leakage of a cable, the resistance
of the ground around a lightning rod, or of a conical contact.
\end{method}

unit volume at \qty{10}{A}. (b) Time to melt from \qty{20}{\degreeCelsius} if no
heat escapes (ignore the rise of resistivity with temperature). (c)
Same at \qty{30}{A}. (d) In reality the wire loses heat to the air: how does
that set the rated current of a fuse, and why are fuses sealed in a
sand-filled cartridge?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:charges-currents-conduction:9}
\emph{Relaxation.} (a) Derive $\partial_t\rho = -(\gamma/\varepsilon_0)\rho$ from charge
conservation, Ohm's law and $\operatorname{div}\vect E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$. (b)
$\tau_r$ for copper, sea water ($\gamma = \qty{5}{S/m}$), tap water
(\qty{5e-2}{S/m}), glass (\qty{1e-12}{S/m}), PTFE (\qty{1e-16}{S/m}). (c)
Up to what frequency is each a "good conductor" (charge relaxing within
a period)? (d) A charged glass rod keeps its charge for minutes: is
that consistent?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:charges-currents-conduction:10}
\emph{Grounding.} A hemispherical electrode of radius $a = \qty{0.50}{m}$
is buried flush with the surface of soil of conductivity $\gamma =
\qty{0.010}{S/m}$; a current $I$ enters it and spreads radially into the
half-space. (a) $\vect j(r)$, $\vect E(r)$, potential $V(r)$ with $V(\infty) = 0$.
(b) Resistance of the grounding, $R = 1/2\pi\gamma a$; value. (c) A
lightning stroke of \qty{20}{kA} hits the rod: potential of the rod;
potential at $r = \qty{10}{m}$ and \qty{10.7}{m}; the "step voltage" between
the feet of a person standing there (feet \qty{0.7}{m} apart, radially).
(d) Why are cattle, with their four legs far apart, killed by nearby
strikes more often than people?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:charges-currents-conduction:11}
\emph{Hall sensor.} A strip of thickness $t$ and width $w$ carries $I$
along $x$ in a field $B$ along $z$. (a) From $\vect E_H = -\vect j\wedge\vect B
/nq$, show that the Hall voltage across the width is $V_H = IB/nqt$. (b)
Copper strip, $t = \qty{0.10}{mm}$, \qty{1}{A}, \qty{1}{T}: $V_H$. (c) A doped
semiconductor with $n = \qty{1e22}{m^{-3}}$, same geometry: $V_H$; why
sensors use semiconductors. (d) A sensor with $n = \qty{1e22}{m^{-3}}$, $t =
\qty{20}{\micro m}$, fed with \qty{1}{mA}, reads \qty{10}{\micro V}: field measured.
What does the sign of $V_H$ tell?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:charges-currents-conduction:12}
\emph{Electrolyte.} In water, Na$^+$ and Cl$^-$ ions have mobilities
$\mu_+ = \qty{5.2e-8}{m^2/(V.s)}$ and $\mu_- = \qty{7.9e-8}{m^2/(V.s)}$. (a)
Show that $\gamma = ne(\mu_+ + \mu_-)$ for $n$ ion pairs per unit volume.
(b) Sea water, \qty{0.5}{mol/L} of NaCl: $\gamma$; compare with the measured
\qty{5}{S/m}. (c) Drift speeds at \qty{10}{V/m}; which ion carries more of
the current? (d) A column of sea water, \qty{1}{cm^2} by \qty{10}{cm}, carries
\qty{1}{A}: voltage, power, and the time to warm it by \qty{10}{K} ($c =
\qty{4.0}{kJ/(kg.K)}$); why electrophoresis gels are cooled.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The copper wire, from the atom to the grid}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- one metre of household wire examined
at four scales: the electron, the local laws, the circuit, and the
power line}]
\label{pb:b2:charges-currents-conduction:1}

Copper: molar mass \qty{63.5}{g/mol}, density \qty{8900}{kg/m^3}, one
conduction electron per atom, conductivity $\gamma = \qty{6.0e7}{S/m}$ at
\qty{20}{\degreeCelsius}, rising resistivity $\rho_e(T) = \rho_e(20)[1 +
\alpha(T - 20)]$ with $\alpha = \qty{4.0e-3}{K^{-1}}$; $c = \qty{385}{J/(kg.K)}$. Wire:
section $S = \qty{2.5}{mm^2}$, length \qty{1}{m}, current \qty{16}{A}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The electrons.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Number density $n$ of conduction electrons.
  \item \omterm{def:b2:charges-currents-conduction:densities}{Current density} and \omterm{ex:b2:charges-currents-conduction:drift}{drift velocity}; how long an electron needs
        to cross the metre.
  \item Drude's $\tau$ and the mobility; mean free path with a random
        speed of \qty{1.6e6}{m/s}; how many atomic spacings (\qty{0.26}{nm})
        is that, and what does it say about what the electrons collide
        with?
  \item The thermal vibrations grow with $T$, so $\tau \propto 1/T$ roughly:
        check against $\alpha$ near room temperature ($\dd\rho_e/\rho_e =
        \dd T/T$ would give $\alpha = 1/293$).
  \item Electric field in the wire; total voltage over the metre; the
        kinetic energy of the drift per electron compared with $k_BT$ ---
        is the gas of electrons disturbed by the current?
  \item Number of electrons crossing a section of the wire per second.
  \item Charge of the conduction electrons in the metre of wire; the
        force the field exerts on all of them together; compare with
        the weight of the wire. Where does that force end up (think
        of the collisions)?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The local laws.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Joule power per unit volume and per metre.
  \item The wire, in air, loses heat at a rate $h(T - T_a)$ per unit
        surface with $h = \qty{10}{W/(m^2.K)}$ (convection): equilibrium
        temperature rise; check whether the resistivity correction
        matters.
  \item The same wire buried in insulation that lets only $h =
        \qty{2}{W/(m^2.K)}$ through: new temperature rise, with the
        resistivity correction included (solve the balance).
  \item Left without any cooling, rate of temperature rise at \qty{16}{A}
        and at \qty{160}{A} (a short circuit); time to reach the melting
        point (\qty{1085}{\degreeCelsius}) in the second case; what protects
        the installation?
  \item At \qty{50}{Hz}, $\omega\tau = ?$ Is Ohm's law affected? (The skin
        effect of \cref{ch:b2:waves-in-media} is a different matter.)
  \item Write the relaxation time $\varepsilon_0/\gamma$ of copper; what does it
        imply for the \omterm{def:b2:charges-currents-conduction:densities}{charge density} inside the wire, and where does
        the charge that produces the field $E$ inside the wire sit?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Conservation of charge in the circuit.}
The wire feeds a capacitor $C = \qty{100}{\micro F}$ through a switch; the
current is $I(t) = I_0\eu^{-t/\tau_c}$ with $I_0 = \qty{16}{A}$, $\tau_c = \qty{1}{ms}$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Charge that reaches the plate; final voltage.
  \item Apply $\dd Q/\dd t = -I_{\text{out}}$ to a closed surface around the
        plate: which side carries a conduction current, which does not?
        What quantity must therefore vary in the gap?
  \item At $t = \qty{0.5}{ms}$, rate of change of the plate charge; if the
        plates are \qty{1}{cm^2} apart by \qty{0.1}{mm} in air, rate of
        change of the field between them.
  \item Show that $\varepsilon_0\,\dd E/\dd t \times$ plate area equals the current
        in the wire --- the quantity that \cref{ch:b2:maxwell-equations}
        adds to Ampère's law.
  \item The wire is cut and the two ends are \qty{1}{mm} apart in air:
        what current flows (air $\gamma \approx \qty{1e-14}{S/m}$)? At what
        field does air break down (\qty{3}{MV/m}): what voltage across the
        gap makes a spark?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The line.}
A \qty{230}{V} line of \qty{10}{mm^2} copper, \qty{500}{m} long (go and return:
\qty{1}{km} of wire), feeds a farm drawing \qty{40}{A}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Resistance, voltage drop, and power lost in the line; fraction
        of the delivered power.
  \item The same power at \qty{20}{kV} with a transformer at each end:
        current, drop and loss; conclude why transmission is done at
        high voltage.
  \item Aluminium ($\gamma = \qty{3.6e7}{S/m}$, \qty{2700}{kg/m^3}) is used on
        pylons: section for the same resistance as \qty{10}{mm^2} of
        copper; mass per kilometre for both; why aluminium wins up
        there and copper in the wall.
  \item A Hall sensor clamped around the line reads its field: for a
        clamp meter with $n = \qty{1e22}{m^{-3}}$, $t = \qty{0.1}{mm}$, fed with
        \qty{5}{mA} in a \qty{0.1}{T} field concentrated by an iron core,
        the Hall voltage.
  \item After a hot day's load the wire sits at \qty{80}{\degreeCelsius}:
        its resistance then, and the extra loss.
  \item Lightning hits the line: \qty{20}{kA} for \qty{100}{\micro s}. Energy
        dissipated in the \qty{1}{km} of wire; temperature rise if none
        escapes; \omterm{def:b2:charges-currents-conduction:densities}{current density} --- compare with Part I.
  \item Sum up: the five quantities ($n$, $\tau$, $\gamma$, $j$, $E$) that
        describe the wire at each scale, and the law that links each
        pair.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
