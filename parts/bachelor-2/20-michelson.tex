\chapter{The Michelson Interferometer}\label{ch:b2:michelson}

In 1887 Michelson and Morley split a beam of light in two, sent the
halves along perpendicular arms, recombined them, and watched the
fringes for the shift that the Earth's motion through the ether should
produce: nothing moved, and physics changed. In 2015 the same
instrument --- two arms, now four kilometres long and folded to a
thousand --- recorded a gravitational wave that changed their lengths
by a thousandth of a proton's diameter. Between those two dates the
\omterm{def:b2:michelson:instrument}{Michelson interferometer} measured the metre in wavelengths, resolved
spectral lines, and became the standard tool of precision optics.
This chapter describes it in its two configurations --- the air wedge
and the parallel plate --- and shows how its fringes measure lengths,
indices and spectra.

\section{The instrument}

\begin{definition}[The Michelson interferometer]\label{def:b2:michelson:instrument}
\index{Michelson interferometer}\index{beam splitter}\index{compensating plate}
A \emph{beam splitter} $Sp$ --- a glass plate with a semi-reflecting face
--- at \qty{45}{\degree} divides an incident beam into two: one goes to the
mirror $M_1$, the other to $M_2$, both perpendicular to their beams; after
reflection each returns to $Sp$, where half of it is sent toward the
observer, where the two halves interfere. A \emph{compensating plate}
$C$, identical to the splitter and parallel to it, is put in the arm
that would otherwise cross the splitter's glass only once, so that both
beams cross the same thickness of glass: the \omterm{thm:b2:two-wave-interference:formula}{path difference} is then a
pure difference of air paths, the same for every wavelength --- which
white-light fringes require. The mirrors' distances from $Sp$ are $d_1$
and $d_2$; one mirror is mounted on a micrometer screw and on tilt
screws.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Equivalent arrangement]\label{prop:b2:michelson:equivalent}
\index{air wedge (Michelson)}\index{air plate (Michelson)}
Seen from the observer, the interferometer is equivalent to the mirror
$M_2$ and the image $M_1'$ of $M_1$ in the splitter: a thin layer of air
between two reflecting surfaces, of thickness $e = |d_1 - d_2|$, whose
two reflections interfere (\omterm{prop:b2:two-wave-interference:film}{division of amplitude}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item $M_1' \parallel M_2$: an \emph{air plate} of thickness $e$ --- \omterm{prop:b2:michelson:rings}{rings of
        equal inclination}, localized at infinity, $\delta = 2e\cos i$;
  \item $M_1'$ slightly tilted by $\alpha$ relative to $M_2$: an \emph{air wedge}
        --- straight \omterm{prop:b2:two-wave-interference:film}{fringes of equal thickness}, localized on the
        mirrors (near the wedge), $\delta = 2\alpha x$ at normal incidence,
        spacing $\lambda/2\alpha$.
\end{itemize}
At \emph{contact} ($e = 0$, $\alpha = 0$) the field is uniform, and for white
light it is the one place where the colours agree: the "white fringe".
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Folding the $M_1$ arm onto the $M_2$ arm by reflection in $Sp$ maps the
two beams onto one line with two mirrors $e$ apart; the two returning
waves are those reflected by the two faces of the air layer (no extra
$\lambda/2$: both reflect on metal, and the splitter treats them
symmetrically, up to a constant phase that shifts the whole pattern).
The thin-film results of \cref{ch:b2:two-wave-interference} then apply
with $n = 1$.
\end{proof}

\section{The two fringe systems}

\begin{proposition}[Rings of the air plate]\label{prop:b2:michelson:rings}
\index{rings of equal inclination}\index{Haidinger fringes}
With $M_1' \parallel M_2$ and an \omterm{prop:b2:two-wave-interference:spatial}{extended source}, the observer (or a lens
of focal length $f$ and a screen in its focal plane) sees concentric
rings; the ring at the angle $i$ has the order $p = 2e\cos i/\lambda$; bright
rings where $p$ is an integer. The centre has the highest order, $p_0 =
2e/\lambda$; near it, $\cos i \approx 1 - i^2/2$, so the $k$-th bright ring from the
centre (counted from the first one, at $\varepsilon = p_0 - \lfloor p_0\rfloor$
below the centre's order) has the radius
\[
r_k = f\,i_k = f\sqrt{\frac{(k - 1 + \varepsilon)\lambda}e} :
\]
rings tighter with larger $e$ (more, thinner rings), spreading as $e \to 0$
until, at contact, one uniform field. Moving a mirror by $\lambda/2$ changes
$p_0$ by one: a ring is born at (or swallowed by) the centre for every
half wavelength of displacement.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\delta = 2e\cos i$ for the parallel plate (\cref{ch:b2:two-wave-interference},
$n = 1$, no extra $\lambda/2$ for two metallic reflections); $2e(1 - i^2/2) =
(p_0 - k + 1 - \varepsilon)\lambda$ with $2e = p_0\lambda$ gives $ei^2 = (k - 1 + \varepsilon)\lambda$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Fringes of the air wedge]\label{prop:b2:michelson:wedge}
\index{fringes of equal thickness (Michelson)}\index{Fizeau fringes}
With $M_1'$ tilted by a small angle $\alpha$ and near-normal incidence, the
\omterm{thm:b2:two-wave-interference:formula}{path difference} at the point of the mirrors at distance $x$ from the
line of contact is $\delta = 2\alpha x$: straight fringes parallel to that line,
spaced $\lambda/2\alpha$, localized on the mirrors (they are viewed by focusing
on $M_2$, or projected on a screen with a lens conjugating $M_2$ and the
screen). The zero-order fringe lies on the contact line, the same for
every wavelength: with white light it is white (or black, according to
the splitter's phases), flanked by a few coloured fringes --- the
signature by which the contact is found.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equal-thickness fringes of a wedge of air, $e(x) = \alpha x$. Localization:
for an \omterm{prop:b2:two-wave-interference:spatial}{extended source} the rays through a given point of the wedge
recombine there, with the same $\delta$ at near-normal incidence.
\end{proof}

\section{Measuring with the Michelson}

\begin{proposition}[Lengths, indices, spectra]\label{prop:b2:michelson:measure}
\index{Fourier transform spectroscopy}\index{channelled spectrum}
Counting $N$ fringes passing the centre while a mirror moves by $\Delta e$
gives $\Delta e = N\lambda/2$: lengths in wavelengths (a micrometre screw
calibrated to \qty{0.1}{\micro m}; the metre itself was so measured). A
transparent plate of thickness $\ell$ and index $n$ inserted in one arm
adds $2(n - 1)\ell$ to $\delta$ and shifts the pattern by $2(n - 1)\ell/\lambda$
fringes: indices (and their variations with pressure or temperature,
in a gas cell). With a doublet of wavelengths $\lambda$ and $\lambda + \Delta\lambda$, the
two fringe systems fall out of step and back in step as $e$ grows, the
contrast vanishing every
\[
\Delta e = \frac{\lambda^2}{2\Delta\lambda} :
\]
the splitting of close lines. More generally the contrast as a
function of $e$ maps out the spectrum of the source (the contrast
decays over $e \sim \ell_c/2$): recording the intensity at the centre while
$e$ scans is \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:michelson:measure}{Fourier transform spectroscopy}}, the method of
infrared spectrometers.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The order at the centre is $2e/\lambda$; each line has its own. The two
systems coincide again when $2e/\lambda - 2e/(\lambda + \Delta\lambda) = 1$, i.e.\ $2e\Delta\lambda
/\lambda^2 = 1$; they are in opposition half-way. The general statement is
the contrast formula of \cref{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:10}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Sodium, and the metre]\label{ex:b2:michelson:sodium}
The sodium doublet ($\Delta\lambda = \qty{0.6}{nm}$ at \qty{589}{nm}): the rings blur and
sharpen every $\Delta e = 589^2/(2 \times 0.6)$ nm $= \qty{0.29}{mm}$ --- a classic
measurement of $\Delta\lambda$ to a few percent with a screw. Michelson (1892)
counted the fringes of the red cadmium line over the length of the
standard metre: $1\,553\,163.5$ wavelengths, the first tie between the
metre and an atom; today the metre is defined through $c$ and the
second, and interferometers check gauge blocks in production to
nanometres.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[The arms that measured nothing, and those that measured a wave]\label{rem:b2:michelson:history}
\index{Michelson--Morley experiment}\index{gravitational-wave detector}
Michelson and Morley expected the Earth's velocity $v$ through the
ether to change the round-trip time along the arm parallel to it by
$(L/c)(v/c)^2$ relative to the perpendicular arm, a fringe shift of
$2L(v/c)^2/\lambda \approx 0.4$ fringe for $L = \qty{11}{m}$ and $v = \qty{30}{km/s}$;
rotating the apparatus should have swung the pattern by that much.
They could see $0.01$ fringe; they saw nothing. The \omterm{rem:b2:michelson:history}{gravitational-wave
detectors} of today are Michelsons with \qty{4}{km} arms, light bouncing
some three hundred times in each, and a laser stable enough to read a
change of arm length $\Delta L/L \sim 10^{-21}$ --- $10^{-18}$ m, a thousandth
of a proton --- against the noise of seismic motion and of the photons
themselves.
\end{remark}

\begin{method}[Using a Michelson]\label{met:b2:michelson:method}
(1) Identify the configuration: parallel plate (rings at infinity;
observe with a lens or the eye at infinity) or wedge (fringes on the
mirrors; focus on $M_2$). (2) Write $\delta = 2e\cos i$ or $2\alpha x$ and the
order. (3) Translate each measurement into fringes: $\Delta e = N\lambda/2$,
$2(n - 1)\ell$ for a plate, $\lambda^2/2\Delta\lambda$ for a doublet. (4) Use the
contact position ($e = 0$, white fringe) as the absolute reference;
note that the rings' radii \emph{shrink} as $e$ grows. (5) Coherence
limits: rings are visible while $2e < \ell_c$; the wedge needs
near-normal incidence.
\end{method}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:1}
A Michelson in sodium light ($\lambda = \qty{589}{nm}$): the mirror is moved by
\qty{0.10}{mm}: number of rings passing the centre; displacement
corresponding to $250$ fringes; precision on $e$ if a tenth of a fringe
can be read.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:2}
Parallel plate of thickness $e = \qty{0.50}{mm}$, lens $f = \qty{1.0}{m}$, $\lambda =
\qty{589}{nm}$: order at the centre; is the centre bright? Radii of the
first three bright rings; what happens to the rings as $e$ is reduced
to \qty{0.05}{mm}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:3}
Air wedge with $\alpha = \qty{1.0e-4}{rad}$, $\lambda = \qty{633}{nm}$: fringe spacing;
number of fringes across \qty{2}{cm} of mirror; the wedge angle is
doubled: what happens? How is the contact found with white light?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:4}
A glass plate \qty{0.10}{mm} thick is inserted in one arm: fringe shift for
$n = 1.52$ at \qty{589}{nm}; the shift is measured to $0.2$ fringe: precision
on $n$. Why could the same measurement not be made with white light
if the plate were \qty{1}{cm} thick?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:5}
\emph{Sodium doublet.} (a) Thickness $e$ between two successive
disappearances of the rings, for $\Delta\lambda = \qty{0.597}{nm}$. (b) A student
measures $17$ disappearances over \qty{5.00}{mm}: $\Delta\lambda$ and its precision.
(c) Orders of the two lines at $e = \qty{0.29}{mm}$: check they differ by
$\tfrac12$. (d) Why is the contrast never exactly zero (the two lines have
intensities in the ratio $2 : 1$): minimum contrast.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:6}
\emph{Index of air.} A \qty{10.0}{cm} cell in one arm is evacuated, then
slowly filled with air at \qty{1}{bar}; $94$ fringes pass ($\lambda = \qty{589}{nm}$).
(a) $n - 1$ for air. (b) Fringes passing for a \qty{1}{\%} change of
pressure; temperature coefficient if $n - 1 \propto \rho$. (c) Why does this
matter for the stability of any interferometer in air? (d) Precision on
$n - 1$ from a tenth of a fringe.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:7}
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{Coherence length}.} With a low-pressure mercury lamp (green line,
\qty{546}{nm}, width \qty{0.01}{nm}) the rings fade as $e$ grows. (a) \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{Coherence
length}; thickness $e$ at which the contrast becomes negligible. (b) Same
with a high-pressure lamp ($\Delta\lambda = \qty{1}{nm}$). (c) With a He--Ne laser
($\Delta\nu = \qty{1}{MHz}$): can one still see rings with $e = \qty{10}{m}$? (d) What
limits white-light fringes to $\pm2$ orders around contact?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:8}
\emph{\omterm{def:b2:michelson:instrument}{Compensating plate}.} Without $C$, one beam crosses the splitter's
glass ($e_g = \qty{5}{mm}$, $n = 1.52$) three times and the other once. (a)
Extra \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical path}; can it be compensated by moving a mirror for
monochromatic light? (b) Why not for white light (dispersion: $n$
varies by $0.01$ across the visible)? (c) What tolerance on the
parallelism of $C$ and $Sp$ keeps the white fringe (take a residual
difference of thickness $< \qty{0.3}{\micro m}$)? (d) Modern splitters are
thin pellicles or cubes: why is $C$ unnecessary then?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:9}
\emph{Michelson--Morley.} Arms of $L = \qty{11}{m}$ (folded), $\lambda = \qty{550}{nm}$,
Earth's orbital speed $v = \qty{30}{km/s}$. (a) In the ether theory the
round trip along the arm parallel to $\vect v$ takes $2L/c(1 - v^2/c^2)$
and the perpendicular one $2L/c\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$: difference, to lowest
order. (b) Fringe shift expected on rotating the apparatus by \qty{90}{\degree}
(the roles of the arms swap). (c) They could detect $0.01$ fringe: upper
bound on $v$. (d) What does a null result say, and what did Einstein
conclude?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:10}
\emph{Fourier spectroscopy.} The intensity at the centre of the rings
is $I(e) = \int S(\nu)[1 + \cos(4\pi\nu e/c)]\dd\nu$ for a source of spectrum
$S(\nu)$. (a) For a single line: $I(e)$; period in $e$. (b) For a Gaussian
line of width $\Delta\nu$: show the fringes have a Gaussian envelope of
width $\sim c/2\Delta\nu$ in $e$ (admit the Fourier transform of a Gaussian
is a Gaussian). (c) Resolution: two lines $\Delta\nu$ apart are separated if
the scan reaches $e_{\max} \approx c/2\Delta\nu$; for $e_{\max} = \qty{10}{cm}$, the
resolving power $\lambda/\Delta\lambda$ at \qty{1}{\micro m}. (d) Why is this method
preferred in the infrared (signal, "multiplex" advantage)?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:11}
\emph{\omterm{rem:b2:michelson:history}{Gravitational-wave detector}.} Arms $L = \qty{4}{km}$, light recycled
to $N = 300$ round trips, $\lambda = \qty{1064}{nm}$, the wave changes the arms by
$\Delta L/L = 10^{-21}$ in opposite directions. (a) Change of \omterm{thm:b2:two-wave-interference:formula}{path difference};
phase shift. (b) The instrument is held on a dark fringe and measures
the small intensity $I = I_0\sin^2(\Delta\varphi/2)$: intensity change for the
signal. (c) With \qty{100}{W} of circulating light at \qty{1064}{nm}, photons
per second; the photon noise in \qty{1}{ms} ($\sqrt N$) against the signal:
is it detectable? What does more power buy? (d) Why are the arms
kilometres long, and why the mirrors suspended?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:michelson:12}
\emph{Localization.} (a) Show that for the wedge the two rays leaving a
point $P$ of $M_2$ for an observer at infinity (incidence $i$) have the
\omterm{thm:b2:two-wave-interference:formula}{path difference} $2e(P)\cos i$ plus a term $\propto\alpha i$ that cancels for
$i = 0$: the fringes are on the mirrors at normal incidence only. (b)
An \omterm{prop:b2:two-wave-interference:spatial}{extended source} illuminates the wedge with incidences up to
$i_{\max}$: what must $i_{\max}$ satisfy for the fringes to stay sharp
($2e\,i_{\max}^2/2 < \lambda/4$ at the edge of the field, $e = \qty{20}{\micro m}$)? (c)
For the parallel plate the rings do not move when the eye moves:
why? (d) A student sees fringes that drift when she moves her head:
which configuration is she in, and what should she adjust?
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The sodium doublet and the index of air}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a complete measurement session on
a Michelson interferometer: finding contact, reading lengths, splitting
the yellow line, weighing the air}]
\label{pb:b2:michelson:1}

The interferometer has a micrometer screw reading \qty{1}{\micro m}; the
sodium lamp gives $\lambda_1 = \qty{589.0}{nm}$ and $\lambda_2 = \qty{589.6}{nm}$ with
intensities $2 : 1$; a white lamp and a He--Ne laser (\qty{632.8}{nm}) are
also available.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- Setting up.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item With the laser, the student sees two spots of the two arms on
        a card; she superposes them with the tilt screws and then sees
        straight fringes: which configuration is this, and what does
        their spacing tell about the residual tilt (spacing \qty{2}{mm})?
  \item Turning the tilt screws, the fringes curve and then become
        rings: why do rings appear, and where is $M_1'$ now?
  \item She translates the mirror and counts $158$ rings swallowed by
        the centre: displacement; in which direction is $e$ changing?
  \item As she goes on, the rings spread and the central spot grows
        to fill the field: what has she reached? The sodium lamp
        replaces the laser and she sees nothing special: why not, and
        why does white light show a bright central fringe flanked by
        coloured ones at that point?
  \item She tilts $M_1$ very slightly at contact and sees, in white
        light, straight coloured fringes: explain, and say how many
        she can expect on each side of the white one.
  \item The laser spots are \qty{10}{mm} across and she wants ten rings
        in the field of a lens of $f = \qty{20}{cm}$ ($\lambda = \qty{632.8}{nm}$):
        thickness $e$ needed (use the radius of the $k$-th ring).
  \item Why must the \omterm{def:b2:michelson:instrument}{compensating plate} be parallel to the splitter,
        and what would she see in white light if it were removed?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The doublet.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Starting from contact with the sodium lamp, she increases $e$:
        the rings' contrast falls, vanishes, returns. Thickness of the
        first disappearance; of the $n$-th.
  \item She records $12$ disappearances between $e = \qty{0.15}{mm}$ and
        $e = \qty{3.35}{mm}$ (the first and the last read on the screw):
        $\Delta\lambda$; precision if each reading is good to \qty{5}{\micro m}.
  \item Orders $p_1$ and $p_2$ of the two lines at the first
        disappearance; at the first return of full contrast.
  \item Minimum contrast at a disappearance, given the $2 : 1$
        intensity ratio (the weaker line's fringes remain).
  \item How many disappearances can she count before the natural
        width of the lines (\qty{0.02}{nm} each) washes everything out?
  \item What would a lamp with three lines of equal spacing show?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The air.}
A cell of length $\ell = \qty{5.00}{cm}$ with glass windows is placed in one
arm and pumped down; air is let in slowly while the laser fringes are
counted.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Optical \omterm{thm:b2:two-wave-interference:formula}{path difference} created by filling the cell to
        \qty{1}{atm} if $n - 1 = \qty{2.9e-4}{}$; number of fringes.
  \item She counts $46$ fringes: her value of $n - 1$.
  \item She lets in only \qty{0.20}{bar}: fringes expected ($n - 1 \propto$
        pressure).
  \item The room warms by \qty{3}{K} during the measurement, and the
        \qty{40}{cm} of open air path in each arm change index by
        $-\qty{1e-6}{}$ per kelvin: fringe drift; is it a problem for
        Part II, for Part III?
  \item Why do the cell's windows not shift the measurement (they are
        there during the whole count)?
  \item The experiment is repeated with CO$_2$ ($n - 1 = \qty{4.5e-4}{}$):
        fringes for a full cell; how could the gas be identified from
        the count alone?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Limits.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item The screw is read to \qty{1}{\micro m}: precision of a length
        measured by fringe counting over \qty{1}{cm}, versus by the screw
        alone.
  \item A vibration of \qty{50}{nm} amplitude at \qty{100}{Hz} shakes one
        mirror: fringe motion; what does the eye see, what does a
        camera at $25$ frames per second see?
  \item The laser's frequency drifts by \qty{1e-8}{} in relative value
        during a count over $e = \qty{1}{cm}$: error in fringes.
  \item A \qty{10}{mm} gauge block is certified to $\lambda/20$ by
        interferometry: absolute and relative precision.
  \item Why is the Michelson the instrument of choice for lengths, and
        the grating (next chapter) for spectra?
  \item Sum up the four quantities measured (a length, a doublet
        splitting, an index, a \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{coherence length}) and the fringe
        observation that gave each.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
