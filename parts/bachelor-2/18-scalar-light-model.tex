\chapter{The Scalar Model of Light}\label{ch:b2:scalar-light-model}

Shine two torches on the same wall and the patch is simply twice as
bright; shine two beams from one laser on it and the patch breaks into
bright and dark fringes. The torches add their intensities, the laser
beams add their amplitudes --- and every interferometer, hologram and
diffraction grating of the coming chapters rests on knowing when light
does which. This chapter sets up the tools: light as a \emph{scalar}
wave with a phase, the \emph{\omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical path}} that measures that phase
along a ray, the way a source emits --- in short \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{wave trains} whose
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{coherence length}} decides whether two waves can interfere --- and
the way a detector records it: an average over billions of periods
that keeps only the intensity.

\section{Light as a scalar wave}

\begin{definition}[Scalar model; monochromatic wave]\label{def:b2:scalar-light-model:scalar}
\index{scalar model of light}\index{vibration (optical)}\index{monochromatic wave}
In most of wave optics the vector nature of light plays no role (the
superposed waves share one polarization, or the light is unpolarized
and the polarizations average out): the field is replaced by a real
\emph{scalar vibration}
\[
s(M, t) = a(M)\cos\bigl(\omega t - \varphi(M)\bigr) ,
\]
with an amplitude $a(M)$ and a phase $\varphi(M)$ at each point, both
slowly varying on the scale of a wavelength. A \emph{monochromatic}
wave has a single $\omega$: an idealization, since a real source emits
over a band of frequencies. Visible light: $\lambda_0$ from $400$ to
\qty{750}{nm} in vacuum, $\nu = c/\lambda_0$ from $7.5 \times 10^{14}$ to $4 \times 10^{14}$ Hz,
periods of about \qty{2}{fs}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Optical path and phase]\label{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}
\index{optical path}\index{phase (optical)}\index{wavefront}\index{Malus--Dupin theorem}
In a medium of index $n$ the wave travels at $c/n$ and its wavelength
is $\lambda_0/n$. The \emph{optical path} along a curve from $A$ to $B$ is
\[
(AB) = \int_A^Bn\,\dd s ,
\]
and the phase accumulated by a \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:scalar}{monochromatic wave} travelling along a
ray from $A$ to $B$ is
\[
\varphi(B) - \varphi(A) = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}\,(AB) = \omega\,\tau_{AB} ,
\]
$\tau_{AB}$ being the travel time. A \emph{wavefront} is a surface of
equal phase; in an isotropic medium the rays of geometrical optics are
perpendicular to the wavefronts (Malus--Dupin theorem), and the optical
path between two wavefronts is the same along every ray.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Justification]
The phase advances by $2\pi$ per local wavelength $\lambda_0/n$, i.e.\ by
$2\pi n\,\dd s/\lambda_0$ per element $\dd s$; summed along the ray. The wave
$a\cos(\omega t - \varphi)$ is a solution of the \omterm{thm:b2:waves-on-strings:equation}{wave equation} in which the
energy travels along $\operatorname{\vect{grad}}\varphi$, perpendicular to the
surfaces $\varphi = $ const: that is the ray, and the "\omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical path} = same
between two \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{wavefronts}" statement is the definition of a \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{wavefront}
read backward. The full theorem (it survives reflections and
refractions) is admitted.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Stigmatism; the lens as a phase plate]\label{ex:b2:scalar-light-model:lens}
\index{stigmatism}\index{thin lens (phase)}
A point source $A$ imaged by a perfect (stigmatic) instrument at $A'$:
all the rays from $A$ to $A'$ have the \emph{same \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical path}} --- the
waves arriving along every ray are in phase at $A'$, which is why they
pile up into a bright point there. For a thin lens of focal length $f$
this is a statement about phase: a plane wave arriving along the axis
must leave as a \omterm{prop:b2:sound-waves:spherical}{spherical wave} converging to the focus $F'$; since a
\omterm{prop:b2:sound-waves:spherical}{spherical wave} centred at distance $f$ has, at distance $r$ from the axis,
the phase $2\pi r^2/2\lambda f$ ahead of its axial value (paraxial
approximation, $\sqrt{f^2 + r^2} \approx f + r^2/2f$), the lens must
\emph{retard} the wave by $\varphi_L(r) = -\pi r^2/\lambda f$: it is thicker at
the centre by exactly the amount that makes all \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical paths} to $F'$
equal. A lens is a phase plate; a hologram or a liquid-crystal display
that imposes the same phase map is a lens.
\end{example}

\section{How light is emitted: coherence}

\begin{proposition}[Wave trains; coherence time and length]\label{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}
\index{wave train}\index{coherence time}\index{coherence length}\index{spectral width}
A classical source (a flame, a lamp, a star) is a collection of atoms
each emitting, at random instants and with random phases, short
\emph{\omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{wave trains}} of duration $\tau_c$: the vibration it produces at a
point keeps a steady phase only over $\tau_c$ and then jumps. A train of
duration $\tau_c$ is not monochromatic: its spectrum spans a frequency
band $\Delta\nu \approx 1/\tau_c$ (a Fourier reciprocity, admitted: a finite
sinusoid cannot have a single frequency). The \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{coherence time}}
$\tau_c$ and the \emph{\omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{coherence length}}
\[
\ell_c = c\,\tau_c \approx \frac c{\Delta\nu} = \frac{\lambda^2}{\Delta\lambda}
\]
characterize the source: white light $\Delta\lambda \approx \qty{300}{nm}$, $\ell_c
\approx \qty{1}{\micro m}$; a spectral lamp ($\Delta\lambda \sim \qty{0.01}{nm}$), $\ell_c \sim
\qty{3}{cm}$; a laser, metres to kilometres. Two waves derived from the
same source can interfere only if their path difference is smaller
than $\ell_c$ --- beyond it they belong to different trains, with
unrelated phases.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\ell_c = c/\Delta\nu$ and $|\Delta\nu/\nu| = |\Delta\lambda/\lambda|$ with $\nu = c/\lambda$ give $\ell_c =
\lambda^2/\Delta\lambda$. The \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{spectral width} itself has physical causes: the
natural width (radiation damping, \cref{ch:b2:dipole-radiation}), the
Doppler broadening by the atoms' thermal motion, collisions --- each
shortening the train.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Two sources, one source]\label{rem:b2:scalar-light-model:twosources}
\index{incoherent sources}
Two distinct lamps (or two distinct atoms) emit trains with
independent random phases: their relative phase changes every $\tau_c
\sim \qty{1e-9}{s}$, a million times faster than any eye or camera
responds --- the interference term averages to zero and the
intensities add. Interference needs two waves that \emph{remember the
same phase jumps}: two copies of one wave, obtained by splitting it
(two slits, two mirrors, the two faces of a film) and recombined with a
delay shorter than $\tau_c$. A laser, whose trains last microseconds or
more, relaxes this constraint enormously; it never removes it.
\end{remark}

\section{Detectors and intensity}

\begin{proposition}[What a detector measures]\label{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:intensity}
\index{intensity (optics)}\index{detector (optics)}\index{photon flux}
Every light detector --- the eye (response time $\sim\qty{0.1}{s}$), a
photographic film, a CCD pixel (\qty{1}{ms}), a photodiode (\qty{1}{ns}) ---
responds on a time long compared with the period ($10^{-15}$ s) and
delivers the \emph{intensity}, the time average of the squared
vibration,
\[
I = K\,\langle s^2\rangle = \tfrac12K\,a^2 ,
\]
($K$ a constant, often dropped; $I$ is proportional to the irradiance
in \unit{W/m^2}). The absolute level matters in one respect only: light
arrives in photons of energy $h\nu$, and a detector counts on average
$I/h\nu$ photons per unit area and time.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\langle\cos^2(\omega t - \varphi)\rangle = \tfrac12$ over any interval much longer
than $2\pi/\omega$. Photons: the last chapter of the Year 1 volume; this is
where the scalar wave meets the quantum.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Orders of magnitude]\label{ex:b2:scalar-light-model:orders}
A \qty{60}{W} bulb radiating \qty{3}{W} of visible light at \qty{2}{m}:
$I = 3/4\pi\cdot4 = \qty{60}{mW/m^2}$, $I/h\nu = 1.6 \times 10^{17}$ photons per square
metre and second, $3 \times 10^{12}$ per second through a \qty{5}{mm} pupil ---
in the eye's \qty{0.1}{s} there is no trace of the graininess. The
faintest star the eye sees sends $\sim 10^3$ photons per second into the
pupil; a rod cell fires on a handful. Sunlight: \qty{1}{kW/m^2}, $3 \times 10^{21}$
photons per square metre and second.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Coherent and incoherent superposition]\label{rem:b2:scalar-light-model:superposition}
\index{coherent superposition}\index{incoherent superposition}
Two vibrations $a_1\cos(\omega t - \varphi_1)$ and $a_2\cos(\omega t - \varphi_2)$ at a
point give $s = s_1 + s_2$ and
\[
I = \tfrac12K\bigl[a_1^2 + a_2^2 + 2a_1a_2\langle\cos(\varphi_1 - \varphi_2)\rangle\bigr] .
\]
If the phase difference is stable during the detection
(\emph{coherent} waves): $I = I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos(\varphi_1 - \varphi_2)$,
the interference term, which the next chapter studies. If it wanders
randomly (\emph{incoherent} waves): $\langle\cos\rangle = 0$ and $I = I_1 +
I_2$ --- the torches.
\end{remark}

\begin{method}[Setting up an optics problem]\label{met:b2:scalar-light-model:method}
(1) Write each wave as $a\cos(\omega t - \varphi)$ with $\varphi = 2\pi(\text{optical
path})/\lambda_0$ from the source (or from a common \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{wavefront}). (2) Decide
coherence: same source, path difference $< \ell_c$? Then add amplitudes
(complex notation is convenient: $\underline s = a\eu^{\iu(\omega t - \varphi)}$, $I =
\tfrac12K|\underline s|^2$); otherwise add intensities. (3) For lenses and
mirrors, use equal \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical paths} between conjugate points, or the
phase-plate picture. (4) Convert to what is measured: intensity,
contrast, photon counts, and check the detector's resolution in time
and space.
\end{method}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:1}
A beam of \qty{600}{nm} crosses \qty{1.0}{cm} of glass ($n = 1.5$). \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{Optical
path}; number of wavelengths inside; extra phase and extra delay
compared with \qty{1}{cm} of air; thickness of glass that delays the
wave by exactly one wavelength relative to air.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:2}
\omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{Coherence time} and length of: white light ($400$--\qty{750}{nm}); a sodium
lamp line of width \qty{0.02}{nm} at \qty{589}{nm}; a red LED (\qty{630}{nm},
$\Delta\lambda = \qty{25}{nm}$); a multimode laser diode ($\Delta\nu = \qty{100}{GHz}$); a
stabilized laser ($\Delta\nu = \qty{1}{kHz}$). Which could give fringes with a
path difference of \qty{1}{mm}? of \qty{10}{m}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:3}
A \qty{1}{mW} laser pointer at \qty{650}{nm}: photons per second; a
\qty{100}{W} bulb (\qty{5}{W} visible, mean \qty{550}{nm}) at \qty{3}{m}: photons per
second through a \qty{6}{mm} pupil; number during the eye's \qty{0.1}{s};
relative fluctuation $1/\sqrt N$ of that number --- is the light grainy?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:4}
A point source at \qty{1.0}{m} from a screen, $\lambda = \qty{500}{nm}$. \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{Optical
path} difference between the centre of the screen and a point \qty{1}{cm}
off axis (exactly, then with the paraxial formula $r^2/2d$); number of
wavelengths; radius of the \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{wavefront} at the screen; at what off-axis
distance does the paraxial formula err by one wavelength?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:5}
\emph{The lens as a phase plate.} (a) A plane wave along the axis has a
uniform phase on the plane of a thin lens; after the lens the phase is
$\varphi_L(r) = -\pi r^2/\lambda f$ relative to the centre. Show that the emerging
\omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{wavefront} is, in the paraxial approximation, a sphere of radius $f$
centred on $F'$. (b) A point source at distance $d$ before the lens
sends a wave whose phase on the lens plane is $+\pi r^2/\lambda d$ (relative to
the centre): show that after the lens the wave converges at $d'$ with
$1/d' = 1/f - 1/d$, the lens formula. (c) Thickness profile of a
plano-convex glass lens ($n = 1.5$) of focal length \qty{10}{cm}: $e(r) =
e_0 - r^2/2(n - 1)f$; sag at $r = \qty{1}{cm}$. (d) A "Fresnel lens" folds
the profile back every time it exceeds $\lambda/(n - 1)$: why does it still
focus, and why only one colour perfectly?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:6}
\emph{Stigmatism of a mirror.} A mirror must send a plane wave arriving
along its axis to a point $F$. (a) Write the condition "equal \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical
paths} from a plane \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{wavefront} to $F$ for every point of the mirror" and
show it defines a parabola $y^2 = 4fx$ with $F$ at its focus. (b) Why is a
spherical mirror only approximately stigmatic (compare $y^2 = 4fx$ with
the circle of radius $2f$ near the vertex)? (c) For a \qty{20}{cm} mirror
of $f = \qty{1}{m}$, the path error of the sphere at the edge, in
wavelengths of \qty{500}{nm}. (d) Why do radio telescopes tolerate
sphericity that optical ones cannot?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:7}
\emph{Fermat.} The \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical path} from $A$ (in medium $n_1$) to $B$ (in
$n_2$) through a point $P$ of the plane interface is $n_1AP + n_2PB$. (a)
Show that it is stationary (minimal) for the point $P$ satisfying
$n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$. (b) Interpret: neighbouring paths have the
same phase, so the waves along them add --- this is why light
"chooses" the Descartes ray. (c) Derive the law of reflection the same
way. (d) Why is the path through a lens from $A$ to its image $A'$ the
same along every ray, and not merely stationary?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:8}
\emph{Why two lamps do not interfere.} Two sodium lamps illuminate a
screen; at a point, their waves have amplitudes $a$ and a relative
phase $\Delta\varphi$ that jumps randomly every $\tau_c = \qty{1e-10}{s}$. (a)
Intensity at that point as a function of $\Delta\varphi$; its maximum and
minimum. (b) The detector averages over $\tau_d = \qty{1}{ms}$: how many
independent values of $\Delta\varphi$? Mean intensity; relative size of the
residual fluctuation of the interference term ($\sim 1/\sqrt N$). (c) A
photodiode with $\tau_d = \qty{1e-11}{s}$: what would it see? (d) How did
the first experiment showing interference between two independent
lasers (1963) manage it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:9}
\emph{Apparent depth.} A coin lies at depth $h$ in water ($n = 1.33$).
(a) Using the \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical path} (or Descartes' law at small angles), show
that an eye above sees it at depth $h/n$. (b) $h = \qty{1.2}{m}$: apparent
depth; by how much does the \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical path} from the coin to the eye
exceed its geometric length? (c) A fish at depth $h$ sees a fly \qty{1}{m}
above the surface at what apparent height? (d) Why does a pool look
shallower still when viewed obliquely?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:10}
\emph{Contrast and coherence.} Two copies of a wave with a path
difference $\delta$ are superposed; the source's spectrum is spread
uniformly over $[\nu_0 - \Delta\nu/2, \nu_0 + \Delta\nu/2]$, each frequency
interfering with itself only. (a) Intensity from one frequency: $2I_0
[1 + \cos(2\pi\nu\delta/c)]$. (b) Integrate over the band and show that the
total is $2I_0[1 + \operatorname{sinc}(\pi\Delta\nu\,\delta/c)\cos(2\pi\nu_0\delta/c)]$ with
$\operatorname{sinc}x = \sin x/x$. (c) Define the contrast $V = (I_{\max} -
I_{\min})/(I_{\max} + I_{\min})$ of the fringes near $\delta$ and show $V =
|\operatorname{sinc}(\pi\Delta\nu\,\delta/c)|$: it vanishes at $\delta = c/\Delta\nu = \ell_c$. (d)
Numbers for a LED ($\Delta\lambda = \qty{25}{nm}$ at \qty{630}{nm}): $\delta$ at which the
contrast first vanishes.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:11}
\emph{Seeing stars.} The eye detects a flash when about $10$ photons
reach a rod within \qty{0.1}{s}; the dark-adapted pupil is \qty{7}{mm}; take
\qty{500}{nm}. (a) Minimum \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:intensity}{photon flux} and irradiance at the eye. (b) The
Sun gives \qty{1}{kW/m^2}; a star of magnitude $m$ gives $10^{-0.4(m + 26.7)}$
of it: irradiance of a magnitude-6 star (the naked-eye limit) and the
photon rate into the pupil; consistent with (a)? (c) A telescope of
\qty{200}{mm} aperture: gain in photons, and the magnitude it reaches.
(d) Why do astronomers speak of the "photon noise" of a faint image,
and how does it scale with exposure time?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:scalar-light-model:12}
\emph{Beyond paraxial.} A \omterm{prop:b2:sound-waves:spherical}{spherical wave} from a point at distance $d$
has, at the transverse distance $r$, the exact extra path $\sqrt{d^2 +
r^2} - d$. (a) Expand to fourth order: $r^2/2d - r^4/8d^3$. (b) The
quadratic (paraxial) term is kept and the quartic dropped when the
latter is below $\lambda/4$: show this requires $r^4 < 2\lambda d^3$. (c) Numbers:
$d = \qty{1}{m}$, $\lambda = \qty{500}{nm}$: maximum $r$; $d = \qty{1}{cm}$ (a microscope):
maximum $r$, and the corresponding angle. (d) Comment: the
"aberrations" of geometrical optics are these neglected terms.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: A sodium lamp and a laser pointer}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- two light sources taken apart: the
yellow lamp of the old street, the red pointer in the lecture hall, and
the one number that decides whether each can make fringes}]
\label{pb:b2:scalar-light-model:1}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The sodium lamp.}
Sodium emits its yellow doublet at $\lambda_1 = \qty{589.0}{nm}$ and $\lambda_2 =
\qty{589.6}{nm}$. The natural width of each line is $\Delta\nu_{\text{nat}} =
\qty{10}{MHz}$; in the lamp the atoms are at \qty{500}{K} (sodium mass
\qty{23}{u}, $k_B = \qty{1.38e-23}{J/K}$, $u = \qty{1.66e-27}{kg}$).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Frequencies of the two lines and their difference.
  \item \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{Coherence length} of a single line if only its natural width
        counted.
  \item Doppler broadening: an atom moving at $v$ along the line of
        sight emits at $\nu(1 + v/c)$; with the thermal rms speed $\sqrt{k_BT
        /m}$ along one axis, estimate $\Delta\nu_D$ and compare with the
        natural width.
  \item \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{Coherence length} of one Doppler-broadened line; in a real
        high-pressure lamp collisions broaden the line a further ten
        times: \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{coherence length} then.
  \item The two lines together: over what path difference do their
        fringe systems go from coincidence to opposition and back
        (the "beat length" $\lambda^2/\Delta\lambda$)? How many fringes is that?
  \item A student makes a two-beam interferometer with the lamp and
        increases the path difference from zero: describe the
        contrast seen --- the beats and the final extinction --- with
        the numbers found.
  \item The lamp radiates \qty{1}{W} of yellow light: photons per second;
        energy of one photon in eV, and why the light is yellow.
  \item Two sodium lamps side by side: can their lights interfere?
        Why is the question different for the two lines of one lamp?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The laser pointer.}
A \qty{1}{mW} red diode laser at \qty{650}{nm} emits, when cheap, several
longitudinal modes spread over $\Delta\lambda = \qty{0.5}{nm}$; a stabilized
single-mode one has $\Delta\nu = \qty{1}{MHz}$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{Coherence lengths} of the two pointers.
  \item Photons emitted per second; mean spacing between photons along
        the beam (in metres); compare with the \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{coherence length} ---
        what does that say about "photons interfering"?
  \item Convert the \qty{0.5}{nm} spread into a frequency width; the
        diode's cavity is \qty{1}{mm} long with $n = 3.5$: spacing of its
        longitudinal modes ($c/2nL$) and the number of modes in the
        spread.
  \item The beam (\qty{1}{mm} diameter) falls on a photodiode whose
        every photon yields an electron: current.
  \item The cheap pointer is used in a two-beam interferometer with a
        \qty{2}{mm} path difference: fringes or not? And the stabilized one
        with \qty{10}{m}?
  \item The diode's output is in fact switched on and off in
        \qty{1}{ns} pulses: can the pulses from two successive periods
        interfere with each other? What sets the coherence here, the
        pulse length or the line width?
  \item Why does a laser's light look "speckled" on a wall, and why
        does the lamp's not?
  \item A red LED at \qty{650}{nm} has $\Delta\lambda = \qty{25}{nm}$: \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{coherence
        length}; why do some projectors prefer LEDs to lasers?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{Optical paths} and detectors.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item A \qty{2}{mm} glass slide ($n = 1.5$) is inserted in one arm of
        the interferometer: extra \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical path}, and the number of
        fringes that shift past a mark.
  \item The slide is tilted by \qty{10}{\degree}: extra path (use $e/\cos r$
        for the length in the glass, minus the air it replaces, to
        first approximation $e(n/\cos r - \cos(i - r)/\cos r)$ --- or simply
        estimate with the longer geometric path); order of magnitude
        of the fringe shift.
  \item The detector is a camera with \qty{10}{\micro m} pixels; the fringes
        are \qty{50}{\micro m} apart: how many pixels per fringe, and what
        happens to the measured contrast if the spacing falls to
        \qty{10}{\micro m}?
  \item Exposure \qty{10}{ms} at \qty{1}{\micro W/cm^2} on a pixel: photons per
        pixel; relative photon noise.
  \item Two equal coherent waves of intensity $I_0$ meet with a path
        difference $\delta$: write the intensity $I(\delta)$ and the period
        of the fringes in $\delta$.
  \item The fringes drift past a point at \qty{1}{kHz} (a vibrating
        mirror): what does the eye see, what does the photodiode see?
  \item The interferometer's arms run through \qty{10}{m} of air ($n - 1 =
        \qty{2.9e-4}{}$): \omterm{def:b2:scalar-light-model:path}{optical path} in excess of vacuum, in fringes;
        what a \qty{1}{\%} change of air pressure does to the pattern.
  \item The lamp and the pointer give the same \qty{1}{\micro W/cm^2}: which
        gives more photons per second, and why does it not matter for
        the fringes?
  \item Sum up: for each source, the \omterm{prop:b2:scalar-light-model:coherence}{coherence length}, what limits it,
        and the interferometer path difference it allows.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
