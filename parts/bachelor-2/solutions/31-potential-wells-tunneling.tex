\begin{solution}{pb:b2:potential-wells-tunneling:1}
\textbf{1.} $\kappa = \sqrt{2m\Phi}/\hbar = \qty{1.09e10}{m^{-1}}$.

\textbf{2.} $\eu^{2.2} = 8.8$; $\eu^{0.022}$: $2.2\%$ per picometre.

\textbf{3.} \qty{0.5}{pm}.

\textbf{4.} $6 \times 10^9$ per second; \qty{0.16}{ns} apart, against $\hbar/\Phi
\sim 10^{-16}\,\unit{s}$: one at a time.

\textbf{5.} About half; the rest decays so fast with distance that the
image is that of one atom.

\textbf{6.} $\times 8.8$; constant current keeps the tip safe and turns
the exponential into a linear height.

\textbf{7.} $\Delta T = 10^{-12}/(10^{-5} \times 0.02) = \qty{5e-6}{K}$; symmetric
mounts, speed, low-expansion materials, and a \omterm{def:b2:feedback-oscillators:loop}{feedback} that follows the
drift.

\textbf{8.} A trapezoid, lowered on the collecting side: the effective
barrier thins and $I$ grows faster than linearly.

\textbf{9.} $\kappa' = \qty{0.89e10}{m^{-1}}$; at \qty{0.5}{nm} the exponent drops from
$10.9$ to $8.9$, $I \times 7$: the tip retracts by $\ln 7/2\kappa' \approx \qty{0.1}{nm}$
--- a bump that is electronic, not geometric.

\textbf{10.} $V(R_{\text{n}}) = 2 \times 90 \times 1.44/8 = \qty{32}{MeV}$; $b = \qty{62}{fm}$.

\textbf{11.} $v = \qty{1.4e7}{m/s}$; $9 \times 10^{20}$ hits per second.

\textbf{12.} $G = 96$; $T = \eu^{-96} \approx 10^{-42}$.

\textbf{13.} Rate $\sim 10^{-21}\,\unit{s^{-1}}$, half-life $\sim 10^{13}$ years --- a
thousand times the measured value: for an exponent of a hundred, a
crude model does well to land within a few orders of magnitude.

\textbf{14.} $G = 79$: $\eu^{17} \approx 10^7$ times shorter, about $10^6$ years ---
one extra MeV, seven orders of magnitude.

\textbf{15.} $G = 92$: $\eu^{4.5} \approx 90$ times shorter --- a fermi of radius
is two orders of magnitude.

\textbf{16.} A proton is not pre-formed with positive energy (it is
bound by some \qty{7}{MeV}); a $^{12}$C has three times the charge and an
exponent three times larger.

\textbf{17.} Yes: the $\alpha$ always has \qty{4.2}{MeV}; its \omterm{def:b2:schrodinger-wave-functions:psi}{wave function}
merely has an evanescent part under the barrier, where no measurement
finds it with negative kinetic energy.

\textbf{18.} \qty{23.7}{GHz}; \qty{1.27}{cm}.

\textbf{19.} $(|{\uparrow}\rangle \pm |{\downarrow}\rangle)/\sqrt2$; the
symmetric one is lower --- no node, less curvature.

\textbf{20.} $\psi = (\varphi_+\eu^{-\iu E_+t/\hbar} + \varphi_-\eu^{-\iu E_-t/\hbar})/\sqrt2$;
$P_{\downarrow} = \sin^2(\pi\nu t)$; period \qty{42}{ps}.

\textbf{21.} $\eu^{-\Delta/k_BT} = 0.996$: almost equal, no inversion; an
inhomogeneous electric field sorts the beam and sends the upper-state
molecules into the cavity.

\textbf{22.} \qty{1.6e-23}{J}; $6 \times 10^{12}$ molecules per second.

\textbf{23.} $\kappa a = 6.5$.

\textbf{24.} It is fixed by the molecule alone, identical for every
molecule, and insensitive to the outside; shifts come from the
\omterm{thm:b2:sound-waves:doppler}{Doppler effect} in the beam, stray electric fields, collisions and
the cavity's pulling.

\textbf{25.} Confinement quantises ($E_n \propto n^2/L^2$); evanescence lets
the wave through ($T \sim \eu^{-2\kappa a}$); the exponents are set by
$\sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar$ and the width --- mass, height, distance.
\end{solution}
