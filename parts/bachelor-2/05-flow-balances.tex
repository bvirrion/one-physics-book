\chapter{Momentum and Energy Balances in Flows}\label{ch:b2:flow-balances}

A firefighter braces against the hose: the water leaving at thirty
metres per second pushes back with hundreds of newtons, though nothing
touches the nozzle but water. A jet engine hangs from a wing and pulls
an aircraft through the sky by throwing air backward. A garden
sprinkler spins with no motor. In each case a fluid enters a region,
leaves it with a different momentum, and the difference is a force.
This chapter writes the laws of mechanics --- momentum, angular
momentum, energy --- not for a fixed mass of fluid but for the fluid
crossing a fixed region, an \emph{\omterm{def:b2:flow-balances:cv}{open system}}: the form in which
engineers size turbines, rockets, pumps and pipes.

\section{Balances for an open system}

\begin{definition}[Control volume; open system]\label{def:b2:flow-balances:cv}
\index{control volume}\index{open system}
A \emph{control volume} (or control surface) is a fixed region of space
$\Sigma$ through which fluid flows; the fluid inside it at any instant
is an \emph{open system}, which exchanges mass with the outside through
the inlets and outlets of $\Sigma$. The closed system to which Newton's
laws apply is the fluid that is inside $\Sigma$ at time $t$, followed
to $t + \dd t$: it then occupies $\Sigma$ minus what has left plus what
has entered.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Momentum balance in steady flow]\label{thm:b2:flow-balances:momentum}
\index{momentum balance (steady flow)}\index{momentum flux}
For a steady flow through a \omterm{def:b2:flow-balances:cv}{control volume} with one inlet (section
$S_1$, uniform velocity $\vect v_1$) and one outlet ($S_2$, $\vect v_2$), the
\omterm{def:b2:fluid-kinematics:flowrate}{mass flow rate} $D_m$ being the same at both,
\[
\sum\vect F_{\text{ext}} = D_m\,(\vect v_2 - \vect v_1) ,
\]
where $\sum\vect F_{\text{ext}}$ is the sum of all external forces on the
fluid inside $\Sigma$: weight, forces from walls and bodies in contact
with it, and the pressure forces on the inlet and outlet sections
($P_1S_1\vect n_1$ pushing inward, $-P_2S_2\vect n_2$ at the outlet). With
several inlets and outlets, the right side is the sum of the outgoing
\omterm{thm:b2:flow-balances:momentum}{momentum fluxes} $D_{m,i}\vect v_i$ minus the incoming ones.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Consider the closed system $\mathcal S$ made of the fluid in $\Sigma$ at
time $t$ plus the slab $\delta m = D_m\dd t$ about to enter through $S_1$.
At $t + \dd t$ it consists of the fluid in $\Sigma$ plus the slab $\delta m$
that has left through $S_2$. Its momentum went from $\vect p_\Sigma(t) +
\delta m\,\vect v_1$ to $\vect p_\Sigma(t + \dd t) + \delta m\,\vect v_2$; in steady
flow $\vect p_\Sigma$ is constant, so $\dd\vect p_{\mathcal S} = D_m\dd t\,(\vect v_2
- \vect v_1)$. Newton's second law for $\mathcal S$, whose external forces
are (to first order) those on the fluid in $\Sigma$, gives the result.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Jet on a wall; the fire hose]\label{ex:b2:flow-balances:jet}
A jet of section $s$ and speed $v$ strikes a wall perpendicularly and
spreads along it: the outgoing momentum along the jet is zero, so the
wall receives $F = D_mv = \rho sv^2$ --- \qty{1.1}{kN} for a \qty{5}{cm} jet
at \qty{24}{m/s}. By the same balance applied to the fluid in the hose
and nozzle, the nozzle pushes back on the firefighter with $D_mv$:
\qty{20}{L/s} at \qty{30}{m/s} give \qty{600}{N}, the weight of a person.
If the wall recedes at $u$, only $D_m' = \rho s(v - u)$ reaches it per unit
time and it arrives at the relative speed $v - u$: $F = \rho s(v - u)^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Pelton bucket; rocket thrust]\label{prop:b2:flow-balances:pelton}
\index{Pelton turbine}\index{thrust}\index{rocket equation}
(i) A jet of speed $v$ and \omterm{def:b2:fluid-kinematics:flowrate}{mass flow rate} $D_m$ is turned back through
$180^\circ$ by a bucket moving at $u$ in the jet's direction: the force
on the bucket is $F = 2D_m(v - u)$ (with $D_m$ the flow rate actually
intercepted by the moving buckets of a wheel), the power $Fu$ is
maximal for $u = v/2$ and then equals $\tfrac12D_mv^2$: the jet's whole
kinetic power. (ii) A rocket of mass $m(t)$ ejecting gas backward at
the relative speed $v_e$ and \omterm{def:b2:fluid-kinematics:flowrate}{mass flow rate} $D_m = -\dd m/\dd t$ feels the
thrust $F = D_mv_e$; in free space its speed increases by
$\Delta v = v_e\ln(m_0/m_1)$ (Tsiolkovsky's \omterm{prop:b2:flow-balances:pelton}{rocket equation}); under gravity,
$m\,\dd v/\dd t = D_mv_e - mg$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) In the bucket's frame the jet arrives at $v - u$ and leaves at
$-(v - u)$; the momentum balance gives $2D_m(v - u)$ (the bucket is
perfect: no loss of relative speed). Power $2D_mu(v - u)$, maximal at
$u = v/2$. (ii) Momentum of rocket plus gas ejected in $\dd t$: $(m + \dd m)
(v + \dd v) + (-\dd m)(v - v_e) = mv$ in free space, so $m\,\dd v = -\dd m\,v_e$
and $v_1 - v_0 = v_e\ln(m_0/m_1)$; with gravity the total momentum changes
by $-mg\,\dd t$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A launcher]\label{ex:b2:flow-balances:rocket}
First stage: $D_m = \qty{2500}{kg/s}$ at $v_e = \qty{3000}{m/s}$: thrust
\qty{7.5}{MN}, which lifts a \qty{600}{t} rocket at $7.5 \times 10^6/6 \times 10^5 - g
= \qty{2.7}{m/s^2}$ off the pad. Burning \qty{500}{t} in \qty{200}{s} gives
$\Delta v = 3000\ln6 - 9.81 \times 200 = 5400 - 2000 = \qty{3.4}{km/s}$ --- the
gravity loss is why launchers climb fast, and the logarithm why they
are staged.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Force on a pipe bend]\label{ex:b2:flow-balances:bend}
Water at $P$ and $v$ in a pipe of section $S$ turns through $90^\circ$. The
balance on the fluid in the elbow: $\vect F_{\text{wall}} + PS\vect e_x - PS
\vect e_y = D_m(v\vect e_y - v\vect e_x)$, so the wall pushes the fluid with
$\vect F_{\text{wall}} = -(PS + \rho Sv^2)(\vect e_x - \vect e_y)$ and the fluid
pushes the elbow outward with $(PS + \rho Sv^2)\sqrt2$. For $D = \qty{20}{cm}$,
$P = \qty{3}{bar}$, $v = \qty{3}{m/s}$: $PS = \qty{9.4}{kN}$, $\rho Sv^2 = \qty{0.28}{kN}$,
force \qty{13.7}{kN} along the bisector --- the pressure term dominates,
and it is what the anchor blocks of a pipeline hold.
\end{example}

\section{Angular momentum: turbines and sprinklers}

\begin{theorem}[Angular momentum balance; Euler's turbine equation]\label{thm:b2:flow-balances:angular}
\index{angular momentum balance (flow)}\index{Euler's turbine equation}
In steady flow, the total moment about a point $O$ of the external
forces on the fluid in $\Sigma$ equals the flux of angular momentum
out minus in: $\sum\vect{\mathcal M}_O = D_m(\vect r_2\wedge\vect v_2 - \vect r_1
\wedge\vect v_1)$. For a rotating machine (turbine or pump) of axis $Oz$,
with the fluid entering at radius $r_1$ with azimuthal velocity
$v_{\theta1}$ and leaving at $r_2$ with $v_{\theta2}$, the torque exerted by
the fluid on the rotor and the power it delivers are
\[
\Gamma = D_m(r_1v_{\theta1} - r_2v_{\theta2}) , \qquad
\mathcal P = \Gamma\omega = D_m\omega(r_1v_{\theta1} - r_2v_{\theta2}) .
\]
A turbine takes the swirl out of the fluid; a pump puts it in.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Same closed-system argument with $\vect r\wedge\vect v$ in place of
$\vect v$. The torque on the rotor is the opposite of the torque of the
rotor on the fluid; the pressure forces on the inlet and outlet
surfaces (which are surfaces of revolution) have no moment about the
axis.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The lawn sprinkler]\label{ex:b2:flow-balances:sprinkler}
Two arms of length $R$ eject water tangentially at the relative speed
$w$; the sprinkler turns at $\omega$. Water enters on the axis with no
angular momentum and leaves with $r v_\theta = R(w - R\omega)$ (absolute
azimuthal speed $w - R\omega$, opposite to the rotation): the torque on the
arms is $D_mR(w - R\omega)$. At rest it is $D_mRw$; with no friction the
sprinkler accelerates until $R\omega = w$ --- the water leaves with zero
absolute speed and the torque vanishes; with a friction torque
$\Gamma_f$ it settles at $\omega = (w - \Gamma_f/D_mR)/R$.
\end{example}

\section{Energy balance: pumps, turbines and losses}

\begin{theorem}[Mechanical energy balance of a steady flow]\label{thm:b2:flow-balances:energy}
\index{energy balance (flow)}\index{hydraulic power}\index{head}
For an incompressible fluid in steady flow through a machine (pump or
turbine) between an inlet 1 and an outlet 2, with $\mathcal P_u$ the
mechanical power delivered to the fluid by moving parts (positive for a
pump, negative for a turbine) and $\mathcal P_{\text{diss}} \ge 0$ the power
dissipated by \omterm{def:b2:viscous-flows:viscosity}{viscosity},
\[
D_V\Bigl[\bigl(P_2 + \tfrac12\rho v_2^2 + \rho gz_2\bigr) - \bigl(P_1 + \tfrac12\rho v_1^2
+ \rho gz_1\bigr)\Bigr] = \mathcal P_u - \mathcal P_{\text{diss}} .
\]
Dividing by $\rho gD_V$ gives the balance in \emph{heads} (metres of
fluid): $H_2 - H_1 = H_{\text{pump}} - H_{\text{loss}}$, with $H = P/\rho g +
v^2/2g + z$. For a \omterm{def:b2:euler-bernoulli:perfect}{perfect fluid} with no machine this is Bernoulli; the
\emph{\omterm{thm:b2:flow-balances:energy}{hydraulic power}} of a pump raising the head by $H_{\text{pump}}$ is
$\rho gD_VH_{\text{pump}}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Kinetic energy theorem for the closed system of the proof of
\cref{thm:b2:flow-balances:momentum}: in $\dd t$ its kinetic energy
changes by $D_m\dd t\,(v_2^2 - v_1^2)/2$; the work done on it is that of
gravity, $-D_m\dd t\,g(z_2 - z_1)$, of the pressure forces on the moving
end faces, $(P_1S_1v_1 - P_2S_2v_2)\dd t = (P_1 - P_2)D_V\dd t$, of the
moving parts, $\mathcal P_u\dd t$, and of the internal viscous forces,
$-\mathcal P_{\text{diss}}\dd t$ (the walls, at rest, do no work). Divide by
$\dd t$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Sizing a pump]\label{ex:b2:flow-balances:pump}
Water is to be lifted \qty{30}{m} at \qty{20}{L/s} through \qty{100}{m} of
\qty{10}{cm} pipe ($\lambda = 0.02$): $v = \qty{2.5}{m/s}$, head loss $\lambda(L/D)
v^2/2g = 0.02 \times 1000 \times 0.32 = \qty{6.4}{m}$, exit kinetic head
\qty{0.32}{m}: $H_{\text{pump}} = \qty{36.7}{m}$, \omterm{thm:b2:flow-balances:energy}{hydraulic power} $\rho gD_VH =
\qty{7.2}{kW}$, and \qty{10}{kW} of electricity at $70\%$ efficiency. The
pressure at the pump outlet is $\rho gH_{\text{pump}} \approx \qty{3.6}{bar}$ above
the inlet.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Sudden expansion: the Borda--Carnot loss]\label{prop:b2:flow-balances:borda}
\index{Borda--Carnot loss}\index{head loss (singular)}
When a pipe of section $S_1$ opens suddenly into a pipe of section $S_2
> S_1$, the jet spreads in a turbulent zone and the pressure
\emph{rises} downstream, but less than Bernoulli would say: the
mechanical energy lost per unit volume is
\[
\Delta P_{\text{loss}} = \tfrac12\rho(v_1 - v_2)^2 ,
\]
the kinetic energy of the "lost" relative velocity. A flow leaving a
pipe into a large tank ($v_2 \to 0$) loses all its kinetic energy.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Momentum balance on the fluid between the expansion and a section
downstream where the flow is again uniform; experiment shows the
pressure on the annular wall at the expansion to be $P_1$ (the jet has
not yet spread). Hence $(P_1 - P_2)S_2 = D_m(v_2 - v_1) = \rho S_2v_2(v_2 -
v_1)$, i.e.\ $P_2 - P_1 = \rho v_2(v_1 - v_2)$. Bernoulli would give
$\tfrac12\rho(v_1^2 - v_2^2)$; the difference is $\tfrac12\rho(v_1 - v_2)^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{method}[Setting up a balance]\label{met:b2:flow-balances:method}
(1) Draw the \omterm{def:b2:flow-balances:cv}{control volume}: its boundary should cut the flow only
where the velocity and pressure are known or wanted (uniform sections,
free jets at $P_0$, free surfaces). (2) List the external forces on the
\emph{fluid inside}: weight, wall forces (unknown, the usual target),
pressure on the cut sections. (3) Write the \omterm{thm:b2:flow-balances:momentum}{momentum flux} out minus in;
for moving parts, work in the frame of the part if the flow is steady
there. (4) For torques, the angular-momentum balance; for powers, the
energy balance in heads. (5) The force on the wall is minus the force
of the wall on the fluid; add the atmospheric pressure acting on the
outside of the device if the net force on the device is wanted.
\end{method}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:1}
A fire hose delivers \qty{25}{L/s} through a nozzle of \qty{3.0}{cm}
diameter. Exit speed; reaction force on the nozzle; force of the jet
on a wall it hits perpendicularly; on a wall receding at \qty{10}{m/s}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:2}
A rocket engine ejects \qty{250}{kg/s} at \qty{3.0}{km/s}. Thrust; initial
acceleration of a \qty{60}{t} rocket; acceleration when \qty{40}{t} of
propellant have burned; speed gained after that burn (free space, then
with gravity, duration \qty{160}{s}).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:3}
Water flows at \qty{2.0}{m/s} and \qty{4.0}{bar} in a \qty{15}{cm} pipe that
turns through \qty{90}{\degree}. Force exerted by the water on the elbow
(magnitude and direction); which part comes from the pressure, which
from the momentum change? Same elbow with the pipe open to the air just
after the bend.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:4}
A jet engine on a test stand swallows \qty{80}{kg/s} of still air and
exhausts it at \qty{550}{m/s} (fuel mass negligible). Thrust. In flight
at \qty{240}{m/s}, same exhaust speed relative to the engine: thrust,
propulsive power, and the kinetic power given to the air in the engine
frame; propulsive efficiency.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:5}
\emph{Pelton wheel.} A jet of \qty{0.50}{m^3/s} at \qty{60}{m/s} hits the
buckets of a wheel of radius \qty{0.80}{m}. (a) Force and power on the
buckets at bucket speed $u$ (perfect $180^\circ$ deflection); optimal $u$
and the corresponding rotation speed in rpm. (b) Power and efficiency
at the optimum. (c) Real buckets deflect the jet by \qty{165}{\degree}:
force and power at $u = v/2$; efficiency. (d) Torque at the optimum;
what happens to the torque at standstill and at the runaway speed?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:6}
\emph{Sprinkler.} Two arms of \qty{15}{cm} end in \qty{3.0}{mm} nozzles;
the total flow is \qty{0.20}{L/s}. (a) Relative exit speed. (b) Torque at
rest. (c) Rotation rate with a \omterm{def:b2:rigid-body-mechanics:pivot}{bearing} friction torque of
\qty{5.0e-3}{N.m}; the limiting rate with no friction. (d) Absolute speed
of the water leaving, in each case.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:7}
\emph{Hovering.} A helicopter of \qty{2.0}{t} hovers with a rotor of
\qty{10}{m} diameter. Model the rotor as a disk that takes in still air
and pushes it down through a uniform speed $v_i$ at the disk, $2v_i$ far
below (admit the factor 2). (a) Thrust in terms of $\rho$, $A$, $v_i$;
value of $v_i$. (b) Power given to the air (its kinetic power far
below). (c) Why does a larger rotor need less power? (d) Same
calculation for a \qty{1}{kg} drone with four \qty{25}{cm} propellers;
power-to-weight ratio compared.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:8}
\emph{Sudden expansion.} Water at \qty{4.0}{m/s} in a \qty{5}{cm} pipe enters
a \qty{10}{cm} pipe. (a) Downstream speed; pressure rise according to
Bernoulli and according to Borda--Carnot; loss in pascals and in metres
of head. (b) The same expansion made gradual (a diffuser) recovers $80\%$
of the Bernoulli rise: loss. (c) Loss when the \qty{5}{cm} pipe discharges
into a large tank. (d) Power lost in case (a) and (c) at the given flow.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:9}
A pump draws water from a well \qty{5.0}{m} below it and delivers
\qty{10}{L/s} to a tank \qty{25}{m} above it through \qty{60}{m} of \qty{6}{cm}
pipe ($\lambda = 0.025$, plus a singular loss of $2\,v^2/2g$ for the fittings).
(a) Speed and losses. (b) Pump head and \omterm{thm:b2:flow-balances:energy}{hydraulic power}; shaft power
at $65\%$ efficiency. (c) Pressure at the pump inlet (suction side) and
outlet. (d) The water's vapour pressure is \qty{2.3}{kPa}: maximum height
of the pump above the well.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:10}
\emph{Water rocket.} A \qty{2.0}{L} bottle holds \qty{1.0}{L} of water and
air at \qty{5.0}{bar} (absolute); the nozzle has a diameter of \qty{2.2}{cm};
empty bottle \qty{0.10}{kg}. (a) Initial exit speed (Bernoulli, water
surface at rest) and thrust; initial acceleration. (b) As water leaves
the air expands isothermally: pressure and exit speed when half the
water is gone. (c) Estimate the burn duration and the speed reached
(neglect gravity and drag, treat the thrust as its mean value). (d)
What does the empty bottle do once the water is gone, and why does a
little water (not a lot) give the best height?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:11}
\emph{Francis turbine.} Water enters a runner at $r_1 = \qty{1.0}{m}$ with
$v_{\theta1} = \qty{20}{m/s}$ and leaves at $r_2 = \qty{0.50}{m}$ with no swirl;
flow \qty{30}{m^3/s}; rotation \qty{150}{rpm}. (a) Torque and power. (b)
Head extracted, $H = \mathcal P/\rho gD_V$. (c) If the runner ran at
\qty{200}{rpm} with the same inlet velocity and the exit swirl became
$v_{\theta2} = \qty{3}{m/s}$ in the direction of rotation: power and the
fraction of the head turned into useless exit swirl. (d) Why is the
Pelton wheel used for high heads and small flows, the Francis for the
opposite?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:flow-balances:12}
\emph{Hydraulic jump.} In a horizontal channel of unit width, water
of depth $h_1$ flowing at $v_1$ jumps abruptly to depth $h_2$ and speed
$v_2$ (the foamy step below a weir or in a kitchen sink). (a) Mass
conservation. (b) Momentum balance on the fluid between the two sides
of the jump, the pressure being hydrostatic on each side: show
$\tfrac12gh_1^2 + v_1^2h_1 = \tfrac12gh_2^2 + v_2^2h_2$. (c) Deduce $h_2/h_1 =
\tfrac12\bigl(-1 + \sqrt{1 + 8\,\mathrm{Fr}_1^2}\bigr)$ with $\mathrm{Fr}_1 =
v_1/\sqrt{gh_1}$ (the Froude number); a jump needs $\mathrm{Fr}_1 > 1$. (d)
Head lost in the jump, $\Delta H = (h_2 - h_1)^3/4h_1h_2$; numbers for $h_1 =
\qty{0.20}{m}$, $v_1 = \qty{4.0}{m/s}$, and the power dissipated per metre
of width.
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: Jets, rockets and runners}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- what pushes a jet aircraft, what
lifts a rocket, what turns a turbine, and what it costs to pump water
uphill}]
\label{pb:b2:flow-balances:1}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The turbojet.}
An engine on a test stand takes in $D_a = \qty{50}{kg/s}$ of still air,
burns $D_f = \qty{1.0}{kg/s}$ of fuel, and exhausts the mixture at $v_e =
\qty{600}{m/s}$ at atmospheric pressure.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Thrust on the test stand.
  \item In flight at $v = \qty{250}{m/s}$ (same $v_e$ relative to the
        engine): thrust, and the propulsive power $Fv$.
  \item Kinetic power given to the gas, computed in the engine's frame.
        Propulsive efficiency $\eta_p = Fv/\mathcal P_{\text{kin}}$; show
        that, neglecting the fuel mass, $\eta_p = 2v/(v + v_e)$.
  \item The fuel releases \qty{43}{MJ/kg}: thermal efficiency of the
        engine (kinetic power over heat power) and overall efficiency.
  \item A turbofan uses the same kinetic power to accelerate
        $\qty{500}{kg/s}$ of air: exhaust speed, thrust and $\eta_p$ at
        \qty{250}{m/s}. Why do airliners have huge fans?
  \item Why can a turbojet not work at rest on a rocket's job, above
        the atmosphere?
  \item Reverse thrust on landing deflects the fan flow forward at
        \qty{45}{\degree} from the axis: braking force for the turbofan
        of question 5 at \qty{70}{m/s}, the fan exhaust being then
        \qty{150}{m/s} relative to the engine; compare with the forward
        thrust the same flow would give.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The rocket.}
A single-stage rocket: initial mass $m_0 = \qty{300}{t}$, propellant
\qty{200}{t}, exhaust speed $v_e = \qty{3.0}{km/s}$, burn time $\tau =
\qty{150}{s}$ at constant \omterm{def:b2:fluid-kinematics:flowrate}{mass flow rate}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Apply the momentum balance to rocket plus the gas ejected in
        $\dd t$ to derive $m\,\dd v/\dd t = D_mv_e - mg$ for a vertical
        flight.
  \item \omterm{def:b2:fluid-kinematics:flowrate}{Mass flow rate}, thrust, and the acceleration at lift-off and
        at burn-out.
  \item Integrate to get the burn-out speed; what is the "gravity
        loss"?
  \item Burn-out speed with the same rocket split into two stages of
        \qty{100}{t} of propellant each, the first stage's empty mass
        (\qty{20}{t}) being dropped before the second ignites (neglect
        gravity here): compare with the single stage.
  \item What exhaust speed would a single stage need to reach orbital
        speed (\qty{7.8}{km/s}, neglecting gravity) with the same
        mass ratio? Comment on the choice of propellants.
  \item At lift-off the exhaust jet (\qty{1333}{kg/s} at \qty{3}{km/s}) hits
        the flame deflector of the pad and is turned horizontally:
        force on the deflector.
  \item The rocket's exhaust is also a fluid flow: why does the thrust
        \emph{increase} with altitude for a real nozzle (think of the
        pressure on the nozzle exit section)?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The Pelton wheel.}
The jet of the previous chapter's plant: $D_V = \qty{60}{m^3/s}$ at $v =
\qty{62.6}{m/s}$, split between two wheels; each wheel has a radius $R =
\qty{1.5}{m}$ and buckets deflecting the jet by \qty{165}{\degree}.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Force on the buckets of one wheel at bucket speed $u$, and the
        power; optimal $u$.
  \item Rotation speed at the optimum, in rpm; torque on the shaft.
  \item The generator must turn at a multiple of \qty{50}{Hz} divided by
        its number of pole pairs: choose the number of pole pairs.
  \item Power of one wheel and efficiency with respect to the jet's
        kinetic power; total electrical power at $95\%$ generator
        efficiency.
  \item Using the angular-momentum balance (\omterm{thm:b2:flow-balances:angular}{Euler's turbine equation})
        on a bucket at the optimum: angular momentum of the water
        entering and leaving, per kilogram, and check the torque.
  \item The load drops suddenly and the wheel runs away toward $u =
        v$: what happens to the force and to the torque, and why is a
        deflector that diverts the jet part of every Pelton plant?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Pumping uphill.}
The plant is also used as storage: at night \qty{40}{m^3/s} are pumped
back up through the penstock (\qty{400}{m}, \qty{3}{m}, $\lambda = 0.015$) to the
reservoir \qty{200}{m} above.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Head losses in the penstock at \qty{40}{m^3/s}; pump head needed.
  \item \omterm{thm:b2:flow-balances:energy}{Hydraulic power}, and electrical power drawn at $88\%$ pump
        efficiency.
  \item Pressure at the bottom of the penstock while pumping; compare
        with its value in turbine mode (previous chapter).
  \item Energy stored per night of \qty{8}{h} (potential energy of the
        water raised); round-trip efficiency of the storage if the
        turbine mode recovers $90\%$ of the available head and the
        generator $95\%$.
  \item Sum up the four balances used in this problem (momentum, angular
        momentum, energy, mass) and the device each one sized.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
