\chapter{Electromagnetic Waves in Plasmas, Conductors and Dielectrics}\label{ch:b2:waves-in-media}

A shortwave broadcast crosses an ocean by bouncing off the upper
atmosphere, which reflects it like a mirror --- yet the same layer lets
television and satellite signals through. A metal spoon is shiny, a
metal mesh in the door of a microwave oven holds in the waves while
you watch your food through it, and a sheet of glass is transparent
to light but opaque to ultraviolet. In each case a wave enters a
material, the material's charges respond, and their response changes
the wave: its speed, its \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:complex}{attenuation}, even whether it propagates at
all. This chapter treats the three standard models of a medium --- a
gas of free electrons, an ohmic conductor, and bound electrons --- with
the one method that handles them all: add the induced current to
\omterm{thm:b2:maxwell-equations:maxwell}{Maxwell's equations} and read off the \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:relation}{dispersion relation}.

\section{Waves in a plasma}

\begin{definition}[Plasma; the free-electron model]\label{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}
\index{plasma}\index{plasma frequency}
A \emph{plasma} is a globally neutral gas of free electrons (density
$n$, mass $m$, charge $-e$) and positive ions, which, being thousands of
times heavier, are taken as fixed. The upper atmosphere (ionosphere,
$n \sim 10^{11}$--$10^{12}$ m$^{-3}$), a discharge tube, the solar corona,
and --- at optical frequencies --- the conduction electrons of a metal
are plasmas. Collisions are neglected (the wave's period is short
compared with the time between collisions). Its characteristic
frequency is the \emph{plasma frequency}
\[
\omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{ne^2}{\varepsilon_0m}} , \qquad
f_p = \frac{\omega_p}{2\pi} \approx 9\,\text{Hz}\times\sqrt{n\,[\text{m}^{-3}]} :
\]
\qty{3}{MHz} to \qty{9}{MHz} for the ionosphere, \qty{2e15}{Hz} (ultraviolet) for
a metal.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Dispersion relation of a plasma]\label{thm:b2:waves-in-media:plasmadispersion}
\index{cut-off frequency (plasma)}\index{evanescent wave (plasma)}
For a transverse PPH wave $\underline{\vect E} = \underline{\vect E}_0\eu^{\iu(\omega t - kx)}$ in
a \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma}, the electrons oscillate with $\underline{\vect v} = -e\underline{\vect E}/\iu m\omega$
and carry the \omterm{def:b2:charges-currents-conduction:densities}{current density} $\underline{\vect j} = \underline\gamma\underline{\vect E}$ with
$\underline\gamma = ne^2/\iu m\omega$; \omterm{thm:b2:maxwell-equations:maxwell}{Maxwell's equations} then give
\[
k^2 = \frac{\omega^2 - \omega_p^2}{c^2} .
\]
Above $\omega_p$ the wave propagates, with $v_\varphi = c/\sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2}
> c$ and $v_g = c\sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2} < c$, $v_\varphi v_g = c^2$; below
$\omega_p$, $k = -\iu\kappa$ with $\kappa = \sqrt{\omega_p^2 - \omega^2}/c$: the wave is
\emph{evanescent}, penetrates over $1/\kappa$ and is totally reflected
(\cref{ch:b2:wave-interfaces}); the \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma} is then a mirror. At $\omega
\gg \omega_p$ the electrons cannot follow and the \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma} is transparent.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Equation of motion $m\,\dd\vect v/\dd t = -e\vect E$ (the magnetic force
$ev B \sim evE/c$ is negligible for $v \ll c$, and the ions' motion is
$m/M$ times smaller): $\iu m\omega\underline{\vect v} = -e\underline{\vect E}$. Then
$\underline{\vect j} = -ne\underline{\vect v}$. The \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma} stays neutral for a
transverse wave ($\vect k\cdot\vect E = 0$ gives $\rho = 0$ by Gauss), so
Maxwell--Ampère reads $-\iu\vect k\wedge\underline{\vect B} = \mu_0\underline\gamma\underline{\vect E}
+ \iu\omega\mu_0\varepsilon_0\underline{\vect E}$ and Maxwell--Faraday $\underline{\vect B} = \vect k
\wedge\underline{\vect E}/\omega$; combining, $k^2\underline{\vect E} = \mu_0(\iu\omega\underline\gamma -
\omega^2\varepsilon_0)(-1)\underline{\vect E}$, i.e.\ $k^2 = \omega^2\mu_0\varepsilon_0 - \iu\omega\mu_0
\underline\gamma = (\omega^2 - ne^2/\varepsilon_0m)/c^2$. The velocities follow as in
\cref{ch:b2:dispersion-wave-packets}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Where the energy goes]\label{rem:b2:waves-in-media:plasmaenergy}
The conductivity is imaginary: $\langle\vect j\cdot\vect E\rangle = 0$, the
electrons take energy from the wave during half a cycle and give it
back during the next --- a lossless medium, in which the wave's energy
is shared between the fields and the electrons' kinetic energy, and
travels at $v_g$. Collisions add a small real part to $\underline\gamma$ and a
small absorption: the ionosphere's D layer absorbs medium waves by
day, which is why distant AM stations come in at night.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[The ionosphere and radio]\label{ex:b2:waves-in-media:ionosphere}
With $n = \qty{1e12}{m^{-3}}$ in the F layer, $f_p = \qty{9}{MHz}$: shortwave
broadcasts below that are reflected (and can hop around the Earth
between the ionosphere and the ground), FM (\qty{100}{MHz}), television
and satellite links pass through. A wave at \qty{3}{MHz} penetrates only
$1/\kappa = c/\sqrt{\omega_p^2 - \omega^2} = \qty{6}{m}$ into the layer before
turning back. A spacecraft re-entering the atmosphere is sheathed in
a \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma} of $n \sim 10^{18}$ m$^{-3}$, $f_p \sim \qty{10}{GHz}$: radio contact is
lost for minutes.
\end{example}

\section{Waves in an ohmic conductor: the skin effect}

\begin{theorem}[Skin effect]\label{thm:b2:waves-in-media:skin}
\index{skin effect}\index{skin depth}
In an ohmic conductor ($\vect j = \gamma\vect E$, $\gamma$ real, at frequencies
with $\omega\tau \ll 1$ and $\varepsilon_0\omega \ll \gamma$, i.e.\ below $\sim\qty{1e16}{Hz}$ for
copper) the \omterm{thm:b2:maxwell-equations:maxwell}{displacement current} is negligible and the fields obey the
diffusion equation $\Delta\vect E = \mu_0\gamma\,\partial_t\vect E$. A PPH wave entering
the conductor at $x = 0$ has
\[
\underline k^2 = -\iu\mu_0\gamma\omega , \qquad \underline k = \frac{1 - \iu}\delta , \qquad
\delta = \sqrt{\frac2{\mu_0\gamma\omega}} ,
\]
so that $\underline E = E_0\,\eu^{-x/\delta}\eu^{\iu(\omega t - x/\delta)}$: it is damped
over the \emph{\omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:complex}{skin depth}} $\delta$, with a phase that also turns by one
radian per $\delta$ --- a wavelength $2\pi\delta$, a \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:relation}{phase velocity} $\omega\delta \ll
c$. The current $\vect j = \gamma\vect E$ is confined to a layer of thickness
$\delta$ under the surface, and $\vect B$ lags $\vect E$ by \qty{45}{\degree} with
$|B| = |E|\sqrt2/\omega\delta$. For copper, $\delta = \qty{9.2}{mm}$ at \qty{50}{Hz},
\qty{0.21}{mm} at \qty{100}{kHz}, \qty{2.1}{\micro m} at \qty{1}{GHz}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\operatorname{\vect{curl}}\vect B = \mu_0\gamma\vect E$ (displacement dropped) and
$\operatorname{\vect{curl}}\vect E = -\partial_t\vect B$: taking the curl of the second,
$-\Delta\vect E = -\mu_0\gamma\partial_t\vect E$ (the conductor is neutral, $\operatorname{div}
\vect E = 0$). For $\eu^{\iu(\omega t - kx)}$: $k^2 = -\iu\mu_0\gamma\omega$, and
$\sqrt{-\iu} = (1 - \iu)/\sqrt2$ gives $\underline k = (1 - \iu)/\delta$ (the root that
decays into the conductor). Faraday: $\underline B = \underline k\underline E/\omega = (1 - \iu)
\underline E/\omega\delta$, of modulus $\sqrt2E/\omega\delta$ and phase $-\pi/4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Consequences of the skin effect]\label{ex:b2:waves-in-media:skinuses}
\index{AC resistance}\index{Faraday cage}
(i) A wire of radius $a \gg \delta$ carries its current in a ring of
thickness $\delta$: its resistance rises from $1/\gamma\pi a^2$ to about $1/\gamma
2\pi a\delta$ per metre --- for a \qty{1}{mm} copper wire, four times the DC
value at \qty{1}{MHz}, a hundred times at \qty{1}{GHz}. High-frequency coils
use "Litz" wire (many insulated strands) or silver-plated tubes; the
power grid's conductors are stranded with an aluminium sheath. (ii) A
metal box attenuates a wave by $\eu^{-d/\delta}$ per thickness $d$: a
\qty{1}{mm} aluminium enclosure blocks \qty{1}{MHz} by \qty{100}{dB} but \qty{50}{Hz}
hardly at all --- low-frequency magnetic fields need iron. (iii)
Submarines receive only very-long-wave radio ($\delta = \qty{2.3}{m}$ at
\qty{10}{kHz} in sea water); mines are detected by the eddy currents a
coil induces in them (\cref{ch:b2:maxwell-equations}, the diffusion
time $\mu_0\gamma d^2$).
\end{example}

\section{Waves in a dielectric: the bound electron}

\begin{proposition}[Elastically bound electron; complex index]\label{prop:b2:waves-in-media:lorentz}
\index{dielectric}\index{polarization (of a medium)}\index{susceptibility}\index{complex index}\index{Lorentz model}
In an insulator each electron is bound to its atom: a displacement
$\vect r$ from equilibrium calls the restoring force $-m\omega_0^2\vect r$ and
a damping $-m\Gamma\,\dd\vect r/\dd t$ (the energy radiated or handed to the
lattice). Driven by the wave, $m\ddot{\vect r} = -m\omega_0^2\vect r - m\Gamma\dot{\vect r}
- e\vect E$; the displaced electrons give the medium the \emph{polarization}
(dipole moment per unit volume) $\vect P = -ne\vect r = \varepsilon_0\underline\chi(\omega)
\underline{\vect E}$ with the \emph{susceptibility}
\[
\underline\chi(\omega) = \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + \iu\Gamma\omega} ,
\qquad \omega_p^2 = \frac{ne^2}{\varepsilon_0m} ,
\]
and the bound current $\vect j = \partial_t\vect P$. \omterm{thm:b2:maxwell-equations:maxwell}{Maxwell's equations} then give
\[
\underline k^2 = \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\,(1 + \underline\chi) = \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\,\underline n^2 ,
\qquad \underline n = n' - \iu n'' ,
\]
with $n'$ the refractive index (\omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:relation}{phase velocity} $c/n'$) and $n''$ the
extinction (the intensity decays as $\eu^{-2n''\omega x/c}$). Far below a
resonance ($\omega \ll \omega_0$, $\Gamma$ negligible), $n'^2 \approx 1 + \omega_p^2/\omega_0^2 +
\omega_p^2\omega^2/\omega_0^4$: the index \emph{rises} with frequency --- normal
dispersion, Cauchy's law $n \approx A + B/\lambda^2$ --- and absorption is
negligible; near $\omega_0$ the medium absorbs, and just above it $n'$
falls with $\omega$ (anomalous dispersion).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Complex amplitudes: $(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + \iu\Gamma\omega)\underline{\vect r} = -e\underline{\vect E}
/m$; $\underline{\vect P} = -ne\underline{\vect r}$. Then, as for the \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma}, $k^2 = \omega^2\mu_0
\varepsilon_0 - \iu\omega\mu_0\underline\gamma$ with $\underline{\vect j} = \iu\omega\underline{\vect P} = \iu\omega\varepsilon_0
\underline\chi\underline{\vect E}$, i.e.\ $\underline\gamma = \iu\omega\varepsilon_0\underline\chi$: $k^2 = (\omega^2/c^2)
(1 + \underline\chi)$. (The \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma} is the case $\omega_0 = 0$, $\Gamma = 0$.)
Expansion: $1/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) \approx (1 + \omega^2/\omega_0^2)/\omega_0^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Glass, water, air]\label{ex:b2:waves-in-media:dielectrics}
Glass has its electronic resonances in the ultraviolet ($\omega_0 \sim 10^{16}$
rad/s): in the visible it is transparent and dispersive, $n = 1.51$ at
\qty{700}{nm}, $1.53$ at \qty{400}{nm}, enough for a prism to spread a
rainbow and for a fibre's pulses to spread (\cref{ch:b2:dispersion-wave-packets});
in the ultraviolet it absorbs --- you do not tan behind a window.
Water has, besides, a rotational resonance of its polar molecules near
\qty{20}{GHz} (a Debye relaxation rather than a sharp line): it absorbs
microwaves strongly, which heats the food and blinds radar in rain,
and has $n'' \approx 0$ in a narrow visible window --- the window through
which eyes evolved. Air, with $n - 1 = 2.9 \times 10^{-4}$, still bends the
setting Sun by half a degree.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[One equation, three media]\label{rem:b2:waves-in-media:summary}
Every linear medium enters \omterm{thm:b2:maxwell-equations:maxwell}{Maxwell's equations} through its induced
current $\underline{\vect j} = \underline\gamma(\omega)\underline{\vect E}$, and the \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:relation}{dispersion
relation} is always $\underline k^2 = \omega^2/c^2 - \iu\omega\mu_0\underline\gamma(\omega)$:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
medium & $\underline\gamma(\omega)$ & $\underline k^2$\\ \hline
\omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma} & $ne^2/\iu m\omega$ (imaginary) & $(\omega^2 - \omega_p^2)/c^2$: cut-off\\
ohmic conductor & $\gamma$ (real) & $-\iu\mu_0\gamma\omega$: \omterm{thm:b2:waves-in-media:skin}{skin effect}\\
dielectric & $\iu\omega\varepsilon_0\underline\chi(\omega)$ & $(\omega^2/c^2)(1 + \underline\chi)$: index
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
A metal is all three in turn: ohmic below \qty{1e13}{Hz}, a \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma}
(reflecting) in the visible, transparent in the far ultraviolet.
\end{remark}

\begin{method}[Waves in a medium]\label{met:b2:waves-in-media:method}
(1) Write the equation of motion of the charges and get $\underline{\vect j} =
\underline\gamma\underline{\vect E}$. (2) Insert into Maxwell--Ampère with the PPH
ansatz: $\underline k^2 = \omega^2/c^2 - \iu\omega\mu_0\underline\gamma$. (3) Take the root
with $\operatorname{Im}k \le 0$ (decay in the direction of travel), split
into real (propagation, $v_\varphi = \omega/k'$) and imaginary (\omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:complex}{attenuation})
parts. (4) Identify the regimes in frequency --- propagating,
evanescent, absorbing --- and the characteristic frequency ($\omega_p$,
$1/\mu_0\gamma\delta^2$, $\omega_0$). (5) Find $\vect B$ from Faraday and, if needed,
the Poynting flux and the dissipated power.
\end{method}

\section{Exercises}

(copper), \qty{1e5}{m^{-3}} (interstellar space). For each, which of the
following passes: a \qty{1}{MHz} AM station, a \qty{100}{MHz} FM station, a
\qty{1.5}{GHz} GPS signal, visible light?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:waves-in-media:2}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:waves-in-media:3}

\lambda\,\dd n/\dd\lambda$ at \qty{550}{nm}; angular spread of a white beam

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:b2:waves-in-media:4}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:waves-in-media:5}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:waves-in-media:6}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:waves-in-media:7}
\emph{Energy in a \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma}.} For the transverse wave of
\cref{thm:b2:waves-in-media:plasmadispersion}: (a) electron velocity
amplitude and the kinetic energy density $\tfrac12nm\langle v^2\rangle$. (b)
Mean electric and magnetic energy densities. (c) Show that the total
energy density is $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E_0^2(1 + \dots)$ --- compute the bracket ---
and that $\langle\Pi\rangle$ divided by it equals $v_g$. (d) What fraction of
the energy is in the electrons at $\omega = 2\omega_p$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:waves-in-media:8}
\emph{Pulsar dispersion.} Radio pulses from a pulsar cross a distance
$L$ of interstellar \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma} ($n \ll$ anything, $\omega \gg \omega_p$). (a) Show
that the group delay is $t \approx (L/c)(1 + \omega_p^2/2\omega^2)$. (b) The delay
between the arrivals at $f_1$ and $f_2$ is $\Delta t = \dfrac{e^2}{8\pi^2
\varepsilon_0mc}\,nL\Bigl(\dfrac1{f_1^2} - \dfrac1{f_2^2}\Bigr)$: compute the constant.
(c) A pulsar at $L = \qty{1}{kpc} = \qty{3.1e19}{m}$ through $n = \qty{3e4}{m^{-3}}$:
delay between \qty{400}{MHz} and \qty{1.4}{GHz}. (d) Astronomers measure
$\Delta t$ and know $n$: what do they get? (The dispersion measure, the
standard distance gauge for pulsars.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:waves-in-media:9}
\emph{A spectral line.} In a dilute gas ($\underline n \approx 1 + \underline\chi/2$) the
\omterm{prop:b2:waves-in-media:lorentz}{Lorentz model} gives $n'' = \tfrac12\operatorname{Im}\underline\chi$. (a) Show that near
$\omega_0$, $n'' \approx \dfrac{\omega_p^2}{4\omega_0}\,\dfrac{\Gamma/2}{(\omega_0 - \omega)^2 + \Gamma^2/4}$, a
Lorentzian of full width $\Gamma$. (b) Absorption coefficient $\alpha = 2n''\omega/c$
at the centre. (c) Sodium vapour, $n = \qty{1e17}{m^{-3}}$, $\lambda_0 = \qty{589}{nm}$,
$\Gamma = \qty{6e7}{s^{-1}}$: $\alpha$ at the centre and the thickness that absorbs
$90\%$ (ignore the Doppler broadening, which in fact widens the line a
hundredfold). (d) Sketch $n' - 1$ across the line; where is the
dispersion anomalous?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:waves-in-media:10}
\emph{Joule heating in the skin.} A wave $E_0\eu^{\iu\omega t}$ (along $y$)
enters a conductor filling $x > 0$. (a) Write $\underline E(x, t)$ and deduce
$\underline B(x, t)$ (along $z$) from Faraday. (b) Mean \omterm{thm:b2:poynting-vector:poynting}{Poynting vector} at the
surface: show $\langle\Pi_x(0)\rangle = E_0^2/2\mu_0\omega\delta$. (c) Mean Joule
power per unit area, $\int_0^\infty\tfrac12\gamma|\underline E|^2\dd x$: show it equals
(b). (d) Express the result as $\tfrac12R_sH_0^2$ with $H_0 = B_0/\mu_0$ the
surface magnetic field and $R_s = 1/\gamma\delta$ the \emph{surface
resistance}; value for copper at \qty{1}{GHz}, and the loss of a
microwave cavity whose walls carry $H_0 = \qty{100}{A/m}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:waves-in-media:11}
\emph{Why metals shine.} Treat silver's conduction electrons ($n =
\qty{5.9e28}{m^{-3}}$) as a collisionless \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{plasma} at optical frequencies.
(a) $\omega_p$ and the corresponding wavelength. (b) At \qty{500}{nm}: $k$ is
imaginary --- penetration depth; the wave is totally reflected (no
absorption in this model): silver is a mirror. (c) At \qty{200}{nm}? Hence
the "ultraviolet transparency" of alkali metals. (d) Real silver
reflects $95\%$, gold looks yellow: what does the model miss (think of
collisions, and of bound electrons whose resonances lie in the
visible for gold)?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{exo:b2:waves-in-media:12}
\emph{Water in the microwave.} The orientation of water's polar
molecules gives a susceptibility $\underline\chi = \chi_s/(1 + \iu\omega\tau)$ (Debye
relaxation), $\chi_s \approx 80$, $\tau = \qty{8}{ps}$, on top of a constant
$\chi_\infty \approx 4$. (a) Real and imaginary parts of $\underline\varepsilon_r = 1 + \chi_\infty
+ \underline\chi$ at \qty{2.45}{GHz} and at \qty{20}{GHz}. (b) \omterm{prop:b2:waves-in-media:lorentz}{Complex index} and the
absorption length $1/(2n''\omega/c)$ of the intensity at \qty{2.45}{GHz}: how
deep does an oven cook? (c) Power absorbed per unit volume for a
field amplitude $E_0$ in the water: $\tfrac12\omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon''E_0^2$; value for
$E_0 = \qty{2}{kV/m}$. (d) Why is \qty{2.45}{GHz} used rather than the
\qty{20}{GHz} of maximum absorption?
\end{exercise}

\section{Problem: The ionosphere, the oven door and the prism}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- three materials seen by a wave: the
plasma that bends GPS signals, the metal that keeps microwaves in, and
the glass that spreads white light}]
\label{pb:b2:waves-in-media:1}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The ionosphere and GPS.}
Electron density in the F layer $n = \qty{1e12}{m^{-3}}$; the total column of
electrons along a vertical path is $N_T = \int n\,\dd z = \qty{2e17}{m^{-2}}$ by
day. GPS: $f_1 = \qty{1575}{MHz}$, $f_2 = \qty{1228}{MHz}$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \omterm{def:b2:waves-in-media:plasma}{Plasma frequency} of the F layer; does GPS pass? Does a
        \qty{5}{MHz} shortwave?
  \item Show that for $\omega \gg \omega_p$ the \omterm{prop:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:group}{group velocity} is $v_g \approx c(1 -
        \omega_p^2/2\omega^2)$ and the \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:relation}{phase velocity} $c(1 + \omega_p^2/2\omega^2)$.
  \item Extra group delay of the GPS signal crossing the layer: show
        $\Delta t = \dfrac{e^2}{8\pi^2\varepsilon_0mc}\,\dfrac{N_T}{f^2}$ and compute it for
        $f_1$; the range error $c\,\Delta t$.
  \item The receiver uses both frequencies: from the two delays it
        removes the error. Express $N_T$ in terms of $\Delta t_1 - \Delta t_2$;
        what precision on the delay difference gives $N_T$ to $1\%$?
  \item How many extra carrier cycles does the delay of question 3
        represent at $f_1$?
  \item The phase of the carrier is \emph{advanced} by the same
        amount as the group is delayed: why (signs of the two
        corrections)? Which one matters for a receiver that counts
        carrier cycles?
  \item At night $N_T$ falls tenfold: error for a single-frequency
        receiver by day and by night.
  \item A solar flare raises $n$ in the D layer (\qty{80}{km}) to
        \qty{1e10}{m^{-3}} with many collisions: which radio services are
        hit, and why does GPS only slightly degrade?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The oven door.}
The cavity walls and the door mesh are steel ($\gamma = \qty{1.0e6}{S/m}$);
the mesh holes are \qty{1.0}{mm} wide in a sheet \qty{0.5}{mm} thick;
$f = \qty{2.45}{GHz}$, $P = \qty{800}{W}$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:complex}{Skin depth} in the steel at \qty{2.45}{GHz}; is the wall "thick"?
  \item Surface resistance $R_s = 1/\gamma\delta$; with a surface magnetic
        field of amplitude $H_0 = \qty{30}{A/m}$ on the walls (\qty{1}{m^2}),
        power lost in the walls, $\tfrac12R_sH_0^2$ per unit area; fraction
        of $P$.
  \item In a hole of width $a \ll \lambda$ the field cannot propagate (the
        hole is a waveguide below cut-off, \cref{ch:b2:guided-waves}):
        it decays as $\eu^{-\pi z/a}$ across the thickness $z$. \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:complex}{Attenuation}
        of the amplitude across the \qty{0.5}{mm} sheet, in dB.
  \item Why can you see through the mesh (wavelength of light
        against the hole size) while the microwaves cannot get out?
  \item The glass window is a dielectric with $\underline\varepsilon_r = 4 - 0.02\iu$:
        index, and the intensity \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:complex}{attenuation} length in it; is the
        glass heated?
  \item A metal fork in the oven: the field at its tips is enhanced;
        with the \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:complex}{skin depth} of question 8, estimate the \omterm{def:b2:charges-currents-conduction:densities}{current
        density} at the surface for $H_0 = \qty{300}{A/m}$ (use $j \approx H_0/\delta$
        at the surface) and the local heating $j^2/\gamma$ --- why sparks?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The wire at radio frequency.}
A copper wire of radius $a = \qty{0.5}{mm}$ ($\gamma = \qty{6e7}{S/m}$) in a
\qty{13.56}{MHz} induction heater coil carries \qty{20}{A} rms.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:complex}{Skin depth}; effective conducting section; \omterm{ex:b2:waves-in-media:skinuses}{AC resistance} per
        metre against DC.
  \item Power dissipated per metre of coil wire; temperature the
        wire would reach with convective cooling $h = \qty{20}{W/(m^2.K)}$.
  \item The coil induces currents in a steel workpiece ($\gamma =
        \qty{1e6}{S/m}$, $\mu_r \approx 1$ when hot): \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:complex}{skin depth} there; why does
        induction heating heat only a surface layer, and how is that
        used for hardening gears?
  \item To reduce the coil's loss the wire is replaced by a \qty{3}{mm}
        copper tube with water inside: new resistance per metre; does
        the inside of the tube carry current?
  \item At what frequency would the \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:complex}{skin depth} in copper equal the
        wire radius? Below it, what approximation replaces the
        "thick-wire" formula?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The prism.}
A glass has $n(\lambda) = 1.500 + \qty{5.0e3}{nm^2}/\lambda^2$, with its resonance
at $\lambda_0 = \qty{100}{nm}$.
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item Check that the Cauchy form follows from the \omterm{prop:b2:waves-in-media:lorentz}{Lorentz model} far
        below resonance, and that $\omega_p^2/\omega_0^2 = n^2(\infty) - 1$: value
        of $\omega_p^2/\omega_0^2$ and of the effective $n$ for this glass.
  \item Index and \omterm{def:b2:dispersion-wave-packets:relation}{phase velocity} at \qty{400}{nm} and \qty{700}{nm}; group
        index at \qty{550}{nm}; delay between the two colours over
        \qty{1}{km} of such glass in a fibre.
  \item A \qty{60}{\degree} prism at minimum deviation for \qty{550}{nm}:
        deviation (use $n\sin(A/2) = \sin((A + D)/2)$); angular distance
        between \qty{400}{nm} and \qty{700}{nm} (differentiate); on a screen
        \qty{2}{m} away, the width of the spectrum.
  \item Why is the glass opaque at \qty{200}{nm}, and what is its
        colour if a trace of iron adds a weak resonance at \qty{700}{nm}?
  \item In the Lorentz picture, why is blue deviated more than red by
        the prism?
  \item In one table give, for the F layer, the steel, the copper and
        the glass at their working frequencies, the nature of
        $\underline\gamma$ and the fate of the wave.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
