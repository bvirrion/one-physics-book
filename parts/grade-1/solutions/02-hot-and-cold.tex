\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:g1:hot-and-cold} --- Hot and Cold}

\begin{solution}{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:1}
The ice cube, then the air of the bedroom, then the bath water, then
the soup fresh from the pot.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:2}
The tap water is \emph{cold} (or cooler) compared to the hot
chocolate, but \emph{warm} (or warmer) compared to the juice.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:3}
For example: the Sun, a flame, a radiator, your own body (or rubbing
hands). The Sun warms the whole park.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:4}
The palms feel warm against the cheek: rubbing warmed them up.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:5}
The cube in the sun melts first: the Sun warms it, and warmth is what
melts ice. The one in the shade gets less warmth and lasts longer.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:6}
The puddle is water --- the same water that was the ice cube. The
hard ice did not disappear; the warmth of the room melted it into
liquid water.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:7}
After a whole morning in the same shade, the slide and the bench are
equally cold. The metal only \emph{feels} colder to the hand ---
touch is not a fair judge, as the metal and wooden spoons showed.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:8}
Touch one bowl, then the other, right away, with the \emph{same}
hand, and ask a friend to check without \omterm{def:g1:five-senses:senses}{hearing} your answer. The same
hand is needed because the three-bowls trick showed that two hands
can disagree about the very same water.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:9}
Lea is right. A coat makes no heat: it only stops warmth from passing
through. The snowman has no warmth to keep in --- instead the coat
keeps the \emph{warm air of the day out}, so the wrapped snowman
melts more slowly and lasts longer.
\end{solution}
