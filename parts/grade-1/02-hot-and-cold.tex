\chapter{Hot and Cold}\label{ch:g1:hot-and-cold}

Soup steams, ice cream makes your teeth ache, and the car seat in
summer stings your legs. Hot and cold are everywhere. In this chapter
we learn to compare them, to see what warms things up --- and to
watch what happens to an ice cube when the warmth wins.

\section{Warmer and colder}

\begin{definition}[Hot and cold]\label{def:g1:hot-and-cold:words}
\emph{Hot}\index{hot} and \emph{cold}\index{cold} are comparing
words, like big and small. Soup is hot \emph{compared to} your hand;
ice is cold \emph{compared to} your hand. Between the two there is a
whole ladder of words: freezing, cold, cool, lukewarm, warm, hot,
burning.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Ordering three drinks]\label{ex:g1:hot-and-cold:drinks}
On the table: a hot chocolate, a glass of tap water, a glass of
orange juice straight from the refrigerator. From coldest to hottest:
the juice, then the tap water, then the hot chocolate. The tap water
is cold next to the chocolate but warm next to the juice ---
comparing words always work in pairs.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Touch can be fooled]\label{rem:g1:hot-and-cold:fooled}
Remember the three bowls of the last chapter: the same lukewarm water
felt warm to one hand and cool to the other. And touch a metal spoon
and a wooden spoon that both spent the night in the same kitchen: the
metal one \emph{feels} colder, though nothing in the kitchen is
colder than anything else. Touch is a helpful guard, but it is not a
fair judge --- one day soon we will need an instrument that cannot be
fooled: the thermometer, which arrives in a later chapter.
\end{remark}

\begin{method}[Comparing hot and cold fairly]\label{met:g1:hot-and-cold:compare}
To decide which of two bowls of water is warmer:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item use the \emph{same} hand for both --- two hands can disagree;
  \item touch one, then the other, right away --- waiting lets things
        change;
  \item to be extra sure, ask a friend to try too, without telling
        them your answer first.
\end{enumerate}
If you and your friend agree, the observation is solid.
\end{method}

\section{What warms things up}

\begin{example}[Warmers]\label{ex:g1:hot-and-cold:warmers}
Some things warm up whatever is near them: the Sun warms the bench in
the park, the flame warms the pan, the radiator warms the room, and
your own body warms the blanket. Even rubbing warms: press your palms
together and rub fast while you count to $10$ --- feel them heat up.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Try it: sun and shade]\label{ex:g1:hot-and-cold:sunshade}
On a sunny day, put one hand on a wall in the sunshine and the other
on the same wall in the shade. Same wall, same day --- but the sunny
side is clearly warmer. The Sun is the great warmer of everything
outdoors; it will get a whole chapter of its own soon.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Coats do not make heat]\label{rem:g1:hot-and-cold:coat}
A coat does not warm you the way a radiator does. Your \emph{body}
makes the warmth; the coat only stops that warmth from escaping,
like a lid on the soup. That is why a coat lying alone on a chair
stays cold inside --- there is nothing in it making warmth to keep.
\end{remark}

\section{When warm wins: melting ice}

\begin{example}[The life of an ice cube]\label{ex:g1:hot-and-cold:icecube}
Put an ice cube on a plate in the kitchen. It is hard and cold. The
warm air of the room warms it, little by little: the cube gets
smaller and smaller, a puddle grows around it, and after a while only
water is left. The warmth of the room turned hard ice into liquid
water --- we say the ice \emph{melted}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Cold can win too]\label{ex:g1:hot-and-cold:freezer}
The freezer does the opposite job: put a small cup of water inside in
the evening, and in the morning you will find it hard as stone ---
ice. Warmth melts ice into water; strong cold turns water back into
ice. Water will keep changing back and forth like this all
through the book.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:1}
Order from coldest to hottest: bath water; \ an ice cube; \ soup
fresh from the pot; \ the air of your bedroom.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:2}
Find the comparing word: the tap water is \dots\ compared to the hot
chocolate, but \dots\ compared to the orange juice from the
refrigerator.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:3}
Name four things that warm what is near them. Which one warms the
whole park?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:4}
Rub your palms together fast while counting to $10$, then touch your
cheek. What do you feel? What did the rubbing do?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:5}
Two ice cubes come out of the freezer together. One goes on a plate
in the sun, the other on a plate in the shade. Which one melts first,
and why?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:6}
What is in the puddle left on the plate after an ice cube melts?
Where did the hard ice go?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:7}
A metal slide and a wooden bench stood in the shade all morning. Tom
touches both and says the slide is colder. Use
\cref{rem:g1:hot-and-cold:fooled} to explain what is really going on.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:8}
Using \cref{met:g1:hot-and-cold:compare}, explain how to find out
which of two bowls of water is warmer. Why should you use the same
hand for both?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:g1:hot-and-cold:9}
Lea builds a snowman and, feeling sorry for him, wraps him in her
warm coat. Her brother says the coat will melt him faster. Lea says
the coat will keep him cold longer. Who is right, and why? (Think of
\cref{rem:g1:hot-and-cold:coat}: what does a coat really do?)
\end{exercise}
